Adding an Equality Constraint

Problem

Minimize $f(q)$ subject to $g(q)=0$.

Feasible motion

Steps must stay tangent to the constraint surface.

Tangent space

$\nabla g(q^\*) \cdot \delta q = 0$

Printed pp. 597-598Adding an Equality Constraint
Now we restrict the design variable to a constraint surface defined by g equals zero. Feasible first-order motion must stay tangent to that surface, meaning the constraint gradient is orthogonal to any allowed step. This tangent-space restriction is the geometric heart of everything that follows in Appendix D.

The Lagrange Condition

The argument

No feasible tangent lowers the cost, so $\nabla f$ has no tangent component left.

The conclusion

It can only point along the constraint normal $\nabla g$.

Stationarity

$\nabla f(q^\*) + \lambda\, \nabla g(q^\*) = 0$

Reading $\lambda$

The multiplier is the signed scale converting one gradient into the other.

Printed pp. 597-598The Lagrange Condition
At a constrained optimum no feasible tangent direction lowers the cost, so the cost gradient has no tangent component left. The only place it can point is along the constraint normal. That forces grad f plus lambda grad g to vanish, the stationarity equation that names the multiplier lambda.

The Lagrangian

Bookkeeping

Fold cost and constraint into one scalar function.

Two derivatives

Zeroing $\partial_q L$ gives stationarity; $\partial_\lambda L$ gives feasibility.

Lagrangian

$L(q,\lambda) = f(q) + \lambda^{\top} g(q)$

Vector form

Stack $m$ constraints in $g$ with a multiplier vector $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

Printed pp. 597-598The Lagrangian
We package the condition by forming the Lagrangian, cost plus lambda times constraint. Setting its gradient with respect to the design variable and the multiplier to zero recovers both stationarity and feasibility at once. This bookkeeping device scales cleanly to several constraints stacked as a vector g with a vector lambda.

Geometric Picture

Parallel gradients

$\nabla f(q^\*) = -\lambda\, \nabla g(q^\*)$

Sign of $\lambda$

The multiplier rescales the constraint normal onto the cost gradient.

Printed pp. 597-598Geometric Picture
The picture to memorize: at the optimum a level curve of the cost is tangent to the constraint curve, and the two gradients are parallel. The multiplier is exactly the signed ratio that rescales the constraint normal onto the cost gradient. We draw the opposing arrows so the sign of lambda becomes visible.

Inequality Constraints and KKT

Real limits

Joint stops, torque bounds, and friction cones are inequalities $h(q)\le 0$.

Active set

Decide which constraints touch the solution before checking signs.

KKT conditions

$\nabla f + \lambda^{\top}\nabla g + \mu^{\top}\nabla h = 0,\;\; \mu \ge 0,\;\; \mu_i h_i = 0$

Slackness

An inactive inequality carries $\mu_i=0$ and contributes no normal.

Printed pp. 597-598Inequality Constraints and KKT
Real robots face inequalities like joint limits and friction cones. The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions extend Lagrange with multiplier sign restrictions and complementary slackness, so an inactive constraint carries a zero multiplier and contributes no normal. Active-set reasoning decides which constraints actually touch the solution before any signs are checked.

One geometric idea quietly organizes planning, control allocation, and force distribution alike.
Modern RoboticsAppendix D

Debugging With Residuals

Trust a candidate when

  • It satisfies the constraint.
  • Stationarity vector is small.
  • Feasible directional derivative $\approx 0$.
  • Curvature margin stays positive.

Pitfall

A stationary point may be a maximum or saddle — check curvature.

Printed pp. 597-598Debugging With Residuals
Because the theory is geometric, it is also testable. A trustworthy candidate satisfies the constraint, drives the stationarity vector small, shows a near-zero directional derivative along the feasible tangent, and keeps a positive curvature margin. These four residuals turn Appendix D into a practical instrument for catching implementation mistakes.
