Motion Planning

Finding a collision-free path for a point in configuration space

The question

How does a robot find a feasible path through a high-dimensional space full of obstacles?

The move

Plan a point through C-space, not a body through the workspace.

Printed pp. 353-402Motion Planning
This chapter, printed pages 353 to 402, reframes robot motion as a search for a point through configuration space rather than a body through workspace. We preview the planning question, the free-versus-forbidden split, and the family of complete, grid, sampling, and optimization methods that answer it.

The Path-Planning Problem

Given

A start $q_{\text{start}}$, a goal $q_{\text{goal}}$, and a collision model on configuration space.

Return

A continuous path in free space, or a report that none exists.

Feasible path

$\tau:[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}},\;\; \tau(0)=q_{\text{start}},\;\tau(1)=q_{\text{goal}}$

One object, many policies

Every planner below searches this same set differently.

Printed pp. 353-402The Path-Planning Problem
We state the problem precisely: given a start configuration, a goal, and a collision model, return a continuous path lying entirely in free space, or report failure. Every later method is just a different policy for searching this same object, so naming it early keeps the chapter organized.

Configuration-Space Obstacles

Collision split

A checker labels every configuration free or forbidden.

Free space

$\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}} = \mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{\text{obs}}$

Warning

A simple workspace obstacle can twist into a complicated C-obstacle.

Printed pp. 353-402Configuration-Space Obstacles
A collision checker maps each candidate configuration to a yes or no verdict, splitting C-space into free and obstacle regions. The key lesson is that a simple workspace obstacle can carve a twisted, high-dimensional forbidden region, which is exactly why planning is hard and why we plan with points.

Planning a Collision-Free Path

The invariant

Stay inside $\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}}$ at every instant along the path.

Not the goal

Visual neatness is irrelevant; free-space membership is what must hold.

Reuse

Different machines now share one search vocabulary.

Printed pp. 353-402Planning a Collision-Free Path
Here the abstraction pays off: a robot body weaving past a table becomes a single point tracing a curve around a C-obstacle blob. We trace start to goal through the free region and stress that membership in free space, not visual neatness, is the invariant every planner must preserve.

Three Families of Planners

Complete

Always find a path if one exists, or prove none does, but scale badly with dimension.

Grid / resolution

Discretize and search; resolution complete at the chosen spacing.

Sampling

Random samples; probabilistically complete as samples grow.

Probabilistic completeness

$\Pr[\text{found by }n\text{ samples}]\to 1\;\text{ as } n\to\infty$

Trade-off

Each family swaps a guarantee for tractable cost. Know which guarantee you hold.

Printed pp. 353-402Three Families of Planners
We organize methods into complete, grid, and sampling families, each trading guarantees against cost. Complete planners promise an answer but scale poorly; grids are resolution complete; sampling planners are probabilistically complete. Knowing which guarantee a method offers tells you when a failure is the method's fault or the resolution's fault.

Graph Search: A* and Dijkstra

Dijkstra

Expand nodes by least accumulated cost $g(n)$ from the start.

A*

Order by $f(n)=g(n)+h(n)$ with an admissible heuristic.

Admissibility

$h(n)\le h^{\ast}(n)\;\Rightarrow\;A^{\ast}\text{ is optimal}$

Printed pp. 353-402Graph Search: A* and Dijkstra
Once a roadmap or grid exists, planning becomes shortest-path search. Dijkstra expands by accumulated cost; A* adds an admissible heuristic that never overestimates remaining distance, so it stays optimal while exploring fewer nodes. The admissibility condition is the load-bearing assumption behind that optimality claim.

Grid-Based Search and Wavefront

Wavefront

Flood distance-to-goal outward from the goal cell across free cells.

Descend

From the start, step to any neighbor with a smaller value.

Resolution complete

A corridor thinner than the spacing can vanish between samples.

Printed pp. 353-402Grid-Based Search and Wavefront
A grid planner discretizes C-space, marks blocked cells, and runs a wavefront flood from the goal so every free cell stores its distance-to-goal. Following decreasing values yields a path. It is resolution complete: a corridor narrower than the grid spacing can vanish between samples, so the planner may confidently solve the wrong problem.

Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM)

Sample

Draw random free configurations as roadmap nodes.

Connect

Link nearby nodes with a local planner into a reusable graph.

Narrow passages

Random samples rarely land in tightly constrained regions.

Printed pp. 353-402Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM)
PRM samples free configurations, connects nearby ones with a local planner into a reusable roadmap, then answers many queries by graph search. It excels in high dimensions where grids are hopeless, but narrow passages stay hard because random samples rarely land in tightly constrained regions of free space.

Rapidly-Exploring Random Trees

Extend step

Sample, find the nearest node, step a short distance toward it.

Growth rule

$q_{\text{new}} = q_{\text{near}} + \epsilon\,\dfrac{q_{\text{rand}}-q_{\text{near}}}{\lVert q_{\text{rand}}-q_{\text{near}}\rVert}$

RRT*

Rewire neighbors so cost converges toward optimal.

Printed pp. 353-402Rapidly-Exploring Random Trees
RRT grows a single tree rooted at the start. Each step samples a target, finds the nearest tree node, and extends a short step toward it, biasing growth into unexplored regions. It is single-query and probabilistically complete; RRT* rewires neighbors so the path cost converges toward optimal as samples increase.

Virtual Potential Fields

Attraction

The goal is a low-energy well pulling the robot in.

Repulsion

Obstacles are high-energy hills pushing the robot away.

Combined field

$U(q)=U_{\text{att}}(q)+U_{\text{rep}}(q),\quad \dot q=-\nabla U(q)$

Printed pp. 353-402Virtual Potential Fields
Potential fields treat the goal as an attractive well and obstacles as repulsive hills; the robot descends the combined gradient. The motion looks smooth and reactive, but superimposed fields can create spurious local minima that trap the robot short of the goal, which is the method's defining failure mode.

Nonlinear Optimization Planning

Path as variables

Optimize the configurations along the path directly.

One objective

Fold clearance, smoothness, and actuator limits into the cost and constraints.

Trajectory program

$\min_{q(\cdot)}\;J[q]\quad\text{s.t.}\;\; q(t)\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{free}},\;\; g(q,\dot q,\ddot q)\le 0$

Caveat

Nonconvex: solvers need a good initial guess and may find only a local optimum.

Printed pp. 353-402Nonlinear Optimization Planning
Optimization planning casts the path as decision variables and minimizes a cost subject to collision and dynamic constraints. It can fold smoothness, clearance, and actuator limits into one objective, but the program is nonconvex, so solvers need good initial guesses and may settle into local optima rather than the global best path.

Smoothing and Takeaways

Smoothing

Shorten and regularize a feasible path, re-checking clearance after every shortcut.

Protected invariant

Free-space membership, not visual smoothness.

Takeaway 1

Planning is geometry plus a search policy.

Takeaway 2

Resolution and sampling choices are part of the model.

Printed pp. 353-402Smoothing and Takeaways
Smoothing comes after feasibility: it shortens or regularizes a jagged feasible path while re-checking clearance and dynamic limits, since the protected invariant is free-space membership, not visual smoothness. The chapter's thesis is that planning equals geometry plus a search policy, and resolution and sampling choices are part of the model.
