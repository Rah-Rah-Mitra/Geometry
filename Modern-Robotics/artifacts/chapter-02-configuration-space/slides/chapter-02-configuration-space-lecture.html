Configuration and Degrees of Freedom

Configuration

The minimal data that locates every point of the robot.

DOF

The smallest number of real parameters that does it.

Dimension count

$\mathrm{dof} = \dim \mathcal{C}$

Point on a coin

A coin lying on a table: position plus heading gives three.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Configuration and Degrees of Freedom
A configuration is the complete specification of every point of the robot, captured as a single point of the configuration space. The number of degrees of freedom equals the dimension of that space: the smallest count of real parameters needed to pin the mechanism down completely and unambiguously.

Counting by Subtraction

Start free

Sum the freedoms of every body moving on its own.

Subtract joints

Each joint removes a fixed number of relative freedoms.

Summation principle

$\mathrm{dof} = \!\!\sum_{\text{bodies}}\!\! f_b \;-\; \!\!\sum_{\text{joints}}\!\! c_j$

Bookkeeping

Freedoms granted minus freedoms removed.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Counting by Subtraction
The summation principle counts freedoms by starting from all rigid bodies moving independently, then subtracting the constraints imposed by every joint. Each joint of a given type removes a fixed number of relative freedoms, so degrees of freedom equal total body freedoms minus total joint constraints across the mechanism.

Joint Types and Their Freedoms

Lower pairs

Revolute and prismatic give one freedom each.

Multi-freedom

Cylindrical gives two, spherical gives three.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Joint Types and Their Freedoms
Joints differ by how many relative freedoms they permit. A revolute or prismatic joint grants one, a cylindrical two, a spherical three. In the plane each joint type allows fewer freedoms than in space, so the same hardware constrains differently depending on whether motion is planar or spatial.

Grubler's Formula

One ground link

$N$ links, $J$ joints, joint $i$ has $f_i$ freedoms.

Rigid-body unit

$m=6$ in space, $m=3$ in the plane.

Mobility

$\mathrm{dof} = m(N-1-J) + \sum_{i=1}^{J} f_i$

Reading it

Loop closures are absorbed by the link/joint count.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Grubler's Formula
Grubler's formula packages the summation principle for mechanisms with one ground link. Degrees of freedom equal m times links-minus-joints-minus-one plus the sum of joint freedoms, where m is six for spatial and three for planar mechanisms. Ground and the loop closures are handled automatically by the count.

Worked Count: Four-Bar Linkage

Plug in

$\mathrm{dof} = 3(4-1-4) + 4(1) = 1$

Interpretation

One actuator drives the entire closed chain.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Worked Count: Four-Bar Linkage
The planar four-bar linkage has four links including ground and four revolute joints. With m equal to three, Grubler gives three times four minus four minus one, plus four, which equals one. A single freedom drives the whole closed loop, matching the crank we turn by hand.

Topology of the C-Space

Shape is intrinsic

The space has a fixed topology, set by the mechanism.

Examples

One revolute joint: a circle $S^1$. A wrist: a sphere $S^2$.

Products of pieces

$S^1\times S^1 = T^2,\qquad S^1\times \mathbb{R} = \text{cylinder}$

Coordinates differ

No relabeling turns a circle into a line segment.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Topology of the C-Space
Configuration spaces have an intrinsic shape independent of coordinates. A single revolute joint traces a circle, a spherical wrist sweeps a two-sphere, and two stacked revolute joints fill a torus. This topology is fixed by the mechanism; no choice of numbers can change a circle into a flat line.

The 2R Arm Torus

C-space of 2R arm

$\mathcal{C} = S^1 \times S^1 = T^2$

Chart warning

The flat angle square tears the real torus at its seams.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76The 2R Arm Torus
A two revolute arm has two angles, each living on its own circle, so its configuration space is the product of two circles, the torus. The flat angle square is only a chart: its left and right edges are the same points, and its top and bottom edges glue together as well.

Explicit versus Implicit

Explicit

Pick coordinates and map them onto the space.

Implicit

Write constraints; the solution set is the space.

Two views of $S^2$

$(\phi,\psi)\mapsto \text{point} \quad\text{vs.}\quad x^2+y^2+z^2=1$

Trade-off

Fewer coordinates can introduce chart singularities.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Explicit versus Implicit
A space can be described two ways. An explicit parametrization names a few coordinates and maps them onto the space, like joint angles for an arm. An implicit description writes constraint equations whose solution set is the space, like the unit-norm equation that carves a sphere out of three-dimensional coordinates.

Rigid-Body Configuration: SE(3)

Position

Three numbers locate the body frame origin.

Orientation

Three more numbers fix the frame's rotation.

Free body in space

$\mathcal{C} = SE(3),\qquad \dim = 6$

Planar case

$SE(2)$ has dimension three: $(x,y,\theta)$.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Rigid-Body Configuration: SE(3)
The configuration of a free rigid body in space needs three numbers for position and three for orientation, giving six degrees of freedom. These configurations form the special Euclidean group, a six dimensional manifold. In the plane the analog is three dimensional, with two translations and one rotation angle.

Holonomic versus Nonholonomic

Pfaffian constraint

$A(q)\,\dot q = 0,\quad \dot x\sin\phi - \dot y\cos\phi = 0$

Key difference

Holonomic: integrable, cuts dimension. Nonholonomic: not.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Holonomic versus Nonholonomic
Holonomic constraints are equations on configuration alone; they cut the dimension of the space and can be integrated. Nonholonomic constraints restrict velocities through Pfaffian forms that cannot be integrated into configuration equations. Rolling without slipping is the classic case: it limits motion directions yet still reaches every configuration.

Task Space and Workspace

Task space

Where the task lives, often end-effector poses.

Workspace

The set of poses the end-effector can reach.

Projection, not C-space

$\text{workspace} = \{\,x(q) : q \in \mathcal{C}\,\}$

Caution

Workspace pictures hide the torus they came from.

Printed pp. 11-58 · PDF pp. 29-76Task Space and Workspace
Task space is the space in which the robot's task is naturally expressed, often end-effector poses. The workspace is the set of configurations the end-effector can actually reach. Both are projections of the configuration space, useful pictures that must not be mistaken for the full C-space.
