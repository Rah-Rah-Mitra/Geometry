Trajectory Generation

Turning a geometric path into a timed, executable motion

Path

The geometric trace: where to go, parameterized by $s \in [0,1]$.

Trajectory

The path plus a clock: when to be there, $q(t)=q\big(s(t)\big)$.

Why it matters

Velocity and torque demands are born from the time scaling, not the drawing.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Trajectory Generation
This chapter, spanning printed pages 325 through 352, asks how geometric waypoints become a smooth, executable time history. We separate the path, which names where to go, from the trajectory, which names when to be there, and we keep both objects visible throughout the lecture.

Path versus Trajectory

Chain rule

For a straight joint move $q(s)=q_0+s(q_1-q_0)$, derivatives factor cleanly.

Shape vs. clock

The same path can be traversed by infinitely many clocks $s(t)$.

Velocity and acceleration

$\dot q(t)=\dot s\,(q_1-q_0)$

$\ddot q(t)=\ddot s\,(q_1-q_0)$

Consequence

Peak speed and torque live in $\dot s$ and $\ddot s$, the time-scaling derivatives.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Path versus Trajectory
A path is a geometric trace parameterized by s running from zero to one; a trajectory attaches a clock through the time scaling s of t. The chain rule shows velocity and acceleration are products of path shape and clock derivatives, so timing is a genuine design decision, not an afterthought.

Polynomial Time Scaling

Design rule

Boundary conditions decide polynomial degree.

Cubic

Four coefficients: fix endpoints and zero endpoint velocities.

Quintic

Six coefficients: also zero endpoint accelerations.

General form

$s(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k\,t^k$

Constraints fix degree

$s(0)=0,\ s(T)=1$

$\dot s(0)=\dot s(T)=0,\ \ddot s(0)=\ddot s(T)=0$

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Polynomial Time Scaling
Boundary conditions decide polynomial degree. A cubic has four coefficients, enough to pin both endpoints and zero the endpoint velocities, while a quintic adds two more coefficients so endpoint accelerations also vanish. The number of constraints you impose dictates how many free coefficients the scaling must carry.

Cubic Time Scaling

Cubic scaling (normalized $\tau=t/T$)

$s(\tau)=3\tau^2-2\tau^3$

$\dot s=\dfrac{6\tau-6\tau^2}{T},\quad \ddot s=\dfrac{6-12\tau}{T^2}$

Catch

$\ddot s(0)\neq 0$: a torque step the actuator cannot deliver cleanly.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Cubic Time Scaling
The cubic time scaling produces a parabolic velocity profile peaking at the midpoint and a linear acceleration profile that jumps discontinuously at the endpoints. That nonzero starting acceleration demands an instantaneous torque step, which torque-limited actuators cannot deliver cleanly, motivating the smoother quintic alternative.
Modern RoboticsChapter 9

Quintic Time Scaling

Quintic scaling (normalized $\tau=t/T$)

$s(\tau)=10\tau^3-15\tau^4+6\tau^5$

$\dot s=\dfrac{30\tau^2-60\tau^3+30\tau^4}{T}$

$\ddot s=\dfrac{60\tau-180\tau^2+120\tau^3}{T^2}$

Payoff

$\ddot s(0)=\ddot s(T)=0$: no torque jump at either endpoint.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Quintic Time Scaling
The quintic time scaling satisfies six boundary conditions: position, velocity, and acceleration at both ends. Its velocity rises and falls smoothly while acceleration starts and returns to zero, eliminating the torque jumps of the cubic. The notebook verifies the quintic endpoint acceleration norm falls below tolerance.

Comparing Time-Scaling Profiles

Cubic

Smooth position, but acceleration jumps at the ends.

Quintic

Smoothest acceleration; highest peak speed for a given $T$.

Trapezoidal

Lowest peak speed for fixed limits; most time-efficient.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Comparing Time-Scaling Profiles
Comparing profiles makes the tradeoff concrete. The trapezoidal scaling reaches a top speed fastest and is time efficient, the cubic is smoother in position, and the quintic is smoothest in acceleration. The path is identical in every case, so all differences in demand come from the scalar clock alone.

Trapezoidal and S-Curve Profiles

Three phases

Constant acceleration, constant-velocity coast, constant deceleration.

Optimality

Time-optimal under separate limits $\dot s_{\max}$ and $\ddot s_{\max}$.

S-curve

Bounds jerk by rounding the corners of the trapezoid.

Coast condition

$\dot s_{\max}\,T \;>\; 1$ requires a coast phase

$\ddot s = \pm\,\ddot s_{\max}\ \text{ on ramps, } 0 \text{ on coast}$

Tradeoff

Sharp trapezoid corners imply unbounded jerk; the S-curve fixes this.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Trapezoidal and S-Curve Profiles
A trapezoidal profile accelerates at a constant rate, coasts at a velocity limit, then decelerates symmetrically, making it the time-optimal scaling under separate velocity and acceleration bounds. Its acceleration is piecewise constant, so its jerk is unbounded at the corners, which the S-curve profile later rounds off.

How Duration Rescales Demands

Scaling laws for fixed path

$\dot q_{\max} \propto \dfrac{1}{T}, \qquad \ddot q_{\max} \propto \dfrac{1}{T^2}$

Notebook check

Over $T\in\{1,2,4\}$: speed ratio $=4$, acceleration ratio $=16$.

Practical rule

If a constraint is exceeded, slow the motion: doubling $T$ quarters peak acceleration.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370How Duration Rescales Demands
Stretching a fixed quintic move over duration T scales peak speed like one over T and peak acceleration like one over T squared. The notebook confirms a four-to-one speed ratio and sixteen-to-one acceleration ratio across durations, explaining why simply slowing a motion down recovers feasibility.

Joint-Space versus Task-Space Paths

Joint-space line

$q(s)=q_0+s(q_1-q_0)$. Simple, always feasible, but curved in task space.

Task-space line

Keeps the end-effector on a Cartesian straight line; intuitive for operators.

Risk

Straight Cartesian paths may cross singularities or violate joint limits.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Joint-Space versus Task-Space Paths
A joint-space straight line interpolates joint vectors and is simple but bends in task space. A task-space straight line keeps the end-effector on a Cartesian line, which is intuitive for the operator but may pass through singularities or exceed joint limits. The representation you choose shapes feasibility.

Trajectories Through Via Points

Via points

Pass near inspection or clearance poses without fully stopping.

Hidden pitfall

Interpolating positions alone can create velocity discontinuities at knots.

Fix

Match velocities (and accelerations) across spline segments.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Trajectories Through Via Points
Via points let a tool pass near inspection poses without stopping. Interpolating positions alone can hide velocity discontinuities at the knots, so a careful generator matches velocities and sometimes accelerations across segments. Smooth position is necessary but never sufficient for a torque-controlled machine.

Time-Optimal Scaling: the Phase Plane

Feasibility in $(s,\dot s)$

$\ddot s_{\min}(s,\dot s)\le \ddot s \le \ddot s_{\max}(s,\dot s)$

Limit curve

Above it, no admissible $\ddot s$ exists; actuator torque saturates.

Bang-bang policy

Maximum acceleration, then maximum braking, hugging the limit curve.

Printed pp. 325-352 / PDF pp. 343-370Time-Optimal Scaling: the Phase Plane
Time-optimal scaling along a fixed path becomes a feasibility problem in the s, s-dot phase plane. A maximum-velocity limit curve marks where any faster motion violates an actuator constraint. The optimal policy bangs between maximum acceleration and maximum braking while staying strictly below that limit curve.

Summary and Takeaways

Separate geometry from timing

Choose the path first, then design the clock $s(t)$.

Boundary derivatives are constraints

They set polynomial degree: cubic zeros velocity, quintic zeros acceleration.

Profiles trade time for smoothness

Trapezoid is fast; quintic is smoothest in torque demand.

Timing rescales demands

$\dot q_{\max}\propto 1/T$, $\ddot q_{\max}\propto 1/T^2$.

Phase-plane certificate

$\text{feasible} \iff (s,\dot s)\ \text{below the limit curve}$
