The Multivariate Gaussian

Density

$p(\mathbf{x}) = \dfrac{\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\big)}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\,|\Sigma|^{1/2}}$

Two parameters

Mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ locates the cloud; covariance $\Sigma$ sets its shape, scale, and tilt.

Level sets

$(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) = k^2$

Geometry

Constant-density contours are ellipsoids. The exponent is half the squared Mahalanobis distance.

Printed pp. 565-567The Multivariate Gaussian
The multivariate Gaussian is fixed by a mean vector and a covariance matrix. Its negative log density is, up to a constant, half the squared Mahalanobis distance, so the level sets of the density are exactly the ellipsoids of constant covariance-weighted distance. Diagonal entries set marginal variance; off-diagonal entries encode correlation.

Covariance Sets the Ellipse

Eigendecomposition

$\Sigma = U\Lambda U^{\!\top}$. Columns of $U$ are principal axes; eigenvalues are squared semi-axes.

Correlation tilts

Off-diagonal terms rotate the ellipse; unequal variances stretch it.

Why it matters

A pixel along a noisy axis is not worth a pixel along a precise axis.

Printed pp. 565-567Covariance Sets the Ellipse
Reading the covariance geometrically is the key skill here. Eigenvectors give the principal axes of the uncertainty ellipse and eigenvalues give the squared semi-axis lengths. Correlation rotates the ellipse away from the coordinate axes. A one-pixel residual along a noisy direction must not count the same as one along a tightly constrained direction.

Mahalanobis Distance

Squared distance

$d^2 = (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})$

Inverse covariance

$\Sigma^{-1}$ is part of the distance. It down-weights high-variance directions.

Whitening

$\mathbf{z} = \Sigma^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}),\quad d^2 = \mathbf{z}^{\!\top}\mathbf{z}$

Plain length

After whitening, $d^2$ is ordinary squared Euclidean length, so residuals compare fairly.

Printed pp. 565-567Mahalanobis Distance
Mahalanobis distance is the central object. The squared form is the residual sandwiched around the inverse covariance, which is exactly ordinary squared length measured after whitening. Whitening multiplies residuals by the inverse square root of covariance, turning an anisotropic correlated cloud into an isotropic unit cloud where Euclidean comparison is finally fair.

Whitening the Residual Cloud

Coordinate change

$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{z}) = \Sigma^{-1/2}\,\Sigma\,\Sigma^{-1/2} = I$

Equal footing

Every direction now carries the same uncertainty.

Gate becomes a radius

A Mahalanobis gate is simply $\|\mathbf{z}\| \le k$ in whitened space.

Printed pp. 565-567Whitening the Residual Cloud
This slide shows whitening as a coordinate change. The raw correlated ellipse maps to a circle once we apply the inverse square root of the covariance. In whitened coordinates the covariance becomes the identity, every direction carries equal uncertainty, and a Mahalanobis gate becomes a plain circular radius test.

Mahalanobis Follows Chi-square

Key fact

$\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)\ \Rightarrow\ d^2 \sim \chi^2_{r}$

Degrees of freedom

$r$ equals the residual rank, the dimension of the residual vector.

Sum of squares

$d^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} z_i^2,\quad z_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$

Reading

Chi-square is a sum of squared standardized residuals, one per whitened coordinate.

Printed pp. 565-567Mahalanobis Follows Chi-square
Here is the distributional fact that makes gates calibrated. If a residual of rank r is truly zero-mean Gaussian with the assumed covariance, its squared Mahalanobis distance follows a chi-square distribution with r degrees of freedom. Equivalently, chi-square is a sum of r independent squared standardized residuals, one per whitened coordinate.

Chi-square Confidence Gates

Acceptance rule

$\text{accept} \iff d^2 \le F^{-1}_{\chi^2_r}(\alpha)$

Two views, one gate

An ellipse in residual coordinates; a concentric circle after whitening.

$r = 2$ thresholds

$68\% \to 2.28$,   $95\% \to 5.99$,   $99\% \to 9.21$.

Printed pp. 565-567Chi-square Confidence Gates
A confidence gate accepts a residual when its squared Mahalanobis distance falls below a chi-square quantile at the chosen probability. We read off the 68, 95, and 99 percent thresholds for two degrees of freedom. These thresholds are ellipses in residual coordinates and concentric circles in whitened coordinates, the same gate seen two ways.

The Chi-square Density

Shape vs. dof

Mean is $r$; the density spreads and shifts right as $r$ grows.

Rank-dependent cutoff

Bigger residual vectors need a larger threshold for the same probability.

Tail = false rejects

The shaded area beyond the cutoff is the fraction of valid residuals wrongly gated out.

Printed pp. 565-567The Chi-square Density
The chi-square density shifts right and flattens as degrees of freedom grow, so the same probability level demands a larger threshold for larger residual vectors. The shaded tail beyond the quantile is the false-rejection rate. Forgetting that the threshold depends on residual rank is a classic calibration error in reconstruction pipelines.

Residual Diagnostics

Histogram vs. density

Plot sampled $d^2$ against the $\chi^2_r$ curve. A correct model overlays cleanly.

Coverage check

Roughly 68, 95, 99 percent of residuals should fall inside the matching gates.

What mismatch means

Underestimated covariance, ignored correlation, or genuine outliers.

Printed pp. 565-567Residual Diagnostics
The gate is only as good as the covariance model. If residuals are correctly modeled, their squared Mahalanobis values histogram onto the chi-square density and empirical gate coverage matches the target probabilities. Mismatch flags underestimated covariance, ignored correlation, or outliers. This diagnostic is a cheap, decisive health check for an estimator.

RANSAC and Goodness of Fit

RANSAC inlier test

$d^2_i \le \chi^2_r(\alpha)\ \Rightarrow\ \text{inlier}$

Principled threshold

The inlier cutoff is a chi-square quantile on per-point reprojection or Sampson residuals, not a guessed pixel count.

Goodness of fit

$\textstyle\sum_i d^2_i \sim \chi^2_{\,m-p}$

Test the model

Compare the total summed squared residual against chi-square with dof = constraints minus parameters.

Printed pp. 565-567RANSAC and Goodness of Fit
Chi-square gating drives two everyday tasks. In RANSAC the inlier threshold is a chi-square quantile on per-point reprojection or Sampson residuals, not an arbitrary pixel number. As a goodness-of-fit test, the total summed squared residual is compared against chi-square with degrees of freedom equal to the number of constraints minus parameters.

Pitfalls and Takeaways

Pitfalls

  • Euclidean gates on anisotropic residuals reject good points, accept bad ones.
  • Wrong degrees of freedom: dof must track residual rank.
  • Non-PSD or badly scaled $\Sigma$ makes distance meaningless.
  • Singular $\Sigma$: use the pseudo-inverse with rank, not dimension.

Takeaways

  • A Gaussian residual is fixed by mean and covariance; $\Sigma$ sets scale and direction.
  • Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean length after whitening.
  • Squared distance follows $\chi^2_r$ when the model is correct.
  • Gates are cumulative probability tests, not correctness guarantees.

Carry forward

State model, dimension, and probability level together for every gate.

Printed pp. 565-567Pitfalls and Takeaways
Close on the failure modes. Euclidean gates on anisotropic residuals reject good points and accept bad ones. The degrees of freedom must track residual rank. A non-positive-definite or badly scaled covariance makes distance meaningless, and a singular covariance forces the pseudo-inverse with rank in place of dimension. Density near the mean is not acceptance.
