Classifying homographies by their fixed points and fixed lines

We meet the planar homology, its degenerate cousin the elation, and the conjugate rotation, and we see why these special forms keep recurring across image mosaics and plane-induced transfers.

Fixed Points = Eigenvectors

Fixed point

A point with $H\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}$ is an eigenvector of $H$.

Fixed line

A line $\mathbf{l}$ with $H^{\!\top}\mathbf{l} = \lambda\mathbf{l}$ is an eigenvector of $H^{\!\top}$.

The classifier

Eigenvalue multiplicities name the transformation.

Eigenvalue patterns

$\{\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3\}$ distinct $\Rightarrow$ 3 fixed points

$\{\lambda,\lambda,\mu\}\Rightarrow$ homology

$\{1,e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta}\}\Rightarrow$ conjugate rotation

Why it matters

The spectrum decides how many correspondences fix the map.

Printed pp. 628-633Fixed Points and Eigenstructure
Every plane projectivity is read through the eigenvectors of its three by three matrix. A fixed point is an eigenvector, and a fixed line is an eigenvector of the transpose. The pattern of eigenvalues, whether real and distinct, repeated, or a complex conjugate pair, names the transformation and tells us how many correspondences pin it down.

The Planar Homology

Axis

A whole line of fixed points: two equal eigenvalues.

Vertex

One extra fixed point off the axis: the third eigenvalue.

Canonical rank-one form

$$H = I + (\mu-1)\,\frac{\mathbf{v}\,\mathbf{a}^{\!\top}}{\mathbf{v}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}}$$

$\mathbf{a}$ = axis, $\mathbf{v}$ = vertex, $\mu$ = characteristic ratio

Eigenvalues

$\{1,1,\mu\}$: the axis direction is repeated.

Printed pp. 628-633The Planar Homology
A planar homology fixes a whole line of points, the axis, plus one extra point off it, the vertex. Two eigenvalues coincide because the axis is a repeated fixed direction; the third differs. Algebraically the matrix is the identity plus a rank-one term built from the vertex and the axis, scaled by a single characteristic ratio.

Homology Geometry

Axis fixed

Every point on $\mathbf{a}$ maps to itself: $H\mathbf{x}\simeq\mathbf{x}$.

Vertex rays

$\mathbf{x}$, $H\mathbf{x}$, and $\mathbf{v}$ are collinear.

Axis crossing

Each ray meets the axis at a fixed point $\mathbf{i}$.

Printed pp. 628-633Homology Geometry
Walk the picture: the axis is fixed pointwise, so any axis point maps to itself. Off the axis, a point and its image lie on a line through the vertex, and that line meets the axis at a fixed point. Four collinear elements give the characteristic cross-ratio that is constant along every ray through the vertex.

The Characteristic Cross-Ratio

One invariant

The third eigenvalue ratio $\mu$ equals a cross-ratio on every vertex ray.

Same on all rays

Constancy is the diagnostic the notebook asserts.

Cross-ratio of four collinear points

$$\mu = \{\mathbf{v},\,\mathbf{i};\,\mathbf{x},\,H\mathbf{x}\}$$

vertex, axis crossing, source, image

Printed pp. 628-633The Characteristic Cross-Ratio
The third eigenvalue ratio of a homology is exactly the cross-ratio of four points on any line through the vertex: the vertex, the axis intersection, the source point, and its image. Because this ratio is the same on every such ray, it is a single invariant that fully describes the homology once axis and vertex are fixed.

The Elation: Vertex On The Axis

Defining incidence

$$\mathbf{a}^{\!\top}\mathbf{v}=0,\qquad H = I + \mathbf{v}\,\mathbf{a}^{\!\top}$$

rank-one term is now nilpotent

Eigenvalues

$\{1,1,1\}$ — all coincide; a projective shear.

Still fixes the axis

Points on $\mathbf{a}$ remain fixed pointwise.

Printed pp. 628-633The Elation
An elation is the limiting homology whose vertex slides onto the axis. The defining incidence is that the axis line contains the vertex, written as the axis dotted with the vertex equals zero. Now all three eigenvalues coincide, the rank-one term is nilpotent, and the map acts like a projective shear that still fixes the axis pointwise.

The Harmonic Homology

Cross-ratio $=-1$

The characteristic ratio is the harmonic value $\mu=-1$.

Involution

$H^2 = I$: applying it twice is the identity.

Harmonic special case

$$H = I - 2\,\frac{\mathbf{v}\,\mathbf{a}^{\!\top}}{\mathbf{v}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}},\qquad H^2=I$$

vertex and image form a harmonic range with the axis crossing

In vision

Models planar reflective symmetry and perspective mirror images.

Printed pp. 628-633The Harmonic Homology
When the characteristic cross-ratio equals minus one, the homology is harmonic and becomes an involution: applying it twice returns the identity. The vertex and any point form a harmonic range with the axis crossing and the image. Harmonic homologies model planar reflective symmetry and the relation between an object and its mirror image under perspective.

The Conjugate Rotation

A rotation through a homography

$$H = T\,R(\theta)\,T^{-1}$$

$R(\theta)$ Euclidean rotation, $T$ an arbitrary projectivity

Eigenvalues survive

Conjugation preserves the spectrum: $\{1,e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta}\}$.

Shape does not

The warp $T$ destroys Euclidean angles and circles.

Printed pp. 628-633The Conjugate Rotation
A conjugate rotation is a Euclidean rotation viewed through a homography: take a rotation matrix and sandwich it between a projectivity and its inverse. Conjugation preserves eigenvalues, so the map keeps the spectrum one and a complex conjugate pair on the unit circle, encoding the rotation angle even though Euclidean shape is gone.

Recovering The Hidden Angle

Read the spectrum

The angle is the argument of the complex eigenvalue pair.

Warp-invariant

Whatever $T$ is, the recovered $\theta$ is unchanged.

Angle from eigenvalues

$$\theta = \big|\arg \lambda_{2,3}\big|,\quad \lambda_{2,3}=e^{\pm i\theta}$$

Lab check

A $36^\circ$ rotation conjugated by a generic $T$ is recovered to machine precision.

Printed pp. 628-633Recovering the Hidden Angle
Because eigenvalues survive conjugation, the rotation angle is read directly from the complex eigenvalue pair of the conjugated matrix, regardless of the warping homography. In the lab a thirty-six degree rotation is conjugated by a generic projectivity and the angle is recovered to machine precision from the argument of the eigenvalue.

Where These Arise In Vision

Panoramic mosaics

A camera rotating about its center induces a conjugate rotation between images.

Plane + screw motion

Two views of a plane related by a screw are linked by a homology.

Plane through epipoles

The map a world plane induces between images, composed with the epipoles, is a homology.

The payoff

Knowing the class predicts the fixed axis, vertex, and the correspondences needed.

Printed pp. 628-633Why Vision Cares
These forms are not curiosities. A camera rotating about its center induces a conjugate rotation between images, the backbone of panoramic mosaics. Two views of a plane related by a screw motion, or the map a world plane induces between images through the epipoles, both turn out to be homologies, so the classification predicts their fixed geometry.
