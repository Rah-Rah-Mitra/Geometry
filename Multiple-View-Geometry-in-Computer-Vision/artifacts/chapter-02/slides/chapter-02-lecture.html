Points and Lines in Homogeneous Coordinates

A point

$\mathbf{x}=(x_1,x_2,x_3)^\top$ with $\mathbf{x}\sim\lambda\mathbf{x}$; finite point $(X,Y)\mapsto(X,Y,1)$.

A line

$\mathbf{l}=(a,b,c)^\top$ for $ax+by+c=0$, also up to scale.

Incidence

$\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{l} = 0 \iff \mathbf{x} \text{ lies on } \mathbf{l}$

One test

A single dot product decides whether any point lies on any line.

Printed pp. 25-64Points and Lines
A homogeneous three-vector represents a point only up to nonzero scale, so the equivalence class is what matters. The same vectors name lines, and incidence reduces to a single dot product vanishing. Emphasize that the trailing coordinate distinguishes finite points from ideal directions at infinity.

Duality: Join and Meet as Cross Products

Join: line through two points

$\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{x} \times \mathbf{x}'$

Meet: intersection of two lines

$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{l} \times \mathbf{l}'$

Principle of duality

Swap the words point and line and any theorem stays true.

Printed pp. 25-64Duality
Stress the symmetry between points and lines. The line through two points and the intersection of two lines are both cross products, so any true statement stays true after swapping the words point and line. This duality is the single most labor-saving idea in the projective toolbox.

Ideal Points and the Line at Infinity

Ideal point

Parallel lines meet where the last coordinate vanishes: $(d_1,d_2,0)^\top$ names a direction.

Line at infinity

$\mathbf{l}_\infty = (0,0,1)^\top$

Perspective

A homography sends $\mathbf{l}_\infty$ to a finite vanishing line in the image.

Printed pp. 25-64Line at Infinity
Walk through why parallel lines meet at an ideal point whose last coordinate is zero. All ideal points lie on one special line at infinity. A homography can send that line to a finite vanishing line, which is exactly how perspective images fold infinity into the picture.

Conics and Dual Conics

Point conic

$\mathbf{x}^\top C\,\mathbf{x} = 0,\quad C=C^\top$

Pole-polar & dual conic

$\mathbf{l} = C\,\mathbf{x},\qquad C^{*} = C^{-1}$

Five points

Five points in general position fix a conic uniquely.

Printed pp. 25-64Conics
Introduce the point conic as a symmetric matrix and the dual conic of tangent lines as its inverse. Show that five points fix a conic. The pole-polar relation packages tangency, polar lines of exterior points, and the contact-point geometry into one matrix-vector product.

Projective Transformations (Homographies)

Definition

$\mathbf{x}' = H\,\mathbf{x},\quad H\in\mathrm{GL}_3,\ \det H\neq 0$

Degrees of freedom

Defined up to scale, so $H$ carries $8$ degrees of freedom.

Action on objects

$\mathbf{l}' = H^{-\top}\mathbf{l},\qquad C' = H^{-\top} C\, H^{-1}$

Read carefully

Points push by $H$; lines and conics use the inverse transpose. Different rules, same map.

Printed pp. 25-64Homographies
Define a homography as an invertible three-by-three matrix acting on homogeneous points up to scale, giving eight degrees of freedom. Make the action on lines explicit: lines transform by the inverse transpose, and conics by a congruence. These three rules cover every object on the plane.

Lines Stay Lines; the Cross-Ratio Survives

Collinearity

A homography cannot bend a line into a curve; incidence is preserved exactly.

Cross-ratio of four collinear points

$\mathrm{Cr} = \dfrac{|\mathbf{x}_1\mathbf{x}_2|\,|\mathbf{x}_3\mathbf{x}_4|}{|\mathbf{x}_1\mathbf{x}_3|\,|\mathbf{x}_2\mathbf{x}_4|}$

Printed pp. 25-64Cross-Ratio
A homography may bend the visual spacing of a grid but never turns a line into a curve. On a single line it induces a one-dimensional projectivity, so the cross-ratio of four collinear points survives even though distances and ratios of distances along the line change.

The Transformation Hierarchy

Euclidean ⊂ Similarity

Rotation, translation, then uniform scale: $\begin{bmatrix}sR&\mathbf{t}\\\mathbf{0}^\top&1\end{bmatrix}$.

Affine ⊂ Projective

$\begin{bmatrix}A&\mathbf{t}\\\mathbf{0}^\top&1\end{bmatrix}$ then a full $\begin{bmatrix}A&\mathbf{t}\\\mathbf{v}^\top&w\end{bmatrix}$.

Printed pp. 25-64Hierarchy
Lay out the ladder from projective down through affine and similarity to Euclidean. Each rung adds constraints to the matrix and gains invariants: length, angle, parallelism, area ratio, cross-ratio. Reading the hierarchy by what each level preserves makes the whole chapter memorable.

Invariants: What Each Level Preserves

Euclidean

Length, angle, area — rigid motions.

Similarity

Angle, ratios of lengths; fixes the circular points $I,J$.

Affine

Parallelism, ratios of areas; fixes $\mathbf{l}_\infty$ as a set, loses angle.

Circular points

$I=(1,i,0)^\top,\quad J=(1,-i,0)^\top$

Printed pp. 25-64Invariants
Connect each subgroup to a fixed geometric object. Projectivities fix nothing special; affinities fix the line at infinity as a set; similarities and Euclidean maps additionally fix the circular points. The invariants form a nested ladder, with each named quantity surviving exactly the maps below it.

Affine Rectification from the Vanishing Line

Find $\mathbf{l}_\infty$

Two pairs of imaged parallels give two vanishing points; join them.

Send it home

$H_A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\l_1&l_2&l_3\end{bmatrix}$

Result

Parallelism and area ratios become measurable.

Printed pp. 25-64Affine Rectification
A perspective image of a plane differs from the plane by a homography. Two vanishing points join to give the imaged line at infinity. Mapping that line back to its canonical position removes the projective part, so parallelism and ratios of areas become measurable in the rectified chart.

Metric Rectification from Orthogonal Pairs

What remains

After affine rectification the residual distortion is a symmetric positive definite $2\times2$ matrix $S$.

One right angle, one constraint

$\mathbf{l}^\top S\,\mathbf{m}=0$

Recover

$S^{-1/2}$ removes anisotropy, leaving only a similarity ambiguity.

Printed pp. 25-64Metric Rectification
After affine rectification the line at infinity is fixed but the circular points are not. The residual distortion is a symmetric positive definite two-by-two matrix, and each known right angle gives one linear constraint. Its inverse square root removes anisotropy, leaving only a similarity ambiguity.

Synthesis and Takeaways

Unify

Homogeneous coordinates make joins, meets, and incidence one algebra.

Transform

Points use $H$; lines and conics use $H^{-\top}$.

Invariant

The cross-ratio is the basic projective invariant on a line.

Structure ladder

$\mathbf{l}_\infty$ carries affine structure; the circular points carry metric structure.

Same algebra, different objects

$\text{conic polarity} \;\equiv\; \text{fixed eigenstructure}$

Printed pp. 25-64Takeaways
Tie the threads together: homogeneous coordinates unify incidence, points and lines transform by different rules, the cross-ratio is the basic projective invariant, the line at infinity carries affine structure, and the circular points carry metric structure. Conic polarity and fixed eigenstructure are the same algebra on different objects.
