Least-squares Minimization

The linear-algebra engine behind every estimator in the book

Inhomogeneous fit

$\min_x \lVert Ax-b\rVert$ — the closest reachable prediction to a target $b$.

Homogeneous fit

$\min_x \lVert Ax\rVert$ with $\lVert x\rVert=1$ — the best null direction of $A$.

Why it matters

DLT, homography, camera, and fundamental-matrix estimation all reduce to these two solves.

This appendix spans printed pages 588 through 596 and supplies the linear-algebra engine behind almost every estimator in the book. We separate two problems: the inhomogeneous fit that minimizes the distance from b to a column space, and the homogeneous fit that hunts for a null direction. Both are projection statements in disguise.

Two Templates, One Toolbox

Inhomogeneous

$\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \lVert Ax-b\rVert_2$

Target present; overdetermined $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $m>n$.

Solved by

Normal equations, QR, or the pseudo-inverse $A^{+}b$.

Homogeneous

$\hat{x} = \arg\min_{\lVert x\rVert=1} \lVert Ax\rVert_2$

No target; the unit vector $A$ shrinks the most.

Solved by

The right singular vector for the smallest singular value of $A$.

Printed pp. 588-596Two Problems
Frame the appendix as exactly two templates. The inhomogeneous problem has a target vector b and asks for the closest reachable prediction. The homogeneous problem has no target and asks for the unit vector that A annihilates most nearly. Almost every DLT step in the book instantiates the second template.

The Geometric View: Orthogonal Projection

Reachable set

$\{Ax : x\in\mathbb{R}^n\}$ is the column space, a flat through the origin.

Best prediction

$A\hat{x}$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $b$ onto that flat.

Certificate

The residual $r=b-A\hat{x}$ is orthogonal to every column: $A^{\top}r=0$.

Printed pp. 588-596Geometric View
Walk students slowly through the projection picture. The set of all predictions Ax is the column space, a flat through the origin. The closest point to b is the foot of the perpendicular, and the residual that connects them is orthogonal to every column. That orthogonality is the entire optimality certificate.

The Normal Equations

Orthogonality condition

$A^{\top}(b-A\hat{x})=0$

Normal equations

$A^{\top}A\,\hat{x} = A^{\top}b$

Structure

$A^{\top}A$ is square, symmetric, positive definite when $A$ has full column rank.

The hidden cost

Forming $A^{\top}A$ is algebraically clean but numerically lossy — it squares the conditioning of the data.

When safe

Fine for well-conditioned, small, dense problems; treat as a derivation tool, not always the solver.

Printed pp. 588-596Normal Equations
Derive the normal equations by demanding the residual be orthogonal to each column, which is A transpose times the residual equals zero. The result is a square symmetric system. Emphasize it is correct but forms A transpose A explicitly, an operation that we will see squares the conditioning of the data.

Conditioning: Why Normal Equations Bite

Key inequality

$\kappa(A^{\top}A) \approx \kappa(A)^2$

Consequence

Nearly dependent columns roughly double the lost digits when you solve via $A^{\top}A$.

Notebook diagnostic

A log-log plot tracks observed $\kappa(A^{\top}A)$ against the $\kappa(A)^2$ reference line.

Printed pp. 588-596Conditioning
This is the practical warning of the appendix. Solving the normal equations squares the condition number, so nearly dependent columns turn a stable fit into a fragile one. The notebook's log-log plot tracks observed conditioning against the square reference; tie the solution gap annotations to lost digits of accuracy.

Stable Solvers: QR and the Pseudo-inverse

QR factorization

$A=QR,\quad R\hat{x}=Q^{\top}b$

Orthogonalize columns; never form $A^{\top}A$.

SVD / pseudo-inverse

$A=U\Sigma V^{\top},\quad \hat{x}=A^{+}b=V\Sigma^{+}U^{\top}b$

Why preferred

QR and SVD work on $\kappa(A)$, not $\kappa(A)^2$, so accuracy survives ill-conditioning.

Consistency

At full column rank, $A^{+}b$ equals the normal-equation answer — same point, safer arithmetic.

Bonus

The SVD also reveals rank and the smallest singular value, which the homogeneous problem needs.

Printed pp. 588-596QR and SVD
Present QR and SVD as the stable alternatives that never form A transpose A. QR orthogonalizes the columns; SVD factors A into rotations and stretches and gives the pseudo-inverse directly. For full column rank the pseudo-inverse reproduces the normal-equation answer without squaring the condition number.

The Homogeneous Problem $\min\lVert Ax\rVert,\ \lVert x\rVert=1$

Why constrain?

Without $\lVert x\rVert=1$, the trivial $x=0$ wins; the unit sphere removes that escape.

Rayleigh quotient

$\dfrac{\lVert Ax\rVert^2}{\lVert x\rVert^2}=\dfrac{x^{\top}A^{\top}A\,x}{x^{\top}x}$

Minimizer

$\hat{x}=v_n$, the right singular vector of the smallest singular value $\sigma_n$.

Printed pp. 588-596Homogeneous Problem
Now switch templates. With no target vector, scaling x to zero would trivially win, so we constrain the norm to one. Minimizing the Rayleigh quotient over the unit sphere selects the right singular vector belonging to the smallest singular value. That vector is the best approximate null direction of A.

Where DLT Lives: The Null-space Estimate

Build

Stack each correspondence into rows of a measurement matrix $A$.

Ideal

Noise-free data has an exact null vector $h$ encoding the true homography or fundamental matrix.

Reality

With noise, $\det$-zero fails; take the smallest singular vector as the least-squares fit, then reshape.

DLT template

$Ah=0,\ \lVert h\rVert=1 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat{h}=v_{\min}(A)$

Pervasive

2D homography, camera $P$, and the fundamental matrix $F$ all use this exact homogeneous solve.

Printed pp. 588-596DLT Null-space
Connect the homogeneous solver to the direct linear transform. Stacking point correspondences builds a measurement matrix whose exact null vector would be the true homography or fundamental matrix. With noise there is no exact null vector, so we take the smallest singular vector as the least-squares estimate, then reshape it.

Total vs Ordinary Least Squares

Ordinary LS

Trusts the horizontal coordinate; minimizes vertical residuals only.

Total LS

Treats both coordinates as noisy; minimizes orthogonal distance to the line.

For images

Both image coordinates are measured, so the orthogonal residual is the honest one.

Printed pp. 588-596Total Least Squares
Contrast ordinary and total least squares using the line-fit panel. Ordinary least squares trusts the horizontal coordinate and measures vertical residuals; total least squares treats both image coordinates as noisy and minimizes orthogonal distance via the smallest singular vector. In image geometry the orthogonal residual is the honest one.

Weighted and Constrained Least Squares

Weighted

$\min_x (Ax-b)^{\top}W(Ax-b)$

$\Rightarrow A^{\top}WA\,\hat{x}=A^{\top}Wb$

Meaning

$W$ encodes confidence — trusted rows pull harder; it bends the inner product, not the geometry.

Constrained (Lagrange)

$\mathcal{L}=\lVert Ax-b\rVert^2+\lambda^{\top}(Cx-d)$

Solution

Stationarity gives a bordered KKT system mixing $x$ and multipliers $\lambda$, enforcing $Cx=d$ exactly.

Printed pp. 588-596Weighted and Constrained
Cover the two modeling extensions. Weights rescale rows by measurement confidence so trusted observations pull harder, giving weighted normal equations. Linear constraints are enforced with Lagrange multipliers, producing a bordered system. Both keep the projection structure intact while changing the inner product or the feasible set.

Structure is geometry
