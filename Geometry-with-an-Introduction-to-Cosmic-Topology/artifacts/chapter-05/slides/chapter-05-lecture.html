Hyperbolic Geometry As A Measurement System

The Poincare disk and upper half-plane turn non-Euclidean geometry into a precise laboratory: geodesics, distance, circles, parallelism, and triangle area are all controlled by a metric that grows toward infinity.

Poincare disk Upper half-plane Mobius invariance Triangle defect

Open by framing the lecture as a shift in measurement, not a shift in drawing skill. The disk is familiar Euclidean paper, but its ruler changes as points approach the boundary. That one decision explains why the boundary is infinitely far away, why geodesics bend in the drawing, and why hyperbolic triangles have area measured by angle defect.

The Disk Boundary Is Infinity

Equal Euclidean strokes represent very different hyperbolic lengths.

Definition

The Poincare disk is \(D=\{z: |z|<1\}\) equipped with the metric \(ds=2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)\).

Important distinction

The unit circle is not part of the space. It records directions to infinity and ideal endpoints for geodesics.

Spend time on the metric density before discussing any curved lines. Students often think the boundary circle is an edge of the space, but it is not included. The diagram makes the key claim visual: equal-looking Euclidean steps near the boundary represent much larger hyperbolic travel than equal-looking steps near the center.

Geodesics Meet The Boundary Orthogonally

Notebook-derived diagram: diameters and orthogonal arcs are hyperbolic lines.

Geodesic test

A hyperbolic line in the disk is a Euclidean diameter or a circular arc whose supporting circle is orthogonal to \( |z|=1 \).

Inspection target

At each ideal endpoint, the tangent of the drawn arc should be perpendicular to the tangent of the boundary circle.

Now turn the metric rule into straightness. In the Poincare disk, hyperbolic lines are Euclidean diameters or circular arcs that strike the boundary at right angles. The visual test is deliberately local: at each ideal endpoint, the geodesic circle must be perpendicular to the unit circle, not merely aesthetically curved.

Mobius Maps Are Hyperbolic Motions

Disk automorphism

\(T_a(z)=e^{i\theta}\dfrac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z}\), with \( |a|<1 \)

Invariant behavior

These maps preserve the disk, send geodesics to geodesics, preserve angles, and preserve hyperbolic distance.

Link this slide back to inversions and clines. Disk-preserving Mobius transformations move points and geodesics while preserving the hyperbolic metric. The formula is less important than the behavior: one can recenter a calculation at the origin, compute there, and map the answer back without changing hyperbolic length or angle.

Use the Cayley transform as a change of coordinates. The disk and upper half-plane pictures look different, but they encode the same geometry. Diameters and orthogonal arcs in the disk become vertical rays or semicircles orthogonal to the real axis in the half-plane, which is often more convenient for algebraic distance calculations.

Distance Is A Cross-Ratio Computation

Invariance principle

Distance can be read from the ideal endpoints of the geodesic through \(p\) and \(q\). Mobius maps preserve the relevant cross-ratio.

Practical move

Map the geodesic to a diameter or vertical ray, compute the simple formula there, and map the result back.

Introduce the distance formula as an invariant, not as a mysterious analytic expression. The two ideal endpoints on the same geodesic provide the reference scale. The logarithm of a cross-ratio records how far two interior points sit between those endpoints, and Mobius invariance explains why the answer is independent of the model.

Circles Look Distorted Near Infinity

Notebook-derived diagram: hyperbolic circles are metrically round even when Euclidean coordinates compress them.

Center matters

The same hyperbolic radius can occupy different Euclidean footprints depending on where the center sits in the disk.

Do not infer length by eye

Near the boundary, the metric density makes small Euclidean displacements expensive in hyperbolic distance.

Use the notebook artifact to separate hyperbolic radius from Euclidean radius. A hyperbolic circle centered near the origin can look fairly round in ordinary coordinates, while one near the boundary is compressed toward the interior. The object is still metrically round; the distortion is caused by the model's nonuniform conversion from Euclidean to hyperbolic scale.

Parallelism Splits Into Three Behaviors

Classify by ideal endpoints: intersecting, limiting parallel, or ultraparallel.

Meeting

Two geodesics cross inside the disk. Their intersection has a genuine hyperbolic angle.

Limiting

They share one ideal endpoint and approach each other only at infinity.

Ultraparallel

They share no ideal endpoint and admit a common perpendicular geodesic.

This is the conceptual center of hyperbolic geometry. Through a point off a line, there are two limiting parallels that share an ideal endpoint with the given line, and many ultraparallels that do not meet it even at infinity. Ask students to identify the behavior by ideal endpoints rather than by Euclidean visual slope.

Angles Survive, Lengths Do Not

Conformal model

The disk and half-plane preserve angles at intersections. This makes triangle angle sums visually meaningful.

Metric caution

Euclidean segment length is not hyperbolic length. Area also needs the hyperbolic density, not ordinary coordinate area.

The Poincare models are conformal, so Euclidean drawing angles equal hyperbolic angles at intersections. This is a gift for triangle work. Lengths and areas, however, are not Euclidean readings from the page. Keep pointing back to the metric density: angle measurement is local shape preservation, while length and area depend on the changing scale factor.

Triangle Defect Is Area

Notebook-derived diagram: area increases as the angle sum drops below \( \pi \).

Gauss-Bonnet in constant curvature -1

Area(\(\triangle\)) = \(\pi - (A+B+C)\)

Instructional test

Move a vertex toward the ideal boundary. The Euclidean picture stretches, the angles shrink, and the predicted area grows.

State the theorem as a measurement principle. In curvature negative one, a hyperbolic triangle has angle sum less than pi, and the missing angle is exactly its area. This reverses Euclidean habit: large triangles do not have arbitrarily large angle sums; instead, their angles collapse as vertices move toward the ideal boundary.

Common Mistakes To Catch

Boundary error

Treating \( |z|=1 \) as reachable points inside the space.

Length error

Measuring arc length with the Euclidean ruler shown on the page.

Line error

Calling a circular arc geodesic without checking orthogonality to the boundary.

Correction habit

Before computing, ask which model is being used, which transformations preserve the geometry, and which visible quantities are actual hyperbolic invariants.

Fast sanity checks
  • Geodesic endpoints lie on the ideal boundary.
  • Angles can be read directly in a Poincare model.
  • Distances and areas require the hyperbolic metric.
Use this warning slide before assigning computations. The most common errors are all model-confusion errors: treating the boundary as a reachable circle, using Euclidean length along arcs, calling every Euclidean circle arc a geodesic, or forgetting that the upper half-plane and disk pictures are related by a coordinate change.
