Curvature As A Local Measurement

Curvature From Circle Growth

Diagnostic

For small radius \(r\), the sign of \(C_k(r)-2\pi r\) distinguishes positive, zero, and negative curvature.

Interpretation

Positive curvature crowds geodesics; negative curvature spreads them. The circumference error is a ruler-based observation.

Estimate \(k\) by plotting \(2\pi r-C(r)\) against \(r^3\).

Treat the image as an experimental instrument. In flat geometry the circumference grows as two pi r. Positive curvature makes circles fall short, while negative curvature makes them grow too quickly. The key point is local: an inhabitant can estimate curvature without seeing the whole surface, using only radius and circumference measurements.
The Unified Family \((X_k,G_k)\)

Scaled Model Spaces

\(k>0\)

Spherical or elliptic behavior; geodesics reconverge and triangle angle sums exceed \(\pi\).

\(k=0\)

Euclidean behavior; scale changes the ruler but not the flatness.

\(k<0\)

Hyperbolic behavior; geodesics diverge and area is tied to angle defect.

This slide introduces the unified notation X sub k without making students memorize three unrelated geometries. The sign of k chooses the family, while the magnitude sets the ruler. Emphasize that rescaling lengths changes numerical curvature, so comparisons across models need a consistent curvature scale.
Hyperbolic Memory

Parallelism Still Remembers Curvature

Measurement

\(\Pi(d)=2\arctan(e^{-d/R})\)

The limiting parallel angle decreases with distance from the line.

In Euclidean geometry, parallelism ignores distance. In hyperbolic geometry, it records a metric scale.

The angle of parallelism is a useful bridge back to the hyperbolic chapter. It shows that distance from a line controls the limiting angle of a nonintersecting geodesic. Students should notice that this is not a Euclidean phenomenon with a fixed parallel direction; it is a curvature-dependent measurement.
Boundary Words

From Polygons To Surfaces

Input

A polygon plus a cyclic boundary word with paired labels and orientations.

Counts

Identify equivalent vertices, count edge pairs, and keep the polygon as one face.

Output

\(\chi=V-E+F\)

A compact surface type becomes a computable invariant.

Move slowly through the boundary word idea. A polygon is not the surface yet; it is an instruction sheet for gluing. Once edges are paired with orientations, vertices merge into equivalence classes, and the counts of vertices, edges, and faces produce Euler characteristic. This is topology made computable.
Computable Classification

Orientability And Euler Characteristic

Orientable Family

\(\chi=2-2g\)

Sphere, torus, double torus, and higher genus surfaces.

Nonorientable Family

\(\chi=2-n\)

Projective plane sums and crosscap models.

The classifier is a bookkeeping lens, not a substitute for constructing the gluing.

Use the classifier as a dashboard rather than a black box. Orientability records whether local orientation survives every gluing cycle. Euler characteristic compresses vertex, edge, and face bookkeeping into one invariant. The classification theorem then says that compact connected surfaces are organized by orientability and genus or crosscap number.
Curvature Meets Topology

Gauss-Bonnet As A Conversion Rate

Closed Surface

\(\int_S K\,dA=2\pi\chi(S)\)

Constant Curvature

\(K\operatorname{Area}(S)=2\pi\chi(S)\)

The sign of \(K\) must agree with the sign of \(\chi\) when area is positive.

With boundary, add geodesic curvature and corner turning terms before comparing to \(2\pi\chi\).

Frame Gauss-Bonnet as the chapter's exchange rate between analysis and topology. The integral of Gaussian curvature plus boundary turning terms equals two pi times Euler characteristic. For closed constant-curvature surfaces, the equation becomes especially sharp: area, curvature sign, and topology constrain one another.
Reading The Surface Map

Which Topologies Accept Which Curvatures

\(\chi>0\)

Positive total curvature; the sphere is the model example.

\(\chi=0\)

Flat total curvature; the torus fits a Euclidean quotient.

\(\chi<0\)

Negative total curvature; higher genus surfaces fit hyperbolic uniformization.

This is the moment to connect the map to examples. The sphere has positive Euler characteristic and fits positive curvature. The torus has zero Euler characteristic and admits a flat quotient model. Higher genus surfaces have negative Euler characteristic, so constant negative curvature becomes the natural uniform geometry.
Geometry By Identification

Quotients Make Surfaces From Space

Definition

A quotient identifies \(x\sim \gamma x\) for each group element \(\gamma\in\Gamma\).

Geometric Requirement

The group should act by isometries, freely enough, and properly discontinuously enough to inherit a clean surface.

Caution

Fixed points produce singular behavior; not every quotient is a smooth surface.

The quotient construction turns a model geometry into a surface by declaring points equivalent under a group of isometries. Stress the clean action condition: if the group has fixed points or pathological accumulation, the quotient may fail to be a smooth surface. Good quotient geometry depends on the group action, not just the model space.
Fundamental Domains

Dirichlet Domains Are Local Maps

Construction

\(D(p)=\{x:d(x,p)\le d(x,\gamma p)\ \forall\gamma\in\Gamma\}\)

Bisectors between \(p\) and translated images \(\gamma p\) become the walls of the observer's domain.

The domain is a navigational chart for the quotient, not a claim that the surface has a boundary.

A Dirichlet domain is the region closer to a chosen basepoint than to any of its translated images. It is a practical way for an inhabitant to draw a fundamental domain from distance comparisons. The bisectors encode which copies of the basepoint become neighbors after quotienting.
Live Computation

Instructor Board Work

1

Pick a boundary word, for example \(aba^{-1}b^{-1}\) or \(aa^{-1}bb^{-1}\).

2

Mark paired edges and trace vertex equivalence classes until every corner is assigned.

3

Compute \(\chi=V-E+F\), then compare with the orientable and nonorientable catalogs.

This board-work slide is meant to be done live, not solved in advance. Choose a simple boundary word, count the identified vertices, paired edges, and polygon face, then compute Euler characteristic. The value is not the arithmetic alone; students see how a gluing instruction turns into a topological invariant.
Conceptual Guardrails

Common Misreadings

Drawing \(\ne\) Intrinsic Geometry

A flat polygon picture can encode a curved or multiply connected surface after identifications.

Quotient \(\ne\) Manifold

The group action must be geometrically well behaved before the quotient inherits a surface geometry.

Local \(\ne\) Global

Curvature is measured pointwise; Euler characteristic is global. Gauss-Bonnet integrates one into the other.

Teaching move: ask students which warning applies whenever they explain a picture, a gluing word, or a quotient domain.

Use these warnings to separate model drawings from intrinsic claims. A flat-looking polygon can encode a curved or multiply connected surface after gluing. A quotient by any group is not automatically a manifold. Finally, curvature sign is local, while Euler characteristic is global; Gauss-Bonnet links them through an integral, not by making them identical.
