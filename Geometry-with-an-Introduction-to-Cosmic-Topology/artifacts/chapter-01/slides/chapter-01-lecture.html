Three Layers Keep The Pictures Honest

ModelThe coordinate stage: plane, sphere, disk, polygon, or quotient window.
TransformationsThe allowed motions or identifications that declare two descriptions equivalent.
MeasurementsThe distances, angles, areas, or incidence relations that inhabitants can check.

Working Rule

A drawing is not yet a geometry. A geometry begins when a model is paired with transformations and invariants.

$$\text{geometry} \approx (\text{space},\ \text{allowed transformations},\ \text{invariants})$$
Translation guideModel, transformation, measurement
Use the three-layer diagram as the organizing contract for the entire course. A model supplies coordinates, a transformation rule says when two descriptions count as the same, and a measurement rule says what inhabitants can test. This will prevent later confusion when a Euclidean-looking picture is carrying a non-Euclidean metric or a quotient identification.

A Finite World Without A Wall

Opposite edges are paired locations, not barriers.

Definition: Flat Torus Window

Start with a rectangle. Identify points whose coordinates differ by a whole width or height.

$$ (x,y) \sim (x+mW,\ y+nH),\quad m,n\in\mathbb{Z} $$

Warning

The square's border is a coordinate artifact. The quotient surface has no edge for an inhabitant to hit.

Finite boundaryless geometryQuotient before embedding
Begin with the flat torus because it is the cleanest example of finite without boundary. The square is only a coordinate window; crossing the right edge returns through the left edge, and crossing the top returns through the bottom. Ask students to separate the drawing's boundary from the actual surface's absence of boundary.

The Tiled Cover Turns Topology Into Computation

Nearest-Image Lab

Lift the quotient to a grid of repeated tiles. The distance in the quotient is the shortest distance to any translated copy.

$$d_T(p,q)=\min_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|p-q+(mW,nH)\|$$
  • Local observers see ordinary flat geometry.
  • Long journeys can return through an identified edge.
  • Repeated images are a topology signal, not a new object.
Applied labRepeated images as evidence
Treat the tiled cover as the computational version of edge identification. Instead of stopping at a wall, copy the object into neighboring tiles and choose the nearest image to the observer. This is the lab seed for cosmic topology: repeated images can be evidence for a closed space even when local measurements look perfectly flat.

Inspection Target

Measure angles along intrinsic geodesics. Do not infer curvature from how the picture bends in the surrounding room.

Angle sum as a measurementFrom local triangle to global model
The same local triangle question separates three geometries. Euclidean triangles have angle sum pi, spherical great-circle triangles have excess, and hyperbolic triangles have defect. Emphasize that the detector is intrinsic: students should imagine measuring angles inside the world, not judging the drawing from an outside embedding.

The Parallel Postulate Becomes A Sorting Test

Flat

Exactly one parallel line through the off-line point.

Hyperbolic

Many nonintersecting geodesics through the point.

Elliptic

No parallel geodesic; great circles meet.

Euclidean, hyperbolic, ellipticOne axiom, three worlds
The parallel postulate slide turns a famous axiom into an observable branching point. Through a point off a line, flat geometry admits one parallel, hyperbolic geometry admits many, and elliptic geometry admits none. The visual is not a proof; it is a sorting test that previews how Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 will diverge.

Sector Defect Makes Curvature Tactile

Changing the sector angle changes small-circle circumference.

Positive Defect

Remove a wedge and glue. The neighborhood has less angle than the flat \(2\pi\) benchmark.

Negative Defect

Insert extra sector. The neighborhood has more angle than \(2\pi\), producing saddle-like spreading.

$$\delta = 2\pi-\theta_{\mathrm{around}}$$
Curvature by local circumferenceDefect and excess
Sector defects give a hands-on way to see curvature before formal curvature tensors appear. Removing a wedge and gluing the cut edges creates positive cone defect, while inserting extra sector creates saddle-like negative defect. Connect this to circumference: small circles around the vertex are shorter or longer than the flat benchmark.

Gluing Rules Build Surfaces

Edge labels encode topology; corner totals test local geometry.

Two Questions

  • Which boundary points are declared to be the same?
  • What total angle gathers at each glued vertex?

Homogeneity Check

If every point has the same local type, the surface can behave like a uniform geometry rather than a patchwork.

Global topology from local instructionsLabels, corners, quotient
Polygon gluing is the bridge from local construction to global surface. The edge labels tell us which boundary pieces are to be identified, while the corner total tells us whether the glued surface can look homogeneous near every vertex. This is a good place to ask what information is topological and what information is metric.

Local Measurement Can Miss Global Shape

Local Data

  • Small triangles may look flat.
  • Short rulers may report Euclidean distances.
  • Neighborhoods may have no visible boundary.

Global Data

  • A long path can return to its start.
  • Several images may represent one object.
  • The whole universe may have finite volume.
Cosmic topology previewLocal evidence versus global closure
Use the local versus global contrast to keep the cosmic theme visible in Chapter 1. A tiny observer may measure ordinary Euclidean distances and angle sums, yet the whole world can be finite because of identifications. The goal is not to solve cosmology now, but to build the geometric language that makes the question precise.

Klein's Filter: Keep The Invariants

Allowed transformations decide which features count as geometric.

Invariant Test

A property is geometric for a chosen setting when every allowed transformation preserves it.

$$I(g\cdot x)=I(x)\quad \text{for every allowed }g$$

Distance, angle, incidence, orientation, and topology become different invariants under different transformation groups.

Erlangen viewpointGeometry as invariant language
Klein's viewpoint is presented here as a practical filter. Once a group of transformations is declared allowable, only properties unchanged by those transformations are geometric properties in that setting. This gives students a portable test: before arguing about a feature, ask whether the permitted motions preserve it.

Three Early Misreadings To Prevent

Embedding Trap

The usual donut picture in three-dimensional space is not the same metric as the flat quotient torus.

Geodesic Trap

A curve drawn on a surface is not automatically a shortest local path for that surface's metric.

Boundary Trap

An edge in a polygon diagram may mark an identification instruction rather than a physical wall.

Common mistakesCoordinate picture versus intrinsic geometry
Name the pitfalls explicitly because the earliest pictures can be misleading. An embedded donut is not automatically the flat quotient torus, a curve drawn on a surface is not automatically a geodesic, and a boundary in the coordinate picture may be an identification rather than an edge. These distinctions are protective habits for the rest of the course.
