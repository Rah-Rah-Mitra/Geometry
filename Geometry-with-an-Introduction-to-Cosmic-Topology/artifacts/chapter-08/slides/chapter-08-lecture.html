Chapter 8

Cosmic Topology As A Detection Problem

Finite global shape can repeat astronomical information without changing the local curvature seen by an observer.

Geometry with an Introduction to Cosmic Topology - printed pages 195-220; PDF pages 205-230.
3-manifold models quotients catalog repeats matching circles
Local geometry sets geodesic behavior; topology asks whether space closes up globally.
Open by separating two questions students often fuse together. Curvature is local: small neighborhoods may look Euclidean, spherical, or hyperbolic. Topology is global: the same local geometry can be wrapped by a group of isometries so that light has multiple routes to us. The lecture treats the chapter as a detection problem, not as a catalog of exotic spaces.

The Question: Can The Universe Leave Repeated Clues?

Operational Form

A candidate universe is modeled as \(M = X/\Gamma\), where \(X\) is a simply connected 3-geometry and \(\Gamma\) is a discrete group of fixed-point-free isometries.

  • Local geometry: the metric behavior of small neighborhoods.
  • Global topology: the identification pattern invisible to a short ruler.
  • Observable signal: repeated sources, repeated separations, or paired CMB circles.
Use this slide to set the notation that will recur throughout the lecture. The universal cover is a computational convenience: it lets us draw repeated copies of sources as if space were unwrapped. The quotient is the physical space. Emphasize that repeated images are not extra galaxies; they are the same object reached along different lifted paths.

Pause on the table and ask what information each row supplies. This distinction prepares them for the rest of the chapter: observational evidence about curvature is not automatically evidence about simple connectivity.

A Flat Torus Is The Cleanest Laboratory

Face Pairing Model

Start with a rectangular box in \(E^3\). Identify opposite faces by translations. A path leaving one face re-enters through the paired face with the same direction.

\(T^3 = \mathbb{R}^3 / (L_x\mathbb{Z} \oplus L_y\mathbb{Z} \oplus L_z\mathbb{Z})\)
  • No boundary is introduced by the cube drawing.
  • The translation lengths become candidate repeated-separation scales.
  • The injectivity radius is half the shortest nonzero deck translation.
Opposite faces are exits and entrances, not physical walls.
This is the slide where the class should abandon the idea of a box with walls. The cube is only a fundamental domain. When a geodesic crosses a face, the quotient rule glues it to the opposite face. That simple flat example is powerful because the deck transformations are translations, so the predicted repeated distances can be computed directly.

Face Pairings Need Not Be Pure Translations

Twisted face pairings encode more structured quotients than a rectangular 3-torus.
Interpretation

A polyhedron may be a coordinate patch for a closed universe. Opposite faces can be paired after a rotation, so the gluing rule carries both displacement and orientation data.

  1. Choose a fundamental cell.
  2. Specify which faces are paired.
  3. Attach an isometry to each pairing.
  4. Check that neighborhoods close up without singular edges.
Use the dodecahedral picture to broaden the students' intuition without overloading them with classification. The important point is that a face-pairing rule is algebraic data, not merely a visual trick. The same style of thinking from the torus persists, but now the isometries may rotate as well as translate, and consistency around edges matters.

Repeated Images Are Orbits Seen Through A Light Cone

Orbit Principle

If \(p\in X\) represents a source, then the lifted copies are \(\{\gamma p:\gamma\in\Gamma\}\). Observations sample a finite part of this orbit.

Pitfall

No detected repeat is not proof of trivial topology. The topology scale may exceed the observable radius, or the catalog may be too sparse or noisy.

Detection is statistical because the observer receives incomplete, time-delayed, astrophysically filtered information.

The orbit language makes repeated images precise. In the universal cover, the same physical source has many mathematical representatives. An observer can only sample representatives whose light lies within the relevant horizon and whose astrophysical properties can be recognized. This slide is also a good moment to warn against treating nondetection as a theorem about the universe.

Cosmic Crystallography: Distances As A Fingerprint

A flat torus toy catalog produces pair-separation spikes at translation-related lengths.
Pair-Separation Histogram

For catalog positions \(x_i\), count pair distances \(d(x_i,x_j)\). A multiply connected flat model can create excess counts near deck-translation lengths.

spike candidates \( \approx \|n_xL_x, n_yL_y, n_zL_z\| \)
  • Strongest in clean simulated catalogs.
  • Weakened by evolution, selection effects, and uncertain source matching.
  • Useful as an intuition pump for global repeats.
Explain that the histogram is not magic; it is a counting device for distances between catalog entries. In a torus, translations create many pairs separated by the same preferred lengths, so spikes can appear. Then temper the claim. Real catalogs have noise, source evolution, survey boundaries, and ambiguous identification, so the method is best introduced as a diagnostic idea rather than a decisive instrument.

Matching Circles: When The Last-Scattering Surface Meets Itself

Geometric Trigger

In the cover, two copies of the observer have last-scattering spheres. If the sphere radius exceeds half their separation, the two spheres intersect in a circle.

circle signal appears when \(R_{\mathrm{LSS}} > \frac{1}{2}d(O,\gamma O)\)

The temperature pattern along paired circles should agree after the correct orientation and phase shift are applied.

The observable sky can contain paired circular traces of the same physical surface.
Move slowly through the geometry because this is often the chapter's most memorable test. Draw two observer copies in the cover, each with a last-scattering sphere. Their intersection is a circle, and in the quotient that circle can appear twice on one sky. The signal is not just a circle shape; it is a matched pattern around the circle after the gluing transformation is accounted for.

Density Parameters Inform Curvature, Not Topology Alone

Near-flat curvature evidence narrows geometry while leaving multiple topologies possible.
Friedmann Check

The total density parameter is compared with one: \(\Omega_0=1\) suggests flat spatial geometry, while deviations suggest positive or negative curvature.

EvidenceWhat it saysWhat it misses
\(\Omega_0\)Curvature scaleGlobal identifications
Catalog repeatsCandidate deck lengthsFull metric model
CMB circlesRepeated LSS tracesAll larger topologies
This slide protects students from overinterpreting density measurements. If observations indicate near-flatness, that constrains the local curvature radius and makes some geometries more plausible, but it does not prove that space is infinite or simply connected. Topology is a global quotient question, so the lecture keeps density evidence in conversation with repeated-image and matching-circle tests.

An Instructor-Ready Detection Pipeline

1. Model

Choose \(X\), a metric, and a candidate group \(\Gamma\). Record the shortest deck motions and fundamental-domain scale.

2. Simulate

Lift sources to the cover, apply selection limits, and compute predicted repeated images, separations, or sky-circle pairs.

3. Compare

Match predictions against catalogs and CMB data while tracking noise, thresholds, and alternative explanations.

Use this as the practical method slide. Students should see that cosmic topology is not a single formula but a loop between model building and evidence. A candidate quotient produces predicted observables, the data can support or weaken those predictions, and a negative result may only exclude a scale, an orientation, or a specific group action rather than every nontrivial topology.

Worked Mini Lab: Move The Torus Lengths

Toy Experiment

Generate a source catalog in a rectangular 3-torus. Vary \(L_x,L_y,L_z\), lift nearby copies, and watch the pair-separation spikes move.

  1. Fix a catalog radius and density.
  2. Choose fundamental lengths.
  3. Compute pair distances in the cover.
  4. Compare the spike locations with the translation norms.
Frame this as a live teaching activity even if the class only sees the static deck. The notebook's parameter lab lets students perturb the fundamental lengths and ask which spike should move first. The important learning target is causal: a deck translation length changes a predicted distance scale, so the histogram should respond in a predictable direction.

Common Pitfalls To Name Explicitly

Pitfall 1

A visible edge in a fundamental domain is not necessarily a physical boundary.

Pitfall 2

Near-flat curvature evidence does not decide whether the universe is infinite.

Pitfall 3

A missing spike or circle excludes at most the tested model, scale, and data threshold.

Checkpoint Prompt

Choose one proposed topology. State its cover \(X\), its group action \(\Gamma\), one predicted observable, and one reason a real data set could fail to show that observable.

This slide is a useful place to slow down before the recap. Each pitfall corresponds to a conceptual boundary in the chapter: drawing versus physical space, curvature versus topology, and nondetection versus impossibility. The checkpoint prompt forces students to keep the model layer, transformation layer, and measurement layer separate in a concise explanation.

Recap: What Cosmic Topology Adds To Geometry

  • A universe model can be written as a quotient \(X/\Gamma\).
  • The cover makes repeated images computable as orbits.
  • Pair-separation histograms search for repeated distances.
  • Matching circles search for repeated last-scattering information.
  • Density evidence constrains curvature while topology remains global.
Final Principle

Cosmic topology asks whether global identifications leave local observations that are repeated, correlated, and geometrically consistent.

A successful detection would connect quotient geometry to repeated information on the sky.
End by returning to the course theme: geometry is not only a list of spaces, but a way to connect transformations, invariants, and measurements. The chapter's tests are attractive because they translate an abstract quotient into observable repetition. The honest conclusion is balanced: topology may be detectable, but every proposed detection must survive geometric consistency checks and observational uncertainty.
