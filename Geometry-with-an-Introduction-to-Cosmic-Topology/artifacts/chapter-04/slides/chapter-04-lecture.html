The Erlangen Filter

Guiding Principle

Start with model space S and transformation group G. A measurement belongs to the geometry only when every G-move leaves it unchanged.

geometry = (space, allowed transformations)

The filter makes visual sameness testable: apply legal moves and inspect what stays stable.

Use this slide to contrast an additive view of geometry with Klein's subtractive view without quoting the source. The diagram should be read as a testing pipeline. A proposed feature, such as slope, distance, angle, or cross ratio, enters the filter. The group acts on the object. If the proposed measurement changes, it is coordinate decoration rather than geometry for this pair.

What Makes A Collection Of Transformations A Group?

Identity

The do-nothing transformation must be legal.

I(a)=a

Closure

Following one legal move by another is still legal.

T,S in G implies T circ S in G

Inverses

Every legal move can be undone by a legal move.

T in G implies T^{-1} in G

The three group properties are not abstract bookkeeping; they are what make congruence a reliable comparison. Identity says every object is comparable to itself. Closure says multi-step motion is still an allowed motion. Inverses say the comparison is reversible. Associativity does not need a separate classroom proof here because composition of functions is already associative.

First Test Case: Translation Geometry

Group Check
  1. T_0(z)=z is the identity.
  2. T_b(T_c(z))=z+c+b=T_{b+c}(z), so translations are closed.
  3. T_b^{-1}=T_{-b}, so every translation can be reversed.
Restriction

Translations can move a figure anywhere, but they cannot rotate it or resize it. Congruence depends on the group, not on visual resemblance alone.

This is the first place to slow down and model the proof habit. Translation geometry is deliberately simple, but it demonstrates the whole chapter. The proof of closure is a computation with parameters. The warning matters because students may instinctively import Euclidean congruence; in translation geometry, a rotated copy is not congruent even if it feels familiar.

A Geometry Is A Pair (S,G)

Definition

A geometry consists of a set S of points and a group G of transformations of that set. A figure is a subset of S.

A cong B iff there exists T in G with T(A)=B.

The notation can feel small, but it changes the meaning of almost every word students already know. A point may be treated as a one-point figure, and a line or circle is a subset of the model space. Congruence is not visual overlay by any imagined motion; it is existence of a transformation in the chosen group.

Orbits Make Congruence Visible

Finite Rotation Geometry

Let H={R_0,R_{pi/2},R_pi,R_{3pi/2}}. For any point z neq 0, its congruence class is the four-point orbit

{z, iz, -z, -iz}.

The origin has a one-point orbit because every rotation fixes it.

This slide should make orbits feel concrete before the theorem on minimal invariant sets appears. Ask students to point to the orbit of a marked point and then ask why the center behaves differently. The key observation is that the group can be small, so congruence classes can be very sparse even in a continuous space.

Fixed Points And Mirror Pairs

Two-Element Group

For reflection across the real axis, G={1,r} with r(z)=bar z.

  • Points on the mirror line are fixed.
  • Off-axis points travel in two-point orbits.
  • A figure above the axis is congruent to its reflected partner, not to an arbitrary translate.
Classroom Diagnostic

When a group has fixed points, the geometry is usually not homogeneous: some points have different orbit behavior than others.

Reflection geometry is useful because it exposes stabilizers and fixed sets without requiring formal group-action language. The mirror line is not just a line in the picture; it is a set of points with one-point orbits. Off the line, points pair with their reflected images. This prepares the later distinction between homogeneity and isotropy.

Invariant Sets Are Closed Under The Group Action

Definition

A collection D of figures is invariant when A in D implies T(A) in D for every T in G.

An invariant function gives the same value before and after every allowed move.

Example

In translation geometry, all lines form an invariant set, and slope is an invariant function. But the set of all lines is too large to describe one congruence class.

Make the distinction between invariant and minimally invariant explicit. An invariant set is closed under every legal motion, so it can be a union of several congruence classes. The example of all lines in translation geometry is perfect: translation sends lines to lines and preserves slope, but lines with different slopes cannot be translated onto one another.

Minimal Invariance Means One Orbit

Theorem

An invariant set D of figures is minimally invariant exactly when any two figures in D are congruent.

D minimal invariant iff D={T(A) : T in G}
Proof Roadmap
  1. Pick a figure A in D.
  2. Collect all its images under G.
  3. Closure makes this orbit invariant.
  4. Minimality forces the orbit to be all of D.

The theorem turns an orbit into the smallest invariant collection.

This theorem is the conceptual center of the first section. Present the proof as a construction rather than as a formal maze. Starting from one figure, the orbit is automatically contained in any invariant set that contains the figure. Because the group is closed under composition, the orbit is itself invariant. Minimality leaves no room for a smaller invariant subset.

Euclidean Geometry: Distances And Angles Survive

Euclidean Group

The transformations are T(z)=e^{i theta}z+b. They include rotations and translations, but not dilations.

|T(z_1)-T(z_2)|=|z_1-z_2|

Angles are preserved because multiplying by e^{i theta} rotates every direction by the same amount.

Euclidean geometry should now appear as a special case of the same framework rather than as the default meaning of geometry. Walk through the distance computation carefully: the translation cancels in the difference, and the unit complex multiplier has modulus one. This is a concise example of the Erlangen filter accepting a measurement.

Homogeneous Is Not The Same As Isotropic

Two Symmetry Tests
  • Homogeneous: any point can be moved to any other point.
  • Isotropic: rotations about each point are available.
  • Translations make the plane homogeneous but do not supply rotations.
  • Euclidean motions supply both tests.
Students often compress all symmetry into one intuition, so this slide separates two different freedoms. Homogeneity is freedom of position; isotropy is freedom of direction at a chosen position. Translation geometry passes the first test by using the vector from p to q, but it fails the second because it has no rotations about a point.

A Metric Must Also Be An Invariant Function

Metric Conditions
  1. d(x,y) ge 0, with equality exactly when x=y.
  2. d(x,y)=d(y,x).
  3. d(x,z) le d(x,y)+d(y,z).
  4. For this geometry, d(Tx,Ty)=d(x,y) for every T in G.
Euclidean Normalization Trick

Because distance is invariant under Euclidean motions, a triangle-inequality proof may translate one point to 0 and rotate another onto the positive real axis before doing algebra.

The chapter's metric definition adds an important geometry-specific requirement to the usual metric axioms: the distance must be invariant under the chosen group. The Euclidean triangle inequality proof uses symmetry as a simplifier. By translating and rotating the configuration, one reduces the algebra without changing the distances being compared.

Mobius Geometry Reuses The Same Contract

Definition

Mobius geometry is (C^+,M), where M is the group of all Mobius transformations.

  • Clines form a minimally invariant set.
  • Cross ratio is invariant.
  • Angle measure is invariant.
  • Three point images determine a transformation.
This slide connects Chapter 4 back to the transformation work from Chapter 3. The point is not to reprove every Mobius fact, but to recast them in the new language. The group property licenses Mobius geometry. The preserved objects and functions, especially clines, cross ratio, and angles, tell us what this geometry is allowed to measure.

Euclidean Distance Fails The Mobius Filter

Counterexample

For T(z)=1/z, the real points 2 and 3 are one Euclidean unit apart, but their images 1/2 and 1/3 are only 1/6 apart.

|2-3|=1,qquad |1/2-1/3|=1/6

Therefore Euclidean distance is not a Mobius invariant.

The inversion example is the cleanest way to make the Erlangen filter fail in public. Nothing subtle is needed: choose two finite real points, apply a legal Mobius transformation, and compare ordinary Euclidean distance before and after. Then immediately contrast this failure with angle preservation, which remains central for the non-Euclidean geometries that follow.

Why This Chapter Sits Before Hyperbolic And Elliptic Geometry

1. Declare

Choose a space and a transformation group.

2. Compute

Find orbits, fixed points, and images of representative figures.

3. Filter

Keep only functions and relations preserved by the group.

4. Interpret

Read congruence, distance, and angle inside the chosen geometry.

The rest of the course specializes the group, then studies which measurements and identifications remain meaningful.

Close by positioning the chapter as a hinge. The class now has a reusable workflow for the coming non-Euclidean chapters: decide the model, decide the legal transformations, identify invariants, and then interpret geometric claims through those invariants. Cosmic topology will later add global identification patterns, but this chapter supplies the symmetry language needed to keep local geometry distinct from global shape.

Recap: The Geometry Workflow

Takeaway

A geometric claim is a claim that survives the transformations named by the geometry.

Congruence

Find a transformation in G, or find an invariant that blocks its existence.

Minimal Invariant Set

Build the orbit of one figure. If every member is congruent to every other, the set is minimal.

Common Mistake

Do not carry Euclidean distance, rotation freedom, or visual similarity into a geometry unless the group preserves it.

Board Prompt

Choose one figure from the notebook artifacts. Name the model space, the transformation group, the orbit, and one proposed invariant that passes or fails.

End with an active recap rather than a passive list. The prompt asks students to transfer the workflow to any artifact from the notebook, which is exactly how the chapter should be assessed. They should be able to name the space, state the group, describe an orbit or invariant set, and explain why a proposed measurement is or is not legitimate.
