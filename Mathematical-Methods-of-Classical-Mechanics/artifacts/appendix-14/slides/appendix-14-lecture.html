Dynamical Systems

Vector fields, phase flow, and the qualitative theory of motion

The object

A smooth vector field on a manifold and the flow it generates.

The question

Not exact solutions, but the long-term shape of every orbit.

The frame

Mechanics is one privileged, symplectic corner of this theory.

Vector Fields and the Phase Flow

Field and flow

$$\dot x = v(x), \qquad x(t) = g^t x_0$$

Group law

$g^{t+s} = g^t \circ g^s$ and $g^0 = \mathrm{id}$: a one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms.

No crossings

Existence and uniqueness force distinct orbits to stay disjoint.

One-parameter groupsAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Define the phase flow as the one-parameter group obtained by integrating the field. Emphasize the group laws and existence-uniqueness as the reason orbits never cross. Point at the streamlines: each curve is one solution, and the field is tangent to it everywhere along the motion.

Fixed Points and Linearization

Equilibrium

$$v(x_*) = 0, \qquad \dot\xi = A\xi,\; A = Dv(x_*)$$

Read the Jacobian

The linear part $A$ at the rest point governs all nearby motion.

Hartman–Grobman

If no eigenvalue is purely imaginary, the flow is locally conjugate to its linearization.

Local theoryAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Explain that equilibria are zeros of the field and that the Jacobian governs nearby motion. Introduce the Hartman-Grobman idea informally: away from eigenvalues on the imaginary axis, the nonlinear flow looks like its linear part. This justifies reading eigenvalues to classify behaviour.

Classifying Equilibria by Eigenvalues

Node

Real eigenvalues, same sign: all curves enter (stable) or leave (unstable).

Saddle

Real, opposite signs: one direction in, one out.

Focus

Complex $\lambda = a \pm bi$, $a\neq 0$: spiralling sink or source.

Center

Pure imaginary $\lambda = \pm bi$: closed loops, neutrally stable.

Spectrum and typeAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Walk the eigenvalue plane: real same-sign gives nodes, real opposite-sign gives saddles, complex with nonzero real part gives foci, pure imaginary gives centers. Connect each case to a sketch and stress that sign of the real part decides attraction versus repulsion, the master stability question.

Phase Portrait Near a Saddle

Stable set $W^s$

Points whose orbits tend to $x_*$ as $t\to+\infty$ — tangent to the negative-eigenvalue direction.

Unstable set $W^u$

Points whose orbits tend to $x_*$ as $t\to-\infty$ — the escape directions.

Compare: focus

a focus spirals; a saddle does not.

Saddle geometryAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
This is the visual centerpiece. Trace the stable manifold as the set flowing into the saddle, the unstable manifold as the set flowing out, and the hyperbolic curves squeezing between them. Contrast with the spiralling focus inset so students feel the difference between saddle and focus geometry.

Stable and Unstable Manifolds

Definitions

$$W^s = \{x: g^t x \to x_*,\, t\to+\infty\}$$ $$W^u = \{x: g^t x \to x_*,\, t\to-\infty\}$$

Stable manifold theorem

$W^s, W^u$ are smooth invariant manifolds tangent at $x_*$ to the stable and unstable eigenspaces of $A$.

Why it matters

Their global tangling — homoclinic intersections — is a primary source of chaos.

Invariant manifoldsAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Promote the eigendirections to invariant manifolds tangent to the linear eigenspaces. Note that they are global objects, often curved, and that their intersections create the homoclinic tangles behind chaos. Mention the stable manifold theorem as the rigorous backbone of the picture just drawn.

Periodic Orbits and Limit Cycles

Periodicity

$$g^{T} x_0 = x_0, \quad T>0$$

Closed orbit

A loop in phase space; in a center, infinitely many nested loops.

Limit cycle

An isolated closed orbit that attracts or repels its neighbours.

Nonlinear only

Van der Pol's oscillator owns one; linear systems never do.

Periodic motionAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Distinguish a closed orbit from a limit cycle, which is an isolated closed orbit attracting or repelling its neighbours. Give the van der Pol oscillator as the canonical example and emphasize that limit cycles are a genuinely nonlinear phenomenon absent from linear systems.

The Poincaré Section and Return Map

First-return map

$$P(x) = g^{\tau(x)} x \in \Sigma$$

Periodic orbit ↔ fixed point

A closed orbit meets $\Sigma$ in a fixed point $P(x_*)=x_*$.

Dimension drop

An $n$-flow becomes an $(n{-}1)$-map; stability is read from $DP$.

Reducing flows to mapsAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
This is the second key visual. Introduce a transverse section and the first-return map sending a point to where its orbit next pierces the section. Explain that a periodic orbit becomes a fixed point of this map, reducing flow analysis by one dimension and converting stability into a derivative.

Structural Stability and Bifurcations

Structurally stable

The phase portrait is unchanged (up to homeomorphism) under all small perturbations of $v$.

Bifurcation

A parameter value where the portrait changes qualitatively — stability is lost or orbits are born.

Prototypes

Saddle-node: equilibria collide and vanish. Hopf: a focus spawns a limit cycle.

Robustness and changeAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Define structural stability as robustness of the phase portrait under small perturbations of the field. Then bifurcations are the breakdown points where portraits change qualitatively. Sketch the saddle-node and Hopf as the prototypes, and connect Hopf to the birth of a limit cycle from a focus.

Invariant Sets and Attractors

Invariant set

$$g^t S = S \quad \text{for all } t$$

Attractor

An invariant set that captures an open neighbourhood of initial conditions.

The zoo

Fixed points, limit cycles, invariant tori, and strange attractors.

Asymptotic fate

Attractors organize where almost every trajectory ends up.

Long-term behaviourAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Build the hierarchy of long-term objects: invariant sets, then attractors as the sets that capture open neighbourhoods of initial data. Mention fixed points, limit cycles, tori, and strange attractors as members. Stress that attractors organize the asymptotic fate of almost every trajectory.

Integrable versus Chaotic Dynamics

Regular (integrable)

Motion winds on invariant tori; action–angle variables linearize the flow: $\dot\theta = \omega$.

Chaotic

Sensitive dependence: nearby orbits separate as $e^{\lambda t}$ with Lyapunov exponent $\lambda>0$.

The tension

Most real systems sit between, with mixed regular and chaotic regions.

Order and chaosAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Contrast regular motion on invariant tori, where action-angle variables linearize the flow, with chaotic motion showing sensitive dependence and positive Lyapunov exponents. Frame this as the central tension of the theory and the reason mechanics needs both integrable models and chaos.

Mechanics as a Special Class of Flows

Hamiltonian flow

$$\dot q = \partial H/\partial p, \qquad \dot p = -\partial H/\partial q$$

Symplectic constraint

The flow preserves phase volume — Liouville — so true attractors cannot exist.

Centers, not foci

Energy conservation replaces spiralling sinks with neutral closed orbits.

The takeaway

Linearization, sections, manifolds, and bifurcations stay the working lens.

Hamiltonian cornerAppendix 14: Dynamical Systems
Close by placing Hamiltonian flows inside the general theory. Highlight the symplectic constraints: volume preservation forbids attractors, centers replace foci, and energy levels foliate phase space. Recap the toolkit, linearization, sections, manifolds, and bifurcations, as the lens for the rest of the book.
