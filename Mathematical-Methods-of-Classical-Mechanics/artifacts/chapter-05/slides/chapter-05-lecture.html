Oscillations

Small oscillations of a Lagrangian system about a stable equilibrium

The claim

Near a stable equilibrium, every conservative system is a sum of independent harmonic oscillators.

The method

Linearize, diagonalize two quadratic forms, read off normal modes.

The payoff

Characteristic frequencies $\omega_i$, superposition, and parametric resonance.

OscillationsSmall oscillations
Chapter five takes a Lagrangian system near a stable equilibrium and shows that, to leading order, every such system is a bundle of independent harmonic oscillators. We motivate why linearization is the right first move and preview the three pillars: quadratic energy forms, simultaneous diagonalization, and normal modes oscillating at characteristic frequencies.

Equilibrium and Linearization

Equilibrium

A point $q^*$ with $\partial U/\partial q=0$: the forces balance.

Stability

Lagrange-Dirichlet: a strict local minimum of $U$ is stable.

Expand

Taylor $U$ to second order; the Hessian survives.

Second-order expansion

$U(q^*+x)=U(q^*)+\tfrac12\,x^\top B\,x+O(|x|^3)$

Stiffness matrix

$B_{ij}=\dfrac{\partial^2 U}{\partial q_i\,\partial q_j}\Big|_{q^*}$

OscillationsLinearization
An equilibrium is a critical point of the potential; stability means it is a strict local minimum. We expand the potential to second order, drop the constant and the vanishing linear term, and keep the Hessian. The kinetic energy is already quadratic in velocities, evaluated at the equilibrium configuration. That pairing of two quadratic forms is the whole subject.

Two Quadratic Forms

Kinetic energy

$T=\tfrac12\,\dot q^\top A\,\dot q,\qquad A=A^\top\succ 0$

Potential energy

$U=\tfrac12\,q^\top B\,q,\qquad B=B^\top$

Lagrangian

$L=\tfrac12\dot q^\top A\dot q-\tfrac12 q^\top B q$.

$A$ is a metric

Mass matrix: symmetric, positive definite, defines lengths in velocity space.

$B$ encodes stability

The equilibrium is stable exactly when $B\succ 0$ as well.

The whole game

Study the pair $(A,B)$ with $A$ positive definite.

OscillationsQuadratic forms
After linearization the Lagrangian is one half q-dot transpose A q-dot minus one half q transpose B q. Matrix A, the mass or metric matrix, is symmetric positive definite. Matrix B, the stiffness matrix, is the potential Hessian and is positive definite exactly when the equilibrium is stable. Everything else is linear algebra applied to this pair.

Linearized Equations of Motion

Euler-Lagrange

$A\,\ddot q+B\,q=0$

Linear, constant-coefficient

A vector ODE with no $q$-dependence in $A,B$: superposition applies.

Mode ansatz

$q(t)=v\,\sin(\omega t+\varphi)$

Calculus → algebra

Substituting turns the ODE into an algebraic condition on $v$ and $\omega$.

OscillationsMode ansatz
The Euler-Lagrange equations become A q double-dot plus B q equals zero, a linear system with constant coefficients. We seek solutions of the form a vector times a sine of omega t. Substituting turns the differential equation into an algebraic eigenvalue condition, replacing calculus with a determinant. This is the bridge from dynamics to spectral linear algebra.

The Generalized Eigenvalue Problem

Eigenproblem with metric $A$

$B\,v=\omega^2\,A\,v$

Characteristic equation

$\det\!\big(B-\omega^2 A\big)=0$

Reality

$A\succ0,\ B=B^\top$ force every $\omega^2$ real.

Stability

$B\succ0$ makes each $\omega^2>0$, so all $\omega_i$ are real frequencies.

Instability

A negative $\omega^2$ gives a growing exponential: the mode runs away.

OscillationsEigenfrequencies
The ansatz produces B v equals omega squared A v, a generalized eigenvalue problem with metric A. Because A is positive definite and B symmetric, all eigenvalues omega squared are real, and stability forces them positive, giving real frequencies. The characteristic polynomial det of B minus omega squared A equals zero supplies the spectrum of frequencies.

Simultaneous Diagonalization

Theorem

Two quadratic forms, one positive definite, are simultaneously diagonalizable by a single linear change of coordinates.

Reduced forms

$C^\top A\,C=I,\qquad C^\top B\,C=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1^2,\dots,\omega_n^2)$

A-orthogonality

Eigenvectors satisfy $v_i^\top A\,v_j=\delta_{ij}$.

OscillationsDiagonalization
The central theorem: two quadratic forms, one positive definite, can be simultaneously reduced. A linear change of coordinates makes A the identity and B diagonal at once. Geometrically we first sphere the metric, then rotate to the principal axes of the potential. The eigenvectors are A-orthogonal, which is exactly the orthogonality the modes inherit.

Normal Coordinates and Modes

Decoupled Lagrangian

$L=\tfrac12\sum_i\big(\dot Q_i^2-\omega_i^2 Q_i^2\big)$

Each coordinate rings alone

$\ddot Q_i+\omega_i^2 Q_i=0$

Normal coordinate

$Q_i$ is the amplitude along eigenvector $v_i$; the modes are uncoupled.

Normal mode

Excite one $Q_i$ only: the system moves in a fixed shape at the single frequency $\omega_i$.

Basis

The $v_i$ form an $A$-orthonormal basis of configuration space.

OscillationsNormal modes
In the new coordinates the Lagrangian splits into a sum of uncoupled one-dimensional oscillators. Each normal coordinate Q i obeys Q i double-dot plus omega i squared Q i equals zero and rings at its own frequency. A normal mode is the motion in which only one normal coordinate is excited, so the whole system moves rigidly in a single shape at a single frequency.

Two Coupled Masses: Its Normal Modes

Symmetric: coupling relaxed

$v_1=(1,1),\quad \omega_1^2=\dfrac{k}{m}$

Antisymmetric: coupling stretched

$v_2=(1,-1),\quad \omega_2^2=\dfrac{k+2\kappa}{m}$

The gap is the coupling

$\omega_2>\omega_1$, and $\omega_2-\omega_1$ grows with the coupling $\kappa$.

OscillationsCoupled masses
The two-mass spring chain is the canonical example. Its symmetric mode moves both masses together, leaving the coupling spring relaxed, so it oscillates at the bare frequency. Its antisymmetric mode moves the masses oppositely, stretching the coupling maximally, so it sits higher. Drawing both shapes makes the abstract eigenvectors physically concrete and memorable.

The Characteristic Frequency Spectrum

One line per mode

The eigenvalues $\omega_i^2$ are the squared positions of the spectral lines.

Diagnosis without integration

The spectrum characterizes the vibrating structure directly from $(A,B)$.

Degeneracy

$\omega_i=\omega_j$ when symmetry forces a repeated eigenvalue.

OscillationsSpectrum
Collecting the roots of the characteristic equation gives the frequency spectrum, one line per mode. The two-mass example shows a low symmetric line and a higher antisymmetric line, with the gap set by the coupling strength. Reading a spectrum like this is how one diagnoses a vibrating structure without integrating any trajectory.

General Motion: Superposition

General solution

$q(t)=\sum_i c_i\,v_i\,\sin(\omega_i t+\varphi_i)$

Initial data

The $2n$ constants $c_i,\varphi_i$ are fixed by $q(0),\dot q(0)$.

Quasi-periodic

Generic motion never exactly repeats unless the $\omega_i$ are commensurable.

Beats

Two close frequencies produce a slow amplitude exchange between the masses.

Natural basis

Modes are the coordinates in which initial conditions decouple.

OscillationsSuperposition
Because the linearized system is linear, the general motion is an arbitrary superposition of normal modes, each with its own amplitude and phase fixed by initial conditions. Generic motion is therefore quasi-periodic, not periodic, unless the frequencies are commensurable. The normal modes form the natural basis in which initial data decompose cleanly.

Perturbation and Parametric Resonance

Mathieu / Hill equation

$\ddot x+\omega_0^2\big(1+\varepsilon\cos\nu t\big)x=0$

Vibrating support

Driving a parameter, not the coordinate, can destabilize the rest point.

Inverted pendulum

Fast vertical shaking can even stabilize the upside-down equilibrium.

OscillationsParametric resonance
When a parameter of the system is driven periodically, the equilibrium can lose stability even with no direct forcing. The pendulum with vibrating support obeys a Hill or Mathieu equation. Stability tongues in the parameter plane mark where small oscillations blow up, most violently when the drive frequency is near twice a natural frequency. A vibrating support can even stabilize the inverted pendulum.

Summary

Linearize

Two quadratic forms: $T=\tfrac12\dot q^\top A\dot q$ and $U=\tfrac12 q^\top Bq$.

Diagonalize

Solve $Bv=\omega^2 Av$; simultaneously reduce $A\to I,\ B\to\Lambda$.

Decouple

Normal coordinates ring independently at frequencies $\omega_i$.

Superpose

General motion is a quasi-periodic sum of normal modes.

One line to remember

$A\ddot q+Bq=0\ \Rightarrow\ \ddot Q_i+\omega_i^2 Q_i=0$

Beyond

Parametric driving opens resonance and stability tongues.

OscillationsRecap
We reduced any stable Lagrangian equilibrium to independent harmonic oscillators through three moves: linearize into two quadratic forms, solve the generalized eigenvalue problem, and read off normal modes and frequencies. Superposition handles general motion, and parametric driving opens the door to resonance and stability tongues. This linear core underlies perturbation theory throughout mechanics.
