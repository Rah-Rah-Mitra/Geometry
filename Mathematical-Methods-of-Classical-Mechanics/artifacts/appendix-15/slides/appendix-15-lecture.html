Normal Forms

Simplifying a vector field near an equilibrium, order by order

The aim

Change coordinates so the nonlinear tail of a field becomes as simple as resonance allows.

The tool

Near-identity polynomial maps solving a homological equation at each degree.

The verdict

Poincare, Poincare-Dulac, and Birkhoff theorems read off what survives.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsVector fields near equilibria
This appendix asks a simple question with a deep answer: how far can we simplify a vector field near an equilibrium by changing coordinates? We will peel nonlinear terms away order by order, discover that resonances among eigenvalues block some terms, and meet the Poincare and Birkhoff normal form theorems that organize the whole story.

The Problem Setup

Equilibrium at origin

Place the fixed point at $x=0$ and split the field into linear and nonlinear parts.

Eigenvalues

The spectrum $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ of the linear part controls everything that follows.

Field near equilibrium

$$\dot x = Ax + \sum_{r\ge 2} v_r(x)$$

$A$ linear, $v_r$ homogeneous of degree $r$.

Question

How much of $\sum v_r$ can a change of variables delete?

Appendix 15: Normal FormsLinear part plus nonlinear tail
We center the equilibrium at the origin and split the field into its linear part, governed by the matrix and its eigenvalues, plus higher order corrections. The goal is to make a near-identity polynomial change of variables that removes as much of the nonlinear tail as possible, leaving the simplest field equivalent to the original.

Near-Identity Transformations

The move

Substitute $x = y + h_r(y)$ with $h_r$ homogeneous of degree $r$.

Triangular effect

It changes terms of degree $r$ and above only; lower degrees stay cleaned.

Generator of degree r

$$x = y + h_r(y),\qquad h_r\in\mathcal H_r$$

Appendix 15: Normal FormsOne degree at a time
The transformations we allow are identity plus a homogeneous polynomial of some degree. Such a map perturbs only terms of that degree and higher, so it never disturbs lower orders already cleaned. This triangular property is what lets us normalize one degree at a time without ever undoing earlier work.

The Homological Equation

Linearize the demand

To kill $v_r$, choose $h_r$ so the bracket with the linear field cancels it.

Operator $L_A$

The map $h_r\mapsto [Ax,\,h_r]$ acts on the space $\mathcal H_r$ of degree-$r$ fields.

Homological equation

$$L_A h_r \equiv \tfrac{\partial h_r}{\partial y}Ay - A\,h_r = v_r$$

Solvable iff

$L_A$ is invertible on $\mathcal H_r$ — i.e. it has no zero eigenvalue.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsA linear equation at each order
Substituting a degree-r change of variables turns the demand to kill the degree-r terms into a linear equation involving the bracket of the linear field with the generator. This is the homological equation. Solvability hinges on whether the linear operator it defines is invertible on the space of degree-r vector monomials.

Resonances

Diagonal action

On the monomial $y^m e_j$ the operator $L_A$ multiplies by $(m,\lambda)-\lambda_j$.

Obstruction

When that eigenvalue is zero, the term is resonant and cannot be removed.

Resonance condition

$$(m,\lambda)=\lambda_j,\quad |m|=\textstyle\sum m_i\ge 2$$

Reading it

$m\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^n$ records the monomial; $e_j$ the component. Resonance = a hidden linear dependence in the spectrum.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsThe eigenvalues of $L_A$
On vector monomials the homological operator acts diagonally with eigenvalues given by the dot product of the exponent vector with the eigenvalues minus a single eigenvalue. When that combination vanishes the monomial is resonant and cannot be removed. The resonance condition is the exact obstruction to further simplification.

Marked points

Plotting the eigenvalues in the complex plane lets us read resonances geometrically. A resonant relation says one eigenvalue is an integer combination of the others with nonnegative coefficients summing to at least two. We mark which lattice combinations land back on an eigenvalue, since exactly those monomials survive normalization.

The Poincare Theorem

Nonresonant case

If $(m,\lambda)\ne\lambda_j$ for all $|m|\ge2$, every homological equation is solvable.

Consequence

All nonlinear terms can be removed: the field is formally linear.

Theorem (Poincare)

$$\dot x = Ax+\dots \;\;\xrightarrow{\;\hat h\;}\;\; \dot y = Ay$$

Formal equivalence to the linear part.

Convergence

In the Poincare domain (eigenvalues on one side of $i\mathbb R$) the series converges and the linearization is analytic.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsFormal linearization
If the eigenvalues are nonresonant, every homological equation is solvable, so all nonlinear terms can be removed formally. The Poincare theorem states that a nonresonant field is formally equivalent to its linear part. In the Poincare domain, where eigenvalues avoid the imaginary axis on one side, this linearization actually converges and is analytic.

The Poincare-Dulac Theorem

Resonant case

Remove everything except the resonant monomials in $\ker L_A$.

Resonant normal form

What remains commutes with the linear flow $e^{At}$.

Theorem (Poincare-Dulac)

$$\dot y = Ay + w(y),\quad w=\!\!\sum_{(m,\lambda)=\lambda_j}\!\! c_{m,j}\,y^m e_j$$

Meaning

The resonant skeleton $w$ is the irreducible nonlinearity — no coordinate change can erase it.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsReduction to resonant terms
When resonances are present we cannot fully linearize, but we can remove everything except resonant monomials. The Poincare-Dulac theorem reduces the field to a normal form containing only resonant terms. This resonant skeleton commutes with the linear flow and captures the genuinely nonlinear behavior that no coordinate change can erase.

Small Denominators

The danger

Even when $(m,\lambda)\ne\lambda_j$, the value can be tiny, so dividing by it blows the coefficients up.

Divergence

Accumulated small divisors can make the formal normalizing series diverge.

Diophantine condition

$$|(m,\lambda)-\lambda_j|\;\ge\;\frac{\gamma}{|m|^{\tau}}$$

Cure

A lower bound of this form tames the divisors and restores convergence of the series.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsConvergence and Diophantine bounds
Even without exact resonance the denominators in the homological solution can be arbitrarily small, since integer combinations of eigenvalues approach an eigenvalue closely. These small denominators can make the formal series diverge. Convergence then requires Diophantine conditions bounding how fast the combinations approach resonance, the heart of the analytic theory.

Hamiltonian Birkhoff Normal Form

Canonical changes

Use symplectic maps generated by a function so Hamilton's structure is preserved.

Normalize $H$, not the field

Simplify the Hamiltonian itself, removing nonresonant terms.

Elliptic equilibrium

$$H = \sum_k \omega_k I_k + \sum_{r\ge2} H_r(I,\varphi)\;\to\; H_*(I)$$

$I_k$ actions, $\omega_k$ frequencies.

Outcome

A series in actions alone — a nearly integrable description of the local dynamics.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsThe symplectic specialization
For Hamiltonian systems we change variables by canonical transformations generated by a function, so the symplectic structure is preserved. Birkhoff normal form simplifies the Hamiltonian itself rather than the field, removing nonresonant terms and leaving a series in the action variables. Near an elliptic equilibrium this organizes the local dynamics into nearly integrable layers.

A Worked Resonance

Take $\lambda=(1,-2)$

A planar field with these eigenvalues; seek quadratic monomials with $(m,\lambda)=\lambda_j$.

Test $j=1$, $\lambda_1=1$

$$m_1\cdot1+m_2\cdot(-2)=1$$

$|m|=2$: only $(m_1,m_2)$ giving sum $1$.

Resonant term found

$$\text{e.g. } y_1 y_2 \text{ in component }2:\;\; (1,1)\!\cdot\!(1,-2)=-1=\lambda_2$$

Verdict

That single quadratic coupling stays; every other quadratic term transforms away.

Lesson

The integer ratio $1:-2$ forces exactly the surviving nonlinearity.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsReading the surviving monomial
We take a two-dimensional saddle with eigenvalues in ratio one to minus two and find the single resonant monomial it forces. The example shows concretely which term cannot be removed and why the normal form keeps exactly that quadratic coupling while every other quadratic term is transformed away cleanly.

Recap

The machine

Near-identity maps solve a homological equation $L_A h_r=v_r$ at each degree.

The obstruction

Resonance $(m,\lambda)=\lambda_j$ leaves terms in $\ker L_A$.

The theorems

Poincare linearizes the nonresonant case; Poincare-Dulac reduces the rest.

The analysis

Small denominators decide convergence; Diophantine bounds rescue it.

One line to remember

$$\text{normal form} = Ay + (\text{resonant terms only})$$

Hamiltonian case

Canonical version is Birkhoff normal form in the actions.

Appendix 15: Normal FormsSynthesis
We normalize a field near equilibrium by successive near-identity changes, each solving a homological equation whose obstruction is resonance among eigenvalues. Nonresonant fields linearize formally by Poincare; resonant ones reduce to a resonant normal form by Poincare-Dulac; convergence is governed by small denominators; and Hamiltonian systems specialize to Birkhoff normal form.
