Poincaré's Last Geometric Theorem

A twisting, area-preserving map of the annulus must have fixed points

Conjectured

Poincaré (1912) — for the restricted three-body problem.

Proved

G. D. Birkhoff (1913) — the Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem.

Payoff

Fixed points of a return map $=$ periodic orbits of a flow.

Geometric TheoremPoincaré–Birkhoff
Open by framing this appendix as Poincaré's parting gift to dynamics. He could not prove the statement he needed for the restricted three body problem, so he published it as a conjecture in nineteen twelve. Stress that one fixed point of a map can mean a whole periodic orbit of a flow.

The Annulus and Its Boundaries

The arena

The closed annulus $A=\{(r,\theta): r_0\le r\le r_1\}$ in polar form.

Two circles

Inner boundary $r=r_0$ and outer boundary $r=r_1$.

Coordinates

$f:A\to A,\quad f(r,\theta)=(R,\Theta)$

Geometric TheoremThe annulus
Establish the stage carefully. The closed annulus has two boundary circles, an inner one and an outer one, and a radial coordinate interpolating between them. Every later picture lives here, so make sure students see the annulus as the natural arena where a return map of a two degree of freedom system acts.

The Twist Condition

Angle advance

$\Theta = \theta + \alpha(r)$ shears each circle by an amount set by its radius.

Opposite senses

$\alpha(r_0)<0<\alpha(r_1)$ — boundaries rotate the other way.

Sign change

$\alpha(r_0)\cdot\alpha(r_1) < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha(r_*)=0$

Geometric TheoremThe twist
Explain that a twist map advances angle by an amount that varies with radius. The decisive hypothesis is opposite rotation on the two boundaries, so the angular displacement changes sign across the annulus. Draw the shearing of a radial fiber on the board and have students predict where displacement must vanish.

Statement of the Theorem

Poincaré–Birkhoff

Let $f$ be an area-preserving homeomorphism of the closed annulus that maps each boundary circle to itself and rotates them in opposite directions. Then $f$ has at least two fixed points.

Why two, not one

An index / parity count forces a second fixed point once the first exists.

Hypotheses

  • Continuous bijection of $A$
  • Preserves area (measure)
  • Boundary twist of opposite sign

Conclusion

$f(p)=p$ for at least two points $p\in A$.

Geometric TheoremStatement
State the Poincaré Birkhoff theorem precisely. An area preserving homeomorphism of the closed annulus satisfying the boundary twist has at least two fixed points. Emphasise the count two, not one, since the parity argument forces a second point once a first exists. Contrast conjecture with Birkhoff's nineteen thirteen proof.

Why Area Preservation Is Essential

Drop the hypothesis

Twist both boundaries oppositely, then drift every interior point around — no fixed point survives.

Counterexample idea

$f(r,\theta)=(r,\;\theta+\beta(r)),\;\beta\neq 0$

Lesson

Area conservation is what pins orbits in place and forces returns.

Geometric TheoremArea preservation
Drive home that area preservation is not decoration. Without it one can rotate the inner and outer circles oppositely yet sweep all interior points around with no fixed point at all. Show the counterexample sketch and let students feel that measure conservation is what pins the orbits in place.

From Hamiltonian Flows to Return Maps

Surface of section

Cut the flow in an energy level with a transverse surface $\Sigma$.

Return map

Follow each orbit to its next crossing: $P:\Sigma\to\Sigma$.

Liouville

$\det DP = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; P$ preserves area

Geometric TheoremReturn map
Connect the abstract map to mechanics. Choosing a surface of section in an energy level reduces a continuous Hamiltonian flow to a discrete return map. Liouville's theorem makes that map area preserving in suitable coordinates, so the topological hypotheses of the theorem are met by genuine mechanical systems.

Periodic Orbits as Fixed Points

Dictionary

  • Fixed point $P(x)=x$ → orbit closing in one circuit
  • $P^q(x)=x$ → orbit of period $q$
Existence transfer

$\exists\,$ fixed point $\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\exists\,$ periodic orbit

Geometric TheoremDictionary
Make the dictionary explicit. A fixed point of the return map is a trajectory that closes after one circuit, so it is a periodic orbit of the flow. A point of period q for the map is an orbit closing after q crossings. This is why fixed point theorems become existence theorems for periodic motion.

Sketch of Birkhoff's Argument

Zero-angle set

Points whose angular displacement vanishes form a curve $\gamma$ joining the two boundaries (twist forces the sign change).

Area blocks escape

Area preservation forbids pushing $\gamma$ wholly inward or outward.

Forced crossing

So $\gamma$ and $f(\gamma)$ must intersect — those intersections are fixed points.

Geometric TheoremProof sketch
Sketch Birkhoff's reasoning without full rigour. The set of points whose angle is unchanged forms a curve joining the two boundaries because the twist forces a sign change. Area preservation then prevents that curve from being pushed entirely inward or outward, forcing intersections that are the fixed points.

The Restricted Three-Body Problem

Poincaré's problem

Find periodic solutions of a massless body near two heavy primaries.

Reduction

Near a known periodic orbit, the search becomes fixed points of an area-preserving annulus map.

Missing lemma

The geometric theorem is exactly what guarantees those fixed points exist.

Geometric TheoremThree-body
Return to Poincaré's motivation. In the restricted three body problem he sought periodic solutions near a known one and reduced the search to fixed points of an area preserving annulus map. The geometric theorem was exactly the missing lemma guaranteeing those periodic orbits exist.

Infinitely Many Periodic Orbits

Rotation interval

The two boundaries have rotation numbers $\rho_0<\rho_1$.

Every rational caught

$\dfrac{p}{q}\in(\rho_0,\rho_1)\;\Rightarrow\;P^q$ has a fixed point

Harvest

Each ratio yields a periodic orbit — infinitely many in all.

Geometric TheoremInfinitely many
Show the harvest. Applying the theorem to iterates of the map, and to rational rotation numbers between the two boundary rotation values, yields fixed points for every such ratio. Each gives a periodic orbit, so a single twisting area preserving map carries infinitely many distinct periodic orbits.

Worked Example: A Standard Twist

The map

$f(r,\theta)=\big(r,\;\theta+2\pi(r-\tfrac12)\big)$

Area preserving

$\det Df = 1$, since $r$ is unchanged and the shear is volume-neutral.

Opposite twist

At $r=0$ angle drops; at $r=1$ it rises — boundaries rotate oppositely.

Geometric TheoremWorked twist
Walk through the explicit standard twist example. The map advances angle by a radius dependent amount and leaves radius fixed before a perturbation. Verify the Jacobian has determinant one and that the boundary rotations have opposite sign, so the hypotheses hold and two fixed points are guaranteed.

Recap and Outlook

Three pillars

  • Annulus arena with two boundary circles
  • Twist of opposite sign on the boundaries
  • Area preservation

Payoff

At least two fixed points $=$ at least two periodic orbits — often infinitely many.

Legacy

Launched symplectic topology: the Arnold conjecture and Conley–Zehnder fixed-point theory generalize it to higher dimensions.

Slogan

twist $+$ conserved area $\;\Rightarrow\;$ periodic motion

Geometric TheoremRecap
Close by collecting the three pillars: the annulus arena, the twist hypothesis, and area preservation. Remind students that the theorem launched modern symplectic topology and the Conley Zehnder and Arnold conjectures. Leave them with the slogan that conserved area turns a twist into guaranteed periodic motion.
