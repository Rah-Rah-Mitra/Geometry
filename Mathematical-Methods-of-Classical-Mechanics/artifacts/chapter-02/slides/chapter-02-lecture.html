Systems with One Degree of Freedom

The setup

A single coordinate $x$ with mass normalized to one, driven by a force $-U'(x)$ from a potential $U$.

Why start here

It is the smallest system where motion is non-trivial yet fully readable from a graph.

Equation of motion

$$\ddot{x} = -\frac{dU}{dx}(x)$$

First-order form

Write $\dot{x}=y$, $\dot{y}=-U'(x)$ to get a vector field on the $(x,y)$ plane.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionOne degree of freedom
We fix the simplest non-trivial setting: a single coordinate moving under a force derived from a potential. Newton's law becomes a first-order vector field on the phase plane of position and velocity, and that field is what we will read off without any explicit integration.

The Phase Plane and Phase Curves

State

A point $(x,y)=(x,\dot x)$ is a complete instantaneous state of the system.

Foliation

One curve through each point: the plane splits into disjoint trajectories.

Equilibria

The field vanishes where $y=0$ and $U'(x)=0$.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionPhase plane
Velocity and acceleration define a planar vector field whose integral curves are the phase curves. Through each point passes exactly one curve, so the plane foliates into disjoint trajectories. Equilibria sit where the field vanishes, and the picture of all curves is the phase portrait we want.

Conservation of Energy

The trick

Multiply $\ddot x=-U'(x)$ by $\dot x$ and recognize a total derivative.

Decomposition

$T=\tfrac12\dot x^2$ is kinetic energy; $U(x)$ is potential energy.

The invariant

$$E = \tfrac12\,\dot{x}^2 + U(x) = \text{const}$$

Why it holds

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \dot{x}\big(\ddot{x}+U'(x)\big) = 0$$

Investigation of the Equations of MotionEnergy E = T + U
Multiplying the equation of motion by velocity and integrating reveals a conserved quantity, the total energy. This is the central invariant of the chapter: kinetic plus potential energy stays constant along motion, so each phase curve lies inside a single level set of the energy function.

Phase Curves are Energy Level Sets

Key identity

Each trajectory satisfies $E(x,y)=h$ for a fixed value $h$.

Solve for velocity

$$y = \pm\sqrt{2\big(h-U(x)\big)}$$

Reading rule

Motion is allowed only where $U(x)\le h$; turning points are where $U(x)=h$.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionLevel sets of E
Because energy is conserved, the phase curves are exactly the connected components of the level sets E equals constant. The motion in a potential well becomes a problem of slicing the energy surface horizontally and reading the resulting curves, which is pure one-variable calculus made geometric.

Equilibria and Their Stability

Equilibria

Critical points: $U'(x_0)=0$, $y=0$. The system can rest there forever.

Linearization

$$\ddot{\xi} = -U''(x_0)\,\xi$$

Minimum: center

$U''(x_0)>0$ gives oscillation $\xi\sim\cos(\omega t)$, $\omega=\sqrt{U''}$ — Lyapunov stable.

Maximum: saddle

$U''(x_0)<0$ gives growth $\xi\sim e^{\lambda t}$ — unstable.

Dirichlet

A strict potential minimum is a stable equilibrium.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionEquilibria & stability
Critical points of the potential are equilibria. A strict minimum gives a center surrounded by closed orbits and is Lyapunov stable; a maximum gives a saddle and is unstable. Linearizing the field at the equilibrium recovers these types from the sign of the second derivative of the potential.

The Period of Oscillation

Trapped motion

Between turning points $x_-,x_+$ the particle bounces back and forth.

From energy

$dt = dx/\dot x$ with $\dot x=\sqrt{2(h-U)}$ turns time into an integral over $x$.

Period integral

$$T(h) = \sqrt{2}\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{h-U(x)}}$$

Harmonic check

For $U=\tfrac12\omega^2 x^2$ the integral gives $T=2\pi/\omega$, independent of amplitude.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionPeriod T(h)
For motion trapped in a well between two turning points, the time of one full oscillation is an explicit integral of one over the velocity. The velocity follows from energy conservation, so the period is computed directly from the potential without ever solving the equation of motion.

The Pendulum and the Separatrix

Model

$\ddot\varphi=-\sin\varphi$, $U(\varphi)=1-\cos\varphi$ on a cylinder of angles.

Three regimes

Libration near the bottom, rotation over the top, and the curve between.

Separatrix

Through the unstable upright saddle; traversed in infinite time.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionPendulum
The pendulum unites every phenomenon: small oscillations near the bottom, rotations over the top, and the dividing curve between them. That separatrix passes through the unstable upright saddle, takes infinite time to traverse, and cleanly partitions the cylinder of states into libration and rotation.

Central Force Fields

Definition

Force along the radius, magnitude depending only on $r=|\mathbf{r}|$: $\mathbf{F}=-U'(r)\,\hat{\mathbf r}$.

Conserved angular momentum

$$M = r^2\dot{\varphi} = \text{const}$$

Kepler's second law

Constant $M$ means equal areas swept in equal times.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionCentral fields
We move to the plane and consider forces pointing along the radius with magnitude depending only on distance. Angular momentum is conserved because there is no tangential force, and this single invariant lets us eliminate the angle and reduce the planar motion to one radial degree of freedom.

Reduction and the Effective Potential

Eliminate the angle

Substitute $\dot\varphi=M/r^2$ into the energy to leave only $r$.

Radial energy

$$E = \tfrac12\dot{r}^2 + V_{\text{eff}}(r),\quad V_{\text{eff}} = U(r)+\frac{M^2}{2r^2}$$

Same machinery

Now a one-DOF problem: turning points, periods, stability all reappear.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionEffective potential
After fixing angular momentum, the radial coordinate moves in an effective potential that adds a centrifugal barrier to the true potential. The chapter's one-degree-of-freedom machinery now applies verbatim: energy levels on this effective curve dictate bounded orbits, unbounded escape, and circular motion.

Kepler's Problem: Orbits as Conics

Inverse square

$U(r)=-k/r$. The radial problem integrates in closed form.

Orbit equation

$$\frac{p}{r} = 1 + e\cos\varphi$$

Energy fixes the type

$E<0$ ellipse, $E=0$ parabola, $E>0$ hyperbola.

Investigation of the Equations of MotionKepler's problem
For the inverse-square attraction the orbit equation integrates to a conic section, with energy fixing whether it is an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola. Bounded negative-energy orbits are ellipses, recovering Kepler's first law and closing the chapter on a fully solved, historically decisive example.
