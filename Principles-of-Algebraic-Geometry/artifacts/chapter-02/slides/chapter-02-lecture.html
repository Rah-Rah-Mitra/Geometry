Divisors, Linear Equivalence, and the Picard Group

Divisor

\(D = \sum n_p \cdot p\), integer formal sum of points.

Picard group

\(\mathrm{Pic}(C) = \mathrm{Div}(C) / \sim\); \(D \sim D'\) iff \(D - D'\) is principal.

Degree decomposition

\(\mathrm{Pic}(C) = \bigsqcup_n \mathrm{Pic}^n(C)\); \(\mathrm{Pic}^0(C)\) is an abelian variety, the Jacobian.

Line bundles

Each Cartier divisor produces a line bundle; isomorphism classes form the Picard group.

Divisors organize the line bundlesDegree is the only discrete invariant
Define divisors on a curve. A divisor is an integer combination of points. Linear equivalence classes form the Picard group, which is the disjoint union of components indexed by degree. The degree-zero component is an abelian variety, the Jacobian. Every line bundle on the curve is the bundle associated to some divisor class.
Section 2
central theorem

Riemann-Roch on Curves

Statement

\(h^0(D) - h^0(K - D) = \deg D - g + 1\)

Canonical divisor

\(K\) has degree \(2g - 2\); \(h^0(K) = g\).

Consequences

For \(\deg D \ge 2g-1\): \(h^0(D) = \deg D - g + 1\). Below this threshold, sections may be sparse.

Why it works

Combines Serre duality \(h^0(K-D) = h^1(D)\) with the Euler characteristic identity.

Sections balanced against obstructionsThe most-used theorem in curve theory
State the Riemann-Roch theorem. For a divisor D on a curve of genus g, the dimension of H zero of L D minus the dimension of H zero of K minus D equals degree D minus g plus one. The theorem balances sections against obstructions; the canonical divisor K is the dual partner.
Section 3
embedding by K

The Canonical Map

Map

\(\phi_K : C \to \mathbb{P}^{g-1}\)

Embedding

For non-hyperelliptic \(C\) with \(g \ge 3\), \(\phi_K\) is an embedding (the canonical curve).

Hyperelliptic exception

If \(C\) admits a degree-two map to \(\mathbb{P}^1\), \(\phi_K\) factors through that map; the image is a rational normal curve.

Reading

The canonical map separates curves into two structural types and gives a default projective realization.

Canonical = intrinsic projective embeddingModulo the hyperelliptic exception
Introduce the canonical map. For curves of genus g at least two, the canonical divisor gives a map to projective space of dimension g minus one. The image is the canonical curve. Hyperelliptic curves are the exception: their canonical map factors through a degree-two cover of the projective line.
Section 4
curve → torus

The Abel-Jacobi Theorem

Map

\(\mathrm{AJ}: \mathrm{Pic}^0(C) \to J(C) := H^0(K)^\vee / H_1(C, \mathbb{Z})\)

Theorem

\(\mathrm{AJ}\) is an isomorphism.

Construction

For \(D = \sum p_i - \sum q_i\) of degree zero, integrate holomorphic differentials along chains.

Reading

The Picard group becomes a complex torus; linear equivalence of divisors becomes addition in an abelian variety.

Algebra of divisors becomes geometry of toriBridge to transcendental methods
Sketch the Abel-Jacobi theorem. Choose a base point; the map sending a degree-zero divisor to its integral of holomorphic differentials lands in the Jacobian. The theorem asserts this map is an isomorphism between the degree-zero Picard group and the Jacobian. This is the bridge between algebra and complex tori.

The Jacobian Variety

Object

\(J(C)\): complex torus of dimension \(g\); principally polarized by the Theta divisor.

Riemann theta

Section of the principal polarization; its divisor is the Theta divisor.

Torelli

The polarized Jacobian determines the curve up to isomorphism.

Schottky

Which principally polarized abelian varieties are Jacobians? Open in general; resolved for low genus.

The Jacobian is the curve's universal targetTheta functions encode its geometry
Describe the Jacobian variety. It is a complex torus of dimension g, principal polarized by the Theta divisor. The geometry of theta functions, Torelli's theorem, and the Schottky problem all study this object.
