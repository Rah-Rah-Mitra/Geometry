Homogeneous Points: Rays Not Coordinates

Each marked vector is a different representative of the same point \([v]\).

Definition

For a vector space \(V\), projective space is the quotient

$$\mathbb{P}(V)=(V\setminus\{0\})/\sim,\quad v\sim \lambda v,\ \lambda\ne 0.$$

Immediate Consequence

  • Coordinates \((x_0,\ldots,x_n)\) are representatives.
  • Homogeneous coordinates \([x_0:\cdots:x_n]\) name the class.
  • Scale-sensitive data, such as length, is not projective data.
Emphasize that a homogeneous vector is a representative, not the point itself. Students should look at a whole ray through the origin and ask what survives rescaling. The answer is incidence with other linear objects, while length and chosen coordinates disappear.

A Chart Is A Cut Through Rays

Affine Chart

On \(U_i=\{[x_0:\cdots:x_n]\mid x_i\ne 0\}\), divide by \(x_i\) and record ordinary coordinates.

$$[x_0:\cdots:x_n]\mapsto \left({x_0\over x_i},\ldots,\widehat{{x_i\over x_i}},\ldots,{x_n\over x_i}\right).$$

Inspection Target

Move the cutting line or plane. The coordinate list changes, but the ray and its incidence relations do not.

Use the chart picture to separate two acts. First the projective point is the ray. Then a chart cuts the ray and records ordinary numbers. Moving the cut changes the numbers, so the coordinate value is useful but not the geometric object.

Topology And The Projective Line

\(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{R})\) can be viewed as directions in the plane, with opposite unit vectors naming the same line.

Quotient Topology

The projection \(V\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{P}(V)\) declares a set open downstairs when its inverse image is open upstairs.

Two-Chart View Of \(\mathbb{P}^1\)

\(x_0\ne 0\)\([1:t]\)\([0:1]\)

The point at infinity is the direction that a chosen affine chart cannot see.

This is where topology enters gently. The quotient says that nearby directions should define nearby projective points, and a chart supplies ordinary coordinates only where one selected coordinate is nonzero. The point at infinity is not an extra afterthought; it is another direction.

Incidence From Linear Subspaces

Projective Subspaces

If \(0\ne W\le V\), then \(\mathbb{P}(W)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(V)\).

  • 1-dimensional \(W\) gives a projective point.
  • 2-dimensional \(W\) gives a projective line.
  • Hyperplanes come from codimension-one subspaces.

Meet And Join

Incidence computations are linear before they are drawn:

$$\operatorname{join}=\mathbb{P}(A+B),\qquad \operatorname{meet}=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B).$$
Connect projective subspaces back to linear algebra. A projective line in the plane is a two-dimensional linear subspace upstairs. Incidence questions are therefore span and intersection questions before any drawing begins, which is why projective geometry feels algebraic.

Projective Frames And Coordinates

Frame Principle

A projective frame supplies enough points in general position to fix homogeneous coordinates up to one common scalar.

Plane Case

In \(\mathbb{P}^2\), four points with no three collinear form the working model:

$$e_0,\ e_1,\ e_2,\ e_0+e_1+e_2.$$

Why General Position Matters

If three chosen points are collinear, they cannot anchor a full projective coordinate system for the plane.

A frame is a coordinate scaffold, not extra geometry. It lets transformations be solved from point correspondences.

A projective frame is the device that prevents coordinates from floating freely. In the plane, four points in general position let a projective map be pinned down up to scale. Stress that general position is doing real work here, because collinear choices cannot determine a full plane map.

Projective Maps: Matrices Mod Scalars

Definition

An invertible linear map \(A:V\to V\) induces a projective map

$$[x]\longmapsto [Ax].$$

Multiplying \(A\) by a nonzero scalar gives the same projective map.

Group Language

$$\operatorname{PGL}(V)=\operatorname{GL}(V)/k^\ast.$$

Practical test: lines map to lines, and incidence relations are preserved.

$$p\mapsto Hp,\qquad \ell\mapsto H^{-T}\ell \quad \text{preserve } \ell^T p=0.$$
Treat a projective transformation as a linear map with the scalar ambiguity removed. Multiplying the matrix by a nonzero constant changes representatives but not projective points. The important test is that lines go to lines and incidence relations are preserved.

Cross-Ratio: A One-Dimensional Invariant

Four Points On A Projective Line

In an affine chart, define

$$[a,b;c,d]={{(c-a)(d-b)}\over{(c-b)(d-a)}}.$$

The expression is unchanged by fractional linear maps \(t\mapsto(\alpha t+\beta)/(\gamma t+\delta)\).

Notebook Lab Habit

The coordinates are allowed to move; the invariant is the quantity to compare.

Before \(f\)\(-1,\ 0,\ 2,\ 5\)\(5/4\)

The cross-ratio is the small invariant that makes the one-dimensional theory concrete. Raw chart coordinates move under a fractional linear map, but this four-point number remains fixed. That is the habit the notebook asks for: inspect the invariant, not the displayed coordinates.

Perspective: Parallel Lines Acquire A Point

Projective Payoff

Central projection may turn a family of parallel affine lines into lines meeting at one vanishing point.

What Survives

  • Collinear points remain collinear.
  • Lines still meet, possibly at infinity.
  • The image plane is a chart, not the whole geometry.
Use the perspective image to make the payoff visible. Parallel affine lines are not sacred projective objects; after projection their images can share a vanishing point. What survives is collinearity and incidence with the image plane, not Euclidean parallelism.

Camera-Like Projection As Matrix

Homogeneous Camera Equation

$$\tilde{x}\sim P\tilde{X},\qquad P\in k^{3\times 4},\ \operatorname{rank}P=3.$$

Projective Reading

  • \(\tilde{X}\) is a world point in homogeneous coordinates.
  • \(\tilde{x}\) is an image point, again only up to scale.
  • Depth changes along a viewing ray do not change the image point.

Applications such as aerial photography use the same incidence language as the abstract chapter.

Frame the camera model as projective geometry wearing applied notation. A world point and an image point are homogeneous vectors related by a rank three matrix. Depth is sacrificed, but incidences such as lines mapping to lines remain the reliable evidence.

Pitfalls: Coordinates, Charts, Drawings

Tuple Trap

A nonzero tuple is a representative. The projective point is the whole equivalence class.

$$[x]=[\lambda x]$$

Chart Trap

A finite coordinate in one chart can become an infinite direction in another.

$$[1:t]=[s:1]\quad \text{where }s=1/t.$$

Drawing Trap

Parallelism, distance, and angle belong to extra affine or metric structure, not bare projective space.

$$\text{incidence survives; metric data need not.}$$

Diagnostic Question

Before claiming a fact is projective, ask whether it survives rescaling, chart change, and an invertible projective map.

Pause on these pitfalls before the recap because they cause most computational mistakes. A tuple may name a point in one chart and fail in another. A drawing can suggest Euclidean facts that projective maps do not preserve, so every claim needs the right invariant.

Recap: Tests For Projective Thinking

Checklist

  1. Name the homogeneous class before choosing a chart.
  2. Translate subspaces and incidences to linear algebra upstairs.
  3. Represent transformations by matrices only up to scale.
  4. Use invariants such as cross-ratio when coordinates move.
  5. Read perspective drawings as chart-dependent views.

Exit Exercise

Given a \(3\times 3\) homography \(H\) and two intersecting lines, predict the image intersection without plotting first. Then verify in a chart.

The chapter's route is a discipline: representatives are temporary, incidence is durable.
