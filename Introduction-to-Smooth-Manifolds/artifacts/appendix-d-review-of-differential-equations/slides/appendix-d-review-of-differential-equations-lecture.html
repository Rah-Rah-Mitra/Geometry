Appendix DReview of Differential Equations
Frame this appendix as the toolbox we draw on whenever a vector field on a manifold is written in local coordinates. After choosing a chart, an integral curve is exactly the solution of a first-order ODE system, so every theorem here transfers directly to flows. The goal of the lecture is not to reprove analysis from scratch but to recognize which hypothesis buys which guarantee: continuity alone gives existence, Lipschitz control gives uniqueness, and smoothness propagates to the solution map. Keep returning to the slogan that a local flow is the geometric face of an initial value problem.

First-Order Systems and Initial Value Problems

The System

\(\dot y(t)=V\bigl(t,y(t)\bigr),\quad y(t_0)=c\)

A curve solves the IVP exactly when its velocity equals the field at the current time and state.

Why First Order

A manifold vector field, read in a chart, becomes a first-order system. Higher-order ODEs reduce to first order by adding derivative coordinates.

An integral curve matches velocity to the fieldSlide 2 / 10
Start from the defining test: differentiate the proposed curve and check whether its velocity vector equals the field evaluated at that point and time. This is the discrete idea that the notebook validates with a symbolic residual for the rotation field. Stress the shape constraint that the right-hand side returns a vector of the same dimension as the state, since this is what makes the field a section. Remind students that order is not a real restriction: any higher-order equation becomes a first-order system on a larger state, which is exactly the bookkeeping we repeat when lifting nonautonomous systems later.

Existence and Uniqueness: Picard-Lindelof

Theorem (Local Existence and Uniqueness)

If \(V\) is continuous and locally Lipschitz in the state variable, then the IVP has a unique solution on some interval around \(t_0\).

Fixed-Point Form

\(I(y)(t)=c+\int_{t_0}^{t} V\bigl(s,y(s)\bigr)\,ds\)

A solution is a fixed point \(I(y)=y\) of the integral operator.

Contraction Estimate

\(\lVert I(y)-I(z)\rVert \le L\,\epsilon\,\lVert y-z\rVert\)

Choose the window so \(L\epsilon<1\); the Banach fixed-point theorem then yields a unique fixed point.

Lipschitz Condition

\(\lvert V(y)-V(z)\rvert \le L\,\lvert y-z\rvert\)

This local bound is the hypothesis that drives both existence and uniqueness.

Existence is a contraction; uniqueness needs Lipschitz controlSlide 3 / 10
Present existence and uniqueness as two sides of one estimate. Rewriting the IVP as a fixed-point problem for the integral operator turns analysis into a contraction argument: on a short enough interval the operator shrinks distances by the factor L epsilon, so Banach gives a unique fixed point. The notebook makes this concrete by iterating the operator for y prime equals sin y and watching successive Picard iterates draw uniformly closer. Emphasize that continuity alone secures existence but not uniqueness; the Lipschitz bound is precisely what prevents two solutions from peeling apart, which the next slide dramatizes.

The Flow of a Vector Field

Definition

The flow \(\Phi_t(x)\) sends a point \(x\) to where its integral curve sits after time \(t\), with \(\Phi_0=\mathrm{id}\).

Group Law

\(\Phi_s\circ\Phi_t=\Phi_{s+t}\)

Following the field for time \(t\) then \(s\) equals following it for time \(s+t\).

Geometric Reading

The flow packages all integral curves into one map; each vector field generates its own one-parameter family of diffeomorphisms.

The flow bundles every integral curve into one mapSlide 4 / 10
This slide is the bridge from analysis to geometry. Once existence and uniqueness hold, the assignment that sends each starting point to its position after time t is well defined, and that map is the flow. The group law is the algebraic shadow of uniqueness: two integral curves through the same point must coincide, so composing time steps simply adds the times. Note the qualifier that for a general vector field the flow is only local, which is why we must track domains; we make the partial nature precise on the maximal-intervals slide. For complete fields the flow is a genuine one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms.

Smooth Dependence on Initial Conditions

Theorem

If \(V\) is smooth, the flow map \(\Phi(t,c)\) is smooth jointly in time and in the initial point \(c\), not merely smooth in \(t\) alone.

Worked Flow (Logistic)

\(\Phi(t,c)=\dfrac{c\,e^{t}}{1-c+c\,e^{t}}\)

Explicit on \(0

Variational Equation

The sensitivity \(z=\partial\Phi/\partial c\) solves the linearization along the solution:

\(\dot z=(1-2\Phi)\,z,\quad z(0)=1\)

Inspection Target

A finite-difference derivative in \(c\) matches the variational solution, making the theorem visible on the solution surface.

Differentiating the flow in c gives the variational equationSlide 5 / 10
Distinguish carefully between two statements: each individual solution is smooth in time, and the map from initial data to solution is itself smooth. The theorem asserts the stronger second claim, and the notebook makes it tangible with the logistic flow, whose closed form lets us plot the solution surface and the partial derivative in c. The key computational object is the variational equation: differentiating the ODE with respect to the initial condition yields a linear equation for the sensitivity along the solution. Point out that the finite-difference check and the symbolic variational solution agree, which is the empirical content of smooth dependence.

Continuation and Maximal Intervals

Maximal Interval

Every solution extends to a largest open interval; beyond it the solution leaves every compact set or blows up.

Example \(\dot x = x^2\)

\(\Phi_t(x)=\dfrac{x}{1-tx}\)

The denominator records the blow-up: \(x>0\) escapes at \(t=1/x\).

Completeness

A field whose flow exists for all time is complete; compact support guarantees this.

The denominator encodes when the solution ceases to existSlide 6 / 10
Local existence only promises a short interval, so we ask how far a solution can be continued. The continuation principle says a maximal solution either lives for all time or escapes every compact set in finite time. The notebook's clean illustration is the field x squared on the line, whose flow x over one minus t x makes the maximal interval readable straight from the denominator: a positive initial value blows up at one over x. Tie this to manifolds by noting that completeness, automatic for compactly supported fields, is exactly the condition that upgrades a local flow to a global one-parameter group.

Autonomous Systems, Equilibria, and the Clock Lift

Autonomous vs Nonautonomous

\(\dot y=V(y)\quad\text{vs}\quad \dot x=V(t,x)\)

An autonomous field is time-independent: the arrows in state space never change. Equilibria are the zeros \(V(y_*)=0\), the constant solutions.

Clock Lift to Autonomous Form

Add a clock coordinate \(s\) with \(\dot s=1\):

\(\bigl(\dot s,\dot x\bigr)=\bigl(1,\;V(s,x)\bigr)\)

The time-dependent system becomes an integral curve of an autonomous field on extended space.

One extra coordinate makes time just another stateSlide 7 / 10
Autonomous systems are the natural geometric case because the vector field is a static section: the same point always carries the same arrow, and equilibria are simply its zeros, which are the constant integral curves. Nonautonomous systems seem different until we apply the clock lift, adding a coordinate s whose derivative is one. The lifted field one comma V of s comma x is autonomous on the extended space, and the original time-dependent curve becomes an ordinary integral curve there. Stress that this is not a formal trick: it is exactly why the flow and existence theorems stated for autonomous fields cover the time-dependent case too.

Linear Systems and the Matrix Exponential

Closed-Form Flow

\(\dot Z=AZ\;\Longrightarrow\; Z(t)=e^{tA}Z(0)\)

The matrix exponential is the flow of a constant-coefficient linear field.

Trace-Determinant Classification

For \(2\times 2\) real \(A\), the signs of \(\operatorname{tr}A\), \(\det A\), and the discriminant \((\operatorname{tr}A)^2-4\det A\) sort the portrait.

Eigenvalues, matrix exponential, and phase portrait agreeSlide 8 / 10
Linear constant-coefficient systems are the laboratory where everything is explicit. The flow is the matrix exponential e to the t A applied to the initial vector, which is just the linear instance of the general flow. For two-by-two real matrices the trace, determinant, and discriminant completely classify the local portrait into saddles, nodes, spirals, centers, and the degenerate repeated-root cases; the notebook plots representatives and marks them in the trace-determinant plane. The applied lab invites students to edit the matrix, rerun, and check that the classifier matches the visible eigenvalues, with a residual confirming the matrix exponential genuinely solves Z prime equals A Z.

Relevance: Integral Curves and Flows on Manifolds

Charts Turn Fields into ODEs

A vector field on \(M\), read in any chart, is a first-order system. Its integral curves are the ODE solutions.

Partial Group Law

\(\Phi_s\bigl(\Phi_t(x)\bigr)=\Phi_{s+t}(x)\)

Holds only on the common domain where every intermediate point is defined.

Transfer of Theorems

Existence, uniqueness, smooth dependence, and maximal intervals all carry over to the local flow of a smooth field.

The local flow is a partial action of time on the manifoldSlide 9 / 10
Now collect the payoff. A smooth vector field on a manifold, expressed in a chart, is exactly a smooth first-order system, so every result in this appendix applies to its integral curves and to the local flow. The subtlety we must respect on manifolds is domains: the composition law Phi s after Phi t equals Phi s plus t is an exact identity but only where all the intermediate points are defined, which is why we call the flow a partial action of time. This is the precise sense in which a vector field generates a one-parameter family of diffeomorphisms locally, and globally when the field is complete.
