First-Order Systems and the Initial Value Problem

The system

An unknown curve $x(t)$ whose velocity is prescribed by a field $F$.

Reduction

Any higher-order equation becomes first-order by stacking derivatives into a vector.

In a chart

A manifold vector field is exactly such a system after choosing coordinates.

Initial value problem

$$x'(t) = F\big(t, x(t)\big), \qquad x(t_0) = x_0$$

Autonomous case

$$x'(t) = V\big(x(t)\big)$$

$F:U\subseteq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$; a solution is a curve $x:J\to\mathbb{R}^n$ on an interval $J\ni t_0$.

Printed pp. 663-683First-Order Systems and the IVP
A vector field in a chart becomes a first-order system. We fix notation: the unknown is a curve, the data is a right-hand side and a starting point, and an integral curve is one whose velocity matches the field at every instant. Higher-order equations reduce to this form.

Integral Curves and the Slope Field

Tangency

A curve is an integral curve exactly when $x'(t)$ equals the field at the current point.

No crossings

Uniqueness means two integral curves cannot meet at a point and then diverge.

Geometry first

Read existence and uniqueness off the picture before proving them.

Printed pp. 663-683Integral Curves of a Vector Field
The slope field picture is the right intuition. Through each point passes exactly one trajectory tangent to the arrows. I want students to read the existence and uniqueness theorem geometrically first: distinct integral curves never cross, because crossing would give two solutions through one point.

From Differential to Integral Equation

Equivalent problem

Integrating the IVP from $t_0$ folds in the initial condition automatically.

Why it helps

A continuous solution of the integral equation is automatically $C^1$ and solves the system.

Operator form

It exhibits the solution as a fixed point of an integral operator $I$.

Integral equation

$$x(t) = x_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} F\big(s, x(s)\big)\, ds$$

Fixed point

$$x = I(x), \qquad I(x)(t) = x_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} F(s,x(s))\,ds$$

Printed pp. 663-683The Integral Equation
Integrating the system converts the initial value problem into an equivalent integral equation. This is the pivotal move: a continuous solution of the integral equation is automatically differentiable and solves the original system. It also packages the initial condition directly into the equation, which is exactly what the fixed-point argument needs.

Lipschitz Control of the Right-Hand Side

Continuity is not enough

Continuous $F$ gives existence but can fail uniqueness.

Local Lipschitz

A bound in the state variable controls how fast solutions separate.

Smooth $\Rightarrow$ Lipschitz

$C^1$ fields are locally Lipschitz by the mean value inequality.

Lipschitz estimate

$$|F(t,y) - F(t,z)| \le L\,|y - z|$$

Failure example

$$x' = 2\sqrt{|x|},\ \ x(0)=0$$

Not Lipschitz at $0$: many solutions share the same start.

Printed pp. 663-683Lipschitz Conditions
Continuity of the right-hand side gives existence but not uniqueness. The extra ingredient is a Lipschitz estimate in the space variable, which smooth fields satisfy locally by the mean value inequality. I stress that Lipschitz control is what prevents two solutions from separating after they touch.

Picard Iteration: Successive Approximations

Iteration

$$x_{k+1}(t) = x_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} F\big(s, x_k(s)\big)\, ds$$

Contraction

On a short interval, $\|I(y)-I(z)\|\le L\varepsilon\,\|y-z\|$ with $L\varepsilon<1$.

Convergence

The iterates are Cauchy in sup-norm; the limit is the fixed point.

Printed pp. 663-683Picard Iteration
Picard iteration builds the solution by repeatedly applying the integral operator to an initial guess. On a short interval the operator is a contraction, so the iterates form a Cauchy sequence converging uniformly. This is the constructive heart of the proof, and the successive approximations are computable.

Fundamental Existence and Uniqueness

Picard–Lindelöf

If $F$ is continuous and locally Lipschitz in $x$, then for each $(t_0,x_0)$ the IVP has a unique solution on some interval $(t_0-\delta,\,t_0+\delta)$.

Smooth corollary

A smooth vector field has a unique integral curve through every point.

Proof roadmap

1 · Recast IVP as $x=I(x)$.
2 · Choose $\varepsilon$ so $I$ is a contraction.
3 · Contraction mapping principle gives a unique fixed point.
4 · The fixed point solves the IVP.

Uniqueness

Two solutions through one point satisfy $|u'|\le L|u|$, so they coincide.

Printed pp. 663-683Existence and Uniqueness Theorem
This is the fundamental theorem, Picard and Lindelof. On the integral operator the contraction mapping principle delivers a unique fixed point on a small interval around the start time. Smooth right-hand sides are locally Lipschitz, so every smooth vector field has unique integral curves.

Maximal Interval and Continuation

Gluing

Overlapping local solutions agree, so they combine into one maximal solution on an open interval.

Escape lemma

If the interval is bounded, the solution leaves every compact set as $t$ approaches the endpoint.

Blow-up

Finite-time singularities are how maximality is enforced.

Example: finite-time blow-up

$$x' = x^2,\quad x(0)=x_0>0$$

$$x(t) = \dfrac{x_0}{1 - t\,x_0}$$

Solution exists only for $t < 1/x_0$.

Local flow form

$$\Phi_t(x) = \dfrac{x}{1 - t x}$$

Printed pp. 663-683Maximal Interval of Existence
Local solutions glue into a single maximal solution defined on an open interval. The escape lemma is the key continuation fact: if the maximal interval is bounded above, the solution must eventually leave every compact set. The example x prime equals x squared shows finite-time blow-up concretely.

Smooth Dependence on Initial Conditions

Flow map

$$\Phi(t, c): \text{ solution at time } t \text{ from start } c$$

The key fact

$\Phi$ is smooth jointly in time, initial point, and parameters.

Why it matters

This is exactly what makes the flow of a smooth field a smooth map — the foundation of the next chapter.

Printed pp. 663-683Smooth Dependence on Initial Data
This is the theorem that makes flows smooth. The solution depends smoothly jointly on time and on the initial point and parameters. I emphasize the distinction from each solution being smooth in time alone; here the whole solution map is smooth, which is far stronger and harder.

The Variational Equation

Sensitivity

Differentiate the flow in its initial condition to measure how trajectories spread.

Linear along the solution

The derivative solves a linear ODE driven by the Jacobian of $F$.

Checkable

It turns the abstract smoothness theorem into a concrete computation.

Variational equation

$$z' = D_x F\big(t,\Phi(t,c)\big)\,z, \qquad z(t_0)=I$$

Logistic flow example

$$\Phi(t,c) = \dfrac{c\,e^{t}}{1 - c + c\,e^{t}}$$

$$z' = (1 - 2\Phi)\,z$$

Printed pp. 663-683The Variational Equation
Differentiating the flow with respect to its initial condition gives a linear ODE, the variational equation, driven by the Jacobian of the field along the solution. This makes smooth dependence checkable: the sensitivity solves a concrete linear system, illustrated here with the logistic flow map.

Linear Systems and the Matrix Exponential

Constant-coefficient system

$$Z' = A Z, \qquad Z(t) = e^{tA}\,Z(0)$$

Matrix exponential

$$e^{tA} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{t^k A^k}{k!}$$

Global solution — no blow-up. Trace and determinant of $A$ classify the portrait.

Printed pp. 663-683Linear Systems and the Matrix Exponential
Constant-coefficient linear systems are solved globally by the matrix exponential. For two by two real matrices the trace and determinant classify the phase portrait into saddles, nodes, spirals, and centers. This is the compact laboratory where eigenvalues, exponentials, and pictures all agree.
