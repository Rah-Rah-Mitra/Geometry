Submersions, Immersions, and Embeddings

When does a nonlinear map look linear in the right coordinates?

Submersion

Differential surjective; locally a projection.

Immersion

Differential injective; locally an inclusion.

Embedding

Immersion that is also a homeomorphism onto its image.

This chapter, printed pages 77 through 97, asks a single guiding question: when can a nonlinear smooth map be trusted to look like a linear map after we choose the right coordinates near a point? The answer is governed entirely by the rank of the differential, and the three full-rank cases organize everything that follows.

The Rank of a Smooth Map

Definition

The rank of $F$ at $p$ is the rank of the linear map $dF_p$, equal to the rank of any coordinate Jacobian.

Coordinate-free

Rank is invariant under change of charts, so it is intrinsic to the map.

Semicontinuity

Rank can only jump up nearby, never down: full rank persists.

Differential in charts

$$\operatorname{rank}_p F=\operatorname{rank}\Big(\tfrac{\partial \hat F^{\,i}}{\partial x^{\,j}}(p)\Big)$$

Full rank

$\operatorname{rank} F=\min(m,n)$ at $p$ is the best a map between an $m$- and $n$-manifold can achieve.

Printed pp. 77-97The Rank of a Smooth Map
The rank of a map at a point is just the rank of its differential there, computed as the rank of any coordinate Jacobian. Because rank is a linear-algebra invariant under change of basis, it is the first diagnostic we apply, and it is lower semicontinuous, so full rank persists on an open set.

Full Rank Is an Open Condition

Why open

A nonvanishing minor of size $r$ is continuous, so rank $\ge r$ on a neighborhood.

Consequence

Being a submersion or an immersion is a local, open condition on the manifold.

Where it fails

A cusp or a fold loses rank on a curve; there constant rank breaks.

Printed pp. 77-97Full Rank Is an Open Condition
A nonzero minor of size equal to the rank is a continuous function, so wherever the differential has full rank it keeps full rank nearby. This is why being a submersion or an immersion is a local, open condition. The four panels separate genuine full-rank behaviour from rank that drops along a curve or a line.

The Rank Theorem: A Universal Normal Form

Theorem (constant rank)

If $F$ has constant rank $r$ near $p$, there are charts in which

$$(x^1,\dots,x^r,x^{r+1},\dots,x^m)\;\longmapsto\;(x^1,\dots,x^r,0,\dots,0)$$

Read the hypothesis

Constant rank, not merely full rank at one point: it must hold on a neighborhood.

Printed pp. 77-97The Rank Theorem
The rank theorem is the chapter's central normal form. If a smooth map has constant rank near a point, suitable charts on source and target turn it into the linear projection that keeps the first r coordinates and zeroes the rest. Emphasize that the hypothesis is constant rank, not merely full rank at a single point.

The Square Case: Inverse Function Theorem

Statement

If $dF_p$ is invertible, then $F$ is a diffeomorphism from a neighborhood of $p$ onto a neighborhood of $F(p)$.

Local diffeomorphism

A map that is a diffeomorphism near every point; the $r=m=n$ case of the rank theorem.

Worked example

$F(x,y)=(x+a y^2,\,y),\quad \det dF=1$

Smooth inverse $F^{-1}(u,v)=(u-a v^2,v)$; grid residual $=0$.

Printed pp. 77-97Inverse Function Theorem
The square full-rank case is the inverse function theorem: an invertible differential gives a smooth local inverse, so the map is a local diffeomorphism. Walk through the triangular example whose Jacobian determinant is one everywhere, making the inverse an explicit polynomial with zero residual on the grid.

Immersions: Injective Differential

Definition

$F$ is an immersion if $dF_p$ is injective at every $p$, so no tangent vector is collapsed.

Local normal form

$$(u^1,\dots,u^k)\;\longmapsto\;(u^1,\dots,u^k,0,\dots,0)$$

Caution

Immersion is local. The image can self-cross or accumulate even when $F$ is injective.

Printed pp. 77-97Immersions
An immersion has injective differential everywhere, so no tangent direction is crushed. The rank theorem specializes to say an immersion locally looks like the standard inclusion of a coordinate plane. Stress that this is purely local: a single regular curve immerses the line, yet its global image can still misbehave.

Submersions and Local Sections

Definition

$F$ is a submersion if $dF_p$ is surjective everywhere; locally a coordinate projection.

Local section theorem

Through every $q$ in the image there is a smooth right inverse

$\sigma:U\to M,\qquad F\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}_U.$

Why it matters

Sections drive openness, the quotient property, and covering-space theory.

Printed pp. 77-97Submersions and Local Sections
A submersion has surjective differential everywhere and locally looks like a projection. The rank theorem produces a smooth local section through every point, a right inverse of the map. Local sections are the engine behind nearly every structural property of submersions that we prove in the rest of the chapter.

Submersions Are Open Maps and Quotients

Open map

Local sections force $F$ to send open sets to open sets.

Quotient map

A surjective submersion is a topological quotient map onto its target.

Characteristic property

$g$ is smooth iff $g\circ F$ is smooth: this is how we pass smoothly to quotients.

Universal property

$$\begin{array}{ccc}M & \xrightarrow{\;F\;} & N\\[2pt] & {\scriptstyle g\circ F}\searrow & \downarrow{\scriptstyle g}\\[2pt] & & P\end{array}$$

If $F$ is a surjective submersion, $g$ is smooth $\Leftrightarrow$ $g\circ F$ is smooth.

Uniqueness

Such a $g$ is unique because $F$ is surjective, and smoothness descends through sections.

Printed pp. 77-97Submersions Are Open Maps
Because each point sits in the image of a local section, a submersion sends open sets to open sets. A surjective submersion is therefore a quotient map, and a map out of the base is smooth exactly when its composite with the submersion is smooth. This is the characteristic property used to pass smoothly to quotients.

Smooth Embeddings

Definition

$F$ is a smooth embedding if it is an immersion and a topological embedding: a homeomorphism onto its image with the subspace topology.

Two ingredients

Infinitesimal injectivity ($dF$) and global topological faithfulness.

Gap

An injective immersion need not be an embedding; the differential cannot see topology.

Sufficient conditions

  • A proper injective immersion is an embedding.
  • An injective immersion of a compact manifold is an embedding.
  • An open immersion (a submersion that is an immersion) is an embedding onto an open set.

Slogan

Embedding $=$ immersion $+$ “the image remembers the domain.”

Printed pp. 77-97Smooth Embeddings
An embedding is an immersion that is also a homeomorphism onto its image with the subspace topology. The differential only sees infinitesimal data, so injectivity of the differential is not enough; we additionally need the image topology to match the domain. Embeddings are the maps that realize one manifold faithfully inside another.

Immersion vs Embedding: The Figure-Eight

Second failure

An irrational-slope line on the torus is an injective immersion whose image is dense, hence not an embedding.

Printed pp. 77-97Immersion That Is Not an Embedding
The figure-eight curve is the canonical counterexample: a smooth injective immersion of an open interval whose two ends approach the crossing point, so the image topology disagrees with the domain. Contrast it with the embedded arc on the right, and mention the dense irrational line on the torus as a second failure.

Submersions and Regular Level Sets

Regular value test

$f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ is a submersion where $\nabla f\neq 0$; there each level set is a smooth hypersurface.

Regular vs singular

$$\nabla(x^2+y^2)=(2x,2y),\quad \nabla(xy)=(y,x)$$

Unit circle is regular; the cross $xy=0$ is singular at the origin.

Printed pp. 77-97Submersions and Level Sets
A scalar map is a submersion exactly where its gradient is nonzero, and there its level sets are regular. The unit circle is a regular level of the squared norm, while the crossing locus of xy equals zero is singular at the origin because both partials vanish there. Rank gives an immediate regular-value test.

Smooth Covering Maps

Definition

A surjective local diffeomorphism $p$ such that each base point has an evenly covered neighborhood.

Model

$p(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^1$

Local sections

Each sheet over an evenly covered arc is a smooth section; it is unique once a lifted point is fixed.

Properness

A proper local diffeomorphism between connected manifolds is a smooth covering.

Printed pp. 77-97Smooth Covering Maps
A smooth covering map is a local diffeomorphism whose sheets repeat over evenly covered neighborhoods. The exponential cover of the circle lifts a small arc to disjoint intervals in the line; picking one sheet gives a smooth local section that is unique once the lifted base point is fixed. Proper local diffeomorphisms are coverings.

Proof Traffic and Takeaways

Rank is intrinsic

It survives coordinate change, so it is the first diagnostic for any smooth map.

Constant rank straightens

It is the hypothesis that turns nonlinear maps into projections and inclusions.

Topology is extra

Embeddings and coverings need data the differential cannot supply.

Printed pp. 77-97Proof Traffic and Takeaways
Trace the dependency: the inverse function theorem feeds the rank theorem, which yields normal forms for immersions and submersions, then local sections, openness, quotient behaviour, and coverings. The recurring moral is that rank is the coordinate-free invariant, constant rank is the hypothesis that straightens the map, and topology supplies what the differential cannot see.
