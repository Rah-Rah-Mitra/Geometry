Riemannian Metrics

Giving a smooth manifold a way to measure lengths, angles, and distance

The new structure

A metric $g$ is a smooth field of inner products $g_p$ on tangent spaces $T_pM$.

What it buys us

Norms, angles, curve length, a distance function, and an identification $TM \cong T^*M$.

Why it exists

Every smooth manifold carries one, built with partitions of unity.

Printed pp. 327-348Riemannian Metrics
This chapter, printed pages 327 to 348, adds the one structure smooth manifolds lack on their own: a way to measure. A Riemannian metric is a smoothly varying inner product on tangent spaces, and from it follow lengths, angles, distance, and the identification of vectors with covectors.

Definition of a Metric

Definition

A Riemannian metric is a smooth $(0,2)$-tensor field $g$ that at each $p$ is

symmetric, $\;g_p(v,w)=g_p(w,v)$,

positive definite, $\;g_p(v,v)>0$ for $v\neq 0$.

A Riemannian manifold

The pair $(M,g)$. Each $g_p$ is a genuine inner product on $T_pM$.

Tensor, not a form

$g$ assigns a form to every point smoothly; it is a section of $T^*M \otimes T^*M$.

The load-bearing axiom

Positive definiteness drives every later result: norms, distance, Gram-Schmidt.

Pseudo-Riemannian

Keeping only nondegeneracy gives signatures and null vectors instead.

Printed pp. 327-348Definition of a Metric
Stress that a metric is a tensor field, not a single bilinear form. At each point it is symmetric and positive definite, and smoothness means the component functions are smooth in any chart. Positive definiteness is the property that everything in the chapter ultimately depends on.

Components in a Chart

Coordinate expression

$g = g_{ij}\,dx^i \otimes dx^j,\quad g_{ij}=g\!\left(\partial_i,\partial_j\right)$

The matrix $G=(g_{ij})$ is symmetric and positive definite at every point.

Unit ellipse

$\{v: v^{\top}Gv=1\}$ is short along large-eigenvalue directions: moving that way is expensive.

Density

$\sqrt{\det G}$ is the local area/volume scale of the metric.

Printed pp. 327-348Local Components
In a chart the metric becomes a symmetric positive-definite matrix of functions. Show that the eigenvalues are positive everywhere and that the unit ellipse, the solution set of v transpose G v equals one, is short where the metric is large. This is the workhorse picture for the whole chapter.

Lengths and Angles

Norm

$|v|_g = \sqrt{g_p(v,v)}$

Angle

$\cos\theta = \dfrac{g_p(v,w)}{|v|_g\,|w|_g}$

Orthogonality

$v \perp w \iff g_p(v,w)=0$. Coordinate axes need not be metric-orthogonal.

The off-diagonal effect

$g_{12}\neq 0$ tilts the measured angle between $\partial_1$ and $\partial_2$ away from $90^\circ$.

Visual square, metric skew

A grid that looks square on paper can be far from orthogonal once $g$ measures it.

Same inner product

Length and angle both come from the single bilinear form $g_p$.

Printed pp. 327-348Lengths and Angles
With an inner product on each tangent space, the norm and the angle formula are immediate. Emphasize that a coordinate grid that looks square on paper may be non-orthogonal in the metric: the off-diagonal entries are exactly what tilts the measured angle away from ninety degrees.

Length of a Curve

Length functional

$L_g(\gamma) = \displaystyle\int_a^b |\gamma'(t)|_g\,dt$

$\gamma:[a,b]\to M$ piecewise smooth; integrate the metric speed.

Reparametrization

$L_g$ is invariant under orientation-preserving reparametrizations of $\gamma$.

The bridge

This integral lifts pointwise linear algebra to genuine global geometry.

Printed pp. 327-348Length of a Curve
Curve length integrates the metric speed of the velocity field. Point out reparametrization invariance, and that this single integral is the bridge from pointwise linear algebra to global geometry. The local norm under the integral sign is the only place the metric enters.

The Riemannian Distance

Definition

$d_g(p,q) = \inf_{\gamma} L_g(\gamma)$

infimum over piecewise smooth curves from $p$ to $q$.

Metric space axioms

$d_g\ge 0$, symmetric, vanishes only on the diagonal, triangle inequality.

Theorem

$d_g$ makes $(M,d_g)$ a metric space whose metric topology equals the original manifold topology.

Proof idea

Locally compare $|\cdot|_g$ with the Euclidean norm in a chart to control $d_g$ from both sides.

Not extra data

Distance is derived from $g$, not an independent structure.

Printed pp. 327-348The Distance Function
Distance is the infimum of lengths over admissible curves joining two points. Walk through symmetry, the triangle inequality, and positivity, and stress the nontrivial theorem that this distance induces the original manifold topology, proved by local comparison with the Euclidean norm.

Existence of Metrics

Theorem

Every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric.

Construction

On each chart pull back the Euclidean metric; glue with a subordinate partition of unity $\{\psi_\alpha\}$.

Glued metric

$g = \sum_\alpha \psi_\alpha\, \varphi_\alpha^{*}\bar g$

Convexity key

A positive weighted average of positive-definite forms is positive definite, so the sum is a metric.

Printed pp. 327-348Existence via Partitions of Unity
Every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric. The proof glues Euclidean metrics pulled back from charts using a partition of unity. The key convexity fact is that a positive weighted average of positive-definite forms is positive definite, so the sum is a genuine metric.

The Musical Isomorphism: Flat

Flat map

$\flat: TM \to T^*M,\quad v^{\flat} = g(v,\,\cdot\,)$

Components: $(v^\flat)_i = g_{ij}v^j$, lowering an index.

Isomorphism

Nondegeneracy of $g$ makes $\flat$ a bundle isomorphism $T_pM \xrightarrow{\sim} T_p^*M$.

Co-metric

$g$ induces an inner product on covectors via the matrix $G^{-1}$.

Printed pp. 327-348Flat: Lowering Indices
The flat map sends a vector to the covector obtained by feeding it into the first slot of the metric. In components this is multiplication by the matrix G, lowering an index. Because the metric is nondegenerate the flat map is an isomorphism between the tangent and cotangent spaces.

Sharp and the Gradient

Sharp map

$\sharp = \flat^{-1}: T^*M \to TM,\quad (\omega^\sharp)^i = g^{ij}\omega_j$

Raising an index with the inverse metric $g^{ij}$.

Gradient

$\operatorname{grad} f = (df)^{\sharp},\quad g(\operatorname{grad} f, Y) = df(Y)$

Metric dependent

The gradient is not the tuple of partial derivatives in general.

Polar example

$G = \mathrm{diag}(1, r^2),\quad G^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(1, r^{-2})$

For $f=r^2\cos\theta$ the sharp factor $1/r^2$ is essential to recover the true gradient.

Round trip

$\sharp\circ\flat = \mathrm{id}$: lower then raise returns the original vector.

Verified symbolically

The notebook checks $g(\operatorname{grad}f, Y) - df(Y)=0$ exactly.

Printed pp. 327-348Sharp and the Gradient
Sharp is the inverse of flat and uses the inverse metric to raise an index. The gradient of a function is the sharp of its differential, so it is metric dependent. Use the polar example where G is diag one, r squared to show the gradient is not just the tuple of partial derivatives.

Pullback Metrics and Isometries

Pullback

$(F^{*}g)_p(v,w) = g_{F(p)}\!\big(dF_p v,\, dF_p w\big)$

When is it a metric?

If $F$ is an immersion ($dF_p$ injective), $F^*g$ is positive definite, hence a metric.

Isometry

A diffeomorphism $F$ with $F^{*}\tilde g = g$; the equivalence of Riemannian manifolds.

Gram matrix

For $F$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$, $F^*\bar g = J^{\top}J$, the Gram matrix of the Jacobian columns.

Local isometry

Drop bijectivity: just $dF$ preserving $g$ pointwise. Lengths of curves are preserved.

Invariants

Distances, angles, and later curvature are isometry invariants.

Printed pp. 327-348Pullbacks and Isometries
A smooth map pulls back a metric by inserting pushed-forward vectors. An isometry is a diffeomorphism whose pullback recovers the metric exactly, the right notion of equivalence for Riemannian manifolds. Pullback by an immersion is automatically a metric because injective differentials keep the Gram matrix positive definite.

Examples: Induced and Round Metrics

Euclidean

$\bar g = \sum_i (dx^i)^2$, the flat reference metric on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Submanifold

Restrict $\bar g$ to tangent directions; components are the Gram matrix of the parametrization.

Helicoid

$F(u,v)=(u\cos v,\,u\sin v,\,v)$

$G = J^{\top}J = \mathrm{diag}(1,\,1+u^2)$

Hyperbolic

$g = y^{-2}(dx^2+dy^2)$ on the upper half-plane: constant negative curvature.

Printed pp. 327-348Induced and Round Metrics
Submanifolds inherit a metric by restricting the ambient one to tangent directions; the components form the Gram matrix of the Jacobian columns. Use the helicoid, whose induced metric is diag one, one plus u squared, and the round sphere as the canonical embedded examples.

Orthonormal Frames

Definition

A local frame $(E_1,\dots,E_n)$ with $g(E_i,E_j)=\delta_{ij}$; the metric is the identity in this frame.

Gram-Schmidt

Apply the orthonormalization process smoothly to any coordinate frame to produce one.

Local, not global

Such frames exist on neighborhoods but obstructions can block a global choice.

Process

Why it helps

In an orthonormal frame, norms, the volume form, and many computations become coordinate-clean.

Coframe

The dual coframe $(\varepsilon^i)$ gives $g=\sum_i \varepsilon^i \otimes \varepsilon^i$.

Printed pp. 327-348Orthonormal Frames
Gram-Schmidt applied smoothly to a local frame produces an orthonormal frame in which the metric is the identity matrix. Such frames exist on any neighborhood but generally not globally. They are the computational tool that makes many later constructions, like the volume form, clean and coordinate-free.

Recap and the Boundary of the Theory

The chain

Inner product $\to$ norm and angle $\to$ curve length $\to$ distance and metric topology.

Musical maps

$\flat$ and $\sharp$ identify $TM$ with $T^*M$ and define the gradient.

Foundations

Metrics exist everywhere; orthonormal frames and induced metrics give working examples.

Warning: pseudo-Riemannian

Lorentz: $\;\langle v,v\rangle = -t^2 + x^2$

Nondegenerate but indefinite: nonzero null vectors have zero length.

Printed pp. 327-348Recap and Pseudo-Riemannian Contrast
Recap the chain from inner product to length to distance, and the musical maps tying TM to its dual. Close with the pseudo-Riemannian contrast: dropping positivity for mere nondegeneracy creates null vectors and breaks the length and distance theory, marking the boundary of the Riemannian world.
