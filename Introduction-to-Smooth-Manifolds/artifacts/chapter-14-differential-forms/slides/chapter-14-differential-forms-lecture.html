Alternating Tensors

Definition

A covariant $k$-tensor $\omega$ is alternating if swapping any two arguments reverses sign.

Consequence

$\omega$ vanishes on any linearly dependent $k$-tuple.

Space

The alternating $k$-tensors on $V$ form $\Lambda^k(V^*)$.

Antisymmetry

$\omega(\dots,v_i,\dots,v_j,\dots) = -\,\omega(\dots,v_j,\dots,v_i,\dots)$

Degeneracy test

$v_1,\dots,v_k$ dependent $\;\Rightarrow\;\omega(v_1,\dots,v_k)=0$

Printed pp. 349-376Alternating Tensors
A covariant k-tensor is alternating when swapping any two arguments flips the sign. The immediate consequence is that it vanishes on any linearly dependent tuple. This is the algebraic seed of orientation: alternating tensors cannot tell apart degenerate inputs, so they naturally measure signed volume rather than raw products.

Multi-index basis

For increasing $I=(i_1<\dots

Determinant form

$\varepsilon^I(v_1,\dots,v_k)=\det\big(v_j^{\,i_a}\big)$.

Printed pp. 349-376Elementary k-Covectors and Dimension
Antisymmetrizing tensor products of dual basis covectors gives elementary k-covectors indexed by increasing multi-indices. Because index order and repetition are already settled, the basis is counted by choosing k slots from n, so the dimension is the binomial coefficient. This explains why Lambda-k is small compared to the full tensor space.

The Wedge Product

Antisymmetrized product

$\wedge$ projects the tensor product onto its alternating part.

Graded commutativity

$\alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{kl}\,\beta\wedge\alpha$.

Self-annihilation

For a 1-form, $\alpha\wedge\alpha=0$.

Exterior algebra

$\Lambda^*(V^*)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\Lambda^k(V^*)$

Generated by 1-forms

$\varepsilon^{i_1}\!\wedge\cdots\wedge\varepsilon^{i_k}$ span $\Lambda^k$

Associative

$(\alpha\wedge\beta)\wedge\gamma=\alpha\wedge(\beta\wedge\gamma)$.

Printed pp. 349-376The Wedge Product
The wedge product antisymmetrizes the tensor product, producing the graded-commutative exterior algebra. The key sign rule says alpha wedge beta equals minus beta wedge alpha for one-forms, so a form wedged with itself dies. Associativity lets us build any k-covector from one-forms, which is how the basis is generated.

Wedge as Oriented Area

Determinant rule

$(\alpha\wedge\beta)(u,v)=\alpha(u)\beta(v)-\alpha(v)\beta(u)$

Signed area

Equals the oriented area of the parallelogram on $(u,v)$.

Orientation

Swap $u,v$ and the sign flips; repeat a vector and it is $0$.

Printed pp. 349-376Wedge as Oriented Area
Evaluate alpha wedge beta on a pair of vectors and you get the determinant of their two measurements. That determinant is exactly the signed area of the parallelogram they span. Swap the vectors and the area changes sign; feed in the same vector twice and the parallelogram collapses to zero. Forms are oriented rulers.

Differential Forms

Smooth section

A $k$-form assigns a $k$-covector $\omega_p\in\Lambda^k(T_p^*M)$ to each point, smoothly.

Local expression

Built from coordinate monomials $dx^{i_1}\!\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}$.

Smoothness

Lives in the coefficient functions $\omega_I$.

Coordinate form

$\omega=\sum_{I}\,\omega_I\,dx^{i_1}\!\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}$

Space of $k$-forms

$\Omega^k(M)=\Gamma\big(\Lambda^k T^*M\big)$

Printed pp. 349-376Differential Forms as Smooth Sections
A differential k-form is a smooth section of the bundle of alternating k-tensors: at every point it is a k-covector, and its coefficient functions vary smoothly in coordinates. The basis monomials dx-superscript-I let us write any form, and smoothness lives entirely in those coefficient functions.

The Exterior Derivative

Unique antiderivation

$d:\Omega^k\to\Omega^{k+1}$ raising degree by one.

On functions

$df$ is the ordinary differential.

Nilpotent

$d\circ d=0$, with no extra data needed.

Graded Leibniz rule

$d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^{k}\alpha\wedge d\beta$

Characterization

$df,\;\;d^2=0,\;\;\text{Leibniz}\;\Rightarrow\;d$ unique

Printed pp. 349-376The Exterior Derivative
The exterior derivative is the unique antiderivation that raises degree by one, reduces to the ordinary differential on functions, and squares to zero. Uniqueness is the punchline: no extra geometric data is needed, so d exists on every smooth manifold. The Leibniz rule carries a sign keyed to the degree of the first factor.

Coordinates and $d^2=0$

Coordinate formula

$d\Big(\textstyle\sum_I \omega_I\,dx^I\Big)=\sum_I d\omega_I\wedge dx^I$

Why it squares to zero

Mixed partials are symmetric; the wedge is antisymmetric. The two cancel.

1-form case

$d(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=(\partial_x Q-\partial_y P)\,dx\wedge dy$.

Printed pp. 349-376Coordinate Formula and d-squared
In coordinates, d differentiates each coefficient and wedges with dx, antisymmetrizing automatically. Equality of mixed partials together with the antisymmetry of the wedge forces d of d to vanish. I always show students the one-form case: the curl-like coefficients reappear, and applying d again cancels in pairs.

Interior Multiplication

Contraction

$\iota_V$ inserts a vector $V$ into the first slot of a form.

Lowers degree

$\iota_V:\Omega^k\to\Omega^{k-1}$.

Antiderivation

$\iota_V\circ\iota_V=0$ with a degree-keyed sign rule.

Definition

$(\iota_V\omega)(X_1,\dots,X_{k-1})=\omega(V,X_1,\dots,X_{k-1})$

Product rule

$\iota_V(\alpha\wedge\beta)=\iota_V\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^{k}\alpha\wedge\iota_V\beta$

Printed pp. 349-376Interior Multiplication
Interior multiplication inserts a vector field into the first slot of a form, lowering degree by one. It is an antiderivation with a degree-keyed sign, and inserting the same vector twice gives zero. Contraction is the algebraic partner of the wedge and the second ingredient in Cartan's magic formula.

Pullback and Naturality

Pullback

$F^*$ carries a form on $N$ back to a form on $M$, no inverse required.

Naturality

Pullback commutes with $d$.

Payoff

Coordinate-free integration and Stokes' theorem.

Commutes with d

$F^*(d\omega)=d\big(F^*\omega\big)$

Respects wedge

$F^*(\alpha\wedge\beta)=F^*\alpha\wedge F^*\beta$

Printed pp. 349-376Pullback and Naturality
Pullback moves forms backward along a smooth map, and unlike vector fields, forms always pull back without any inverse. The decisive fact is naturality: pullback commutes with the exterior derivative. This single identity is what makes integration of forms coordinate-free and powers the proof of Stokes' theorem later.

Closed and Exact Forms

Closed

$d\omega=0$.

Exact

$\omega=d\eta$ for some $\eta$.

Exact $\Rightarrow$ closed

Because $d^2=0$, every exact form is closed.

de Rham cohomology

$H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)=\dfrac{\ker d|_{\Omega^k}}{\operatorname{im} d|_{\Omega^{k-1}}}$

The gap

Closed but not exact forms detect global topology.

Printed pp. 349-376Closed and Exact Forms
A form is closed when its exterior derivative vanishes and exact when it is itself a derivative. Because d squares to zero, every exact form is closed. The converse can fail, and the gap between closed and exact is measured by de Rham cohomology, the topological invariant this whole machinery is built to compute.

Grad, Curl, Divergence in $\mathbb{R}^3$

$d$ on $0$-forms

$df$ is the gradient covector.

$d$ on $1$-forms

Encodes the curl of a vector field.

$d$ on $2$-forms

Gives the divergence.

de Rham complex in $\mathbb{R}^3$

$\Omega^0 \xrightarrow{\ \nabla\ } \Omega^1 \xrightarrow{\ \nabla\times\ } \Omega^2 \xrightarrow{\ \nabla\cdot\ } \Omega^3$

Classical identities

$\nabla\times\nabla f=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times \mathbf F)=0$

Same fact

Both are instances of $d^2=0$.

Printed pp. 349-376Grad, Curl, Divergence in R3
In three dimensions the form calculus reproduces vector calculus exactly. The differential of a function is the gradient, d of a one-form encodes the curl, and d of a two-form gives the divergence. The two instances of d-squared-equals-zero become the classical identities curl of grad is zero and divergence of curl is zero.
