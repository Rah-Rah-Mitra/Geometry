The Total Derivative Is a Linear Map

Definition

$F$ is differentiable at $a$ if a linear map $DF(a)$ obeys

$F(a{+}v)=F(a)+DF(a)v+R(v)$

with $|R(v)|/|v|\to 0$ as $v\to 0$.

The Point

$DF(a)$ is the unique best linear model. The diagnostic is the scaled remainder, not the tangent picture alone.

Differentiability = existence of a controlled linear modelSlide 2 / 10
Stress that differentiability is the existence of a single linear map that approximates the increment with a remainder vanishing to higher than first order. This is the coordinate-free definition that transfers verbatim to manifolds once we have tangent spaces. The notebook turns this into a numerical diagnostic: it shrinks a circle of radius r around a and measures the maximum of remainder over r, watching it fall toward zero. Insist that the tangent plane picture is only the cartoon; the scaled remainder is the actual content of the theorem.

Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix

From Linear Map to Matrix

In standard coordinates $DF(a)$ is recorded by the Jacobian

$\big(DF(a)\big)^i{}_j=\dfrac{\partial F^i}{\partial x^j}(a)$

Caution

Partials may all exist while $F$ fails to be differentiable. Continuity of the partials ($C^1$) guarantees the Jacobian really is the total derivative.

Jacobian = coordinate matrix of the differentialSlide 3 / 10
The Jacobian is the bridge between the abstract linear map and computation. Make the row-column convention explicit: row i is the gradient of the i-th component, column j collects the response to a push in the j-th input direction. The crucial subtlety, which the notebook flags, is that existence of all partials is strictly weaker than differentiability; the standard counterexamples have partials at the origin yet no linear model. Continuous partials (class C-one) repair this and make the Jacobian genuinely equal to the total derivative. On a manifold this matrix is exactly the coordinate representation of the differential between charts.

Chain Rule: Derivatives Compose

$D(G\circ F)(a) = DG\big(F(a)\big)\,\circ\,DF(a)$

Functorial

Linearization respects composition: linearize $F$, then $G$ at the image, then multiply the matrices.

Determinants

$\det D(G{\circ}F)=\det DG\cdot\det DF$

Local area scales multiply.

Notebook Check

SymPy confirms the matrix residual $D(G{\circ}F)-DG(F)\,DF$ is exactly the zero matrix.

Composition of maps becomes a product of matricesSlide 4 / 10
The chain rule is the single most reused fact in differential geometry: it is what makes the tangent map functorial and what guarantees that transition computations between charts are consistent. Present it first as composition of linear maps, then as the concrete matrix product. The determinant corollary previews change of variables: infinitesimal volume scales multiply along a composition. The notebook proves the matrix identity symbolically for an explicit pair of maps, so the residual being the exact zero matrix is a machine certificate, not a numerical approximation.

Inverse Function Theorem

Statement

If $F$ is $C^k$ and $DF(a)$ is invertible, then $F$ restricts to a $C^k$ diffeomorphism of a neighborhood of $a$ onto a neighborhood of $F(a)$.

Proof Idea

Normalize $DF(a)$ to the identity and solve $F(x)=y$ by a contraction mapping; the inverse inherits smoothness.

Strictly Local

No universal neighborhood size, and an invertible derivative at a point does not give global invertibility.

Invertible derivative converts into a local chartSlide 5 / 10
This theorem is the workhorse behind charts and coordinate changes. The hypothesis is purely linear-algebraic, invertibility of one derivative, yet the conclusion is a full nonlinear local diffeomorphism with a smooth inverse. Sketch the proof strategy: reduce to the identity derivative, then run a contraction to solve the equation. Emphasize locality with the notebook's perturbation lab, where the determinant of a nonlinear chart stays positive on a square only until the perturbation size reaches one, after which the hypothesis fails near the origin. The minimum determinant on the box is the numerical certificate.

Implicit Function Theorem

Statement

If $\Phi(a,b)=0$ and $\partial_y\Phi(a,b)$ is invertible, then near $a$ the solution set is a graph $y=g(x)$ with $g$ as smooth as $\Phi$.

Derivative

$Dg=-(\partial_y\Phi)^{-1}\partial_x\Phi$

Level sets become graphs; the engine for submanifoldsSlide 6 / 10
The implicit function theorem is the inverse function theorem in level-set clothing, and it is the analytic source of the regular level set theorem for submanifolds. The key hypothesis is invertibility of the partial derivative in the variables we want to solve away. Then the solution set is locally a graph and the graphing function is exactly as smooth as the constraint. The notebook realizes this by Newton-iterating along x to trace the implicit curve and checking that the residual phi along the recovered graph is essentially zero. The derivative formula falls straight out of differentiating the constraint.

Constant Rank: Normal Forms

Rank Theorem

If $DF$ has constant rank $r$ near $a$, then in suitable local coordinates $F$ looks like the linear projection

$(x^1,\dots,x^n)\mapsto(x^1,\dots,x^r,0,\dots,0)$

Two Limiting Cases

  • Full rank, injective derivative: immersion, $F$ is a local slice.
  • Full rank, surjective derivative: submersion, $F$ is a local projection.
Constant rank linearizes a map up to a change of chartsSlide 7 / 10
The rank theorem is the capstone of this group: it says that a smooth map of locally constant rank can be straightened, by changing coordinates in both source and target, into a plain linear projection. Immersions and submersions are the two extreme cases that the manifold theory leans on constantly for embedded submanifolds, quotients, and fiber structures. This slide is not directly computed in the notebook, but it is the conceptual destination of the inverse and implicit theorems, so present it as the unifying normal-form statement that those two theorems specialize.

Taylor's Theorem and Smoothness Classes

Taylor with Remainder

$f=P_k+R_k$ where $P_k$ is the degree-$k$ polynomial and $R_k$ is controlled by order-$(k{+}1)$ derivatives.

Smoothness Classes

  • $C^k$: continuous partials through order $k$.
  • $C^\infty$: smooth, all orders exist.
  • $C^2$ gives equality of mixed partials.
Smoothness is a budget on controlled remaindersSlide 8 / 10
Taylor's theorem is a control statement, not just an expansion: subtract the degree-k polynomial and the leftover is governed by derivatives of the next order. The notebook validates this by tracing the error of a second-order polynomial along a ray and confirming it stays below a sampled cubic bound on a log-log plot. Tie this to the smoothness hierarchy: class C-k means continuous partials up to order k, smooth means all orders, and the C-two hypothesis is exactly what makes mixed partials commute. These hypotheses do real work; dropping continuity breaks the equality of mixed partials, a counterexample the notebook explicitly records.

Multiple Integrals and Change of Variables

Change of Variables

$\displaystyle\int_{G(U)}\!f=\int_U f(G)\,|\det DG|$

Riemann Setup

Lower / upper sums squeeze the integral; a measure-zero boundary does not change the volume.

Polar Example

$(r,\theta)\mapsto(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$ has $|\det DG|=r$.

The Jacobian determinant is the infinitesimal volume scaleSlide 9 / 10
Change of variables is where the determinant earns its geometric meaning: it is the limit of image-cell volume over parameter-cell volume, the local stretch factor of the map. The polar example makes this concrete, with the determinant equal to r so that cells far from the origin map to larger area. Connect to the Riemann framework the appendix uses: lower and upper sums squeeze the integral and domains with measure-zero boundary integrate cleanly, which the notebook visualizes with a shrinking boundary tube. The absolute value foreshadows the form-versus-density distinction that integration on manifolds will need.
