The Total Derivative

Definition

$F(a+v) = F(a) + DF(a)\,v + R(v)$

with $\dfrac{|R(v)|}{|v|}\to 0$ as $v\to 0$.

Best affine model

$DF(a)$ is the unique linear map whose graph, shifted to $F(a)$, is tangent to the surface.

Printed pp. 642-662The Total Derivative
The total derivative is defined by an approximation quality, not a formula. A linear map qualifies as the derivative exactly when the leftover error shrinks faster than the displacement. Stress the quotient norm-R over norm-v tending to zero; that vanishing-faster-than-linear condition is what makes the tangent plane the best affine model.

The Jacobian Matrix

Coordinate matrix

When $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable, $DF(a)$ is represented by the matrix of partials.

Caution

Partials existing is weaker than differentiability. You need them continuous.

Jacobian

$DF(a) = \left[\dfrac{\partial F^i}{\partial x^j}(a)\right]_{i,j}$

Scalar case

For $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ the Jacobian is the gradient row $\nabla f(a)^{\mathsf T}$.

Printed pp. 642-662The Jacobian Matrix
Once differentiability is granted, the abstract linear map becomes a concrete matrix of partial derivatives. Emphasize that partials existing is weaker than differentiability; you need them continuous. The Jacobian is the coordinate representation of the differential, and this is the bridge between hand computation and the intrinsic objects on a manifold.

The Chain Rule

Composition law

$D(G\circ F)(a) = DG\big(F(a)\big)\,DF(a)$

Functoriality

Linearize $F$, linearize $G$ at the image, multiply the Jacobians in that order.

Printed pp. 642-662The Chain Rule
The chain rule is the statement that linearization respects composition. Linearize F at the point, linearize G at the image, and multiply the Jacobians in that order. I like to call this functoriality. It is why differentials compose cleanly and why coordinate changes on manifolds behave predictably under the tangent map.

Smoothness and Mixed Partials

The $C^k$ hierarchy

$C^k$: all partials up to order $k$ exist and are continuous. $C^\infty$: smooth, of every order.

Hypothesis is real

Drop continuity and there is a classic function whose two mixed partials at $0$ disagree.

Equality of mixed partials ($C^2$)

$\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^i\,\partial x^j} = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^j\,\partial x^i}$

Consequence

Order of differentiation commutes once you have earned $C^2$.

Printed pp. 642-662Smoothness and Mixed Partials
Define the C-k hierarchy and smooth as infinitely differentiable. The headline theorem is equality of mixed partials under a C-two hypothesis. Warn students that the hypothesis is real: there is a classic function whose two mixed partials at the origin disagree because continuity fails. Order of differentiation only commutes when you have earned it.

Directional Derivatives

Definition

$D_v f(a) = Df(a)\,v = \nabla f(a)\cdot v$

No unit vector needed

Scaling the direction $v$ scales the derivative linearly; that linearity is exactly having a total derivative.

Printed pp. 642-662Directional Derivatives
The directional derivative collapses to the differential applied to a vector, which in Euclidean coordinates is the gradient dotted with the direction. Point out that the direction vector need not be a unit vector here; scaling the vector scales the derivative linearly. This linearity in the direction is exactly the content of having a total derivative.

Taylor's Theorem with Remainder

Degree-$k$ expansion

$f(a+v) = P_k(v) + R_k(v)$

$R_k$ is governed by derivatives of order $k+1$.

Integral remainder

The integral form is exact and bounds cleanly, the cleanest for our estimates.

Why it matters

Taylor control is the engine behind smooth bump functions and local normal-form arguments later in the book.

First order

$f(a+v) = f(a) + Df(a)v + O(|v|^2)$

Printed pp. 642-662Taylor's Theorem with Remainder
Taylor's theorem is a control statement: subtract the degree-k polynomial and the remainder is governed by order k-plus-one derivatives. The integral form of the remainder is the cleanest for our purposes because it is exact and bounds cleanly. This is the engine behind smooth bump functions and local normal-form arguments later in the book.

Differentiation Under the Integral

Licensed interchange

$\dfrac{d}{dt}\!\int f(x,t)\,dx = \int \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t}(x,t)\,dx$

The license

Continuity of the parameter partial $\partial_t f$ permits moving the derivative inside.

A family of theorems

One member of a family of interchange results: swap two limit operations only when something is uniformly controlled.

Companion

Uniform convergence lets integrals pass to a limit; derivatives need the derivative sequence uniform.

Printed pp. 642-662Differentiation Under the Integral
Differentiating under an integral sign is a licensed interchange of limit and integral. The license is continuity of the parameter partial derivative. I frame this as one member of a family of interchange theorems, alongside uniform convergence results, where the recurring moral is that you may swap two limit operations only when something is uniformly controlled.

Inverse Function Theorem

Theorem

If $F$ is $C^k$ and $DF(a)$ is invertible, then $F$ restricts to a $C^k$ diffeomorphism between neighborhoods of $a$ and $F(a)$.

Local only

Invertibility of $DF$ at one point gives nothing global and no universal radius.

Printed pp. 642-662Inverse Function Theorem
This is the centerpiece. If the total derivative at a point is invertible, the map is a diffeomorphism on some neighborhood. Stress the word local: invertibility of the derivative at one point gives nothing global and no universal radius. The grid picture, a coordinate mesh sent invertibly to a curvilinear mesh, is the mental image to carry forward.

Implicit Function Theorem

Theorem

If $F(a,b)=0$ and the block $\partial F/\partial y$ at $(a,b)$ is invertible, then near $a$ the level set is a graph $y=g(x)$ with $g$ smooth.

Solving for $y$

The equation $F(x,y)=0$ defines $y$ implicitly as a smooth function of $x$.

Printed pp. 642-662Implicit Function Theorem
The implicit function theorem is the level-set face of the same coin. If the block partial in the hidden variable is invertible, the equation F of x and y equals zero solves for y as a smooth function of x near the point. This is precisely how level sets become graphs, and how regular level sets become embedded submanifolds.

The Proof Engine: Contraction Mapping

Principle

$d\big(\Phi(x),\Phi(y)\big)\le \lambda\, d(x,y),\ \ \lambda<1$

on a complete space gives a unique fixed point $\Phi(x^*)=x^*$.

Found by iteration

Repeated application $x_{n+1}=\Phi(x_n)$ converges to $x^*$.

How it proves the theorems

Normalize $DF$ to the identity, write inversion as a fixed-point problem, and the contraction supplies existence and smooth dependence.

Printed pp. 642-662The Proof Engine: Contraction Mapping
Both theorems are proved by one engine: the contraction mapping principle. A map that shrinks distances by a fixed factor on a complete space has a unique fixed point, found by iteration. Normalize the derivative to the identity, write inversion as a fixed-point problem, and the contraction supplies both existence and smooth dependence.
