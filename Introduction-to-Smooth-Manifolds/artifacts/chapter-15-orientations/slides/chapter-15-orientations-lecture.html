Orienting a Vector Space

Equivalence of bases

Two ordered bases agree when the transition matrix has positive determinant.

Exactly two classes

The relation has precisely two equivalence classes: the two orientations.

A choice

To orient $V$ is to pick one class as positive.

Definition

$(b_i)\sim(c_i)\iff \det\!\big(c=Ab\big)>0$

Consequence

$\dim_{\mathbb R}V=n\ge 1 \Rightarrow$ two orientations

Printed pp. 377-399Orienting a Vector Space
Begin with linear algebra. Two ordered bases agree in orientation exactly when the change-of-basis matrix has positive determinant. This is an equivalence relation with precisely two classes, so a finite-dimensional real vector space carries exactly two orientations, one chosen as standard.

The Two Orientation Classes

Positive class

Standard basis and any positive shear of it.

Negative class

Reflect one axis: $\det=-1$.

Sign is everything

$\det\begin{psmallmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{psmallmatrix}=-1$

Printed pp. 377-399The Two Classes
Make the two classes concrete in the plane. The standard frame and any positive shear of it both turn counterclockwise and sit in the positive class; a reflection flips the second arrow and lands in the negative class. The determinant sign is the entire content of the distinction.

From Pointwise to Global

Pointwise orientation

An orientation of each $T_pM$, point by point.

Continuity condition

Each point has a chart whose frame is positive throughout.

Orientable

A continuous pointwise orientation exists.

Local frame

$\big(\partial_1,\dots,\partial_n\big)_p$ positive near each $p$

Why continuity?

Without it, neighboring tangent spaces could disagree and the choice would jump.

Printed pp. 377-399Pointwise to Global
A pointwise orientation assigns an orientation to each tangent space. Such a choice is an orientation of the manifold only when it varies continuously, meaning every point has a chart whose coordinate frame is positively oriented throughout. Manifolds admitting such a choice are called orientable.

Orientation Forms

Nowhere-vanishing top form

A smooth $n$-form $\omega$ with $\omega_p\neq 0$ for all $p$.

Selects positive bases

Call a frame positive when $\omega$ evaluates positively on it.

Equivalence

$M$ is orientable iff such an $\omega$ exists.

Positive frame

$\omega_p(E_1,\dots,E_n)>0$

Same orientation

$\tilde\omega=f\,\omega,\;\; f>0$

Printed pp. 377-399Orientation Forms
On an n-manifold a nowhere-vanishing n-form is an orientation form. It selects, at each point, the bases on which it evaluates positively, giving a consistent pointwise orientation automatically. Two such forms induce the same orientation when they differ by a positive function, so orientability equals existence of such a form.

Oriented Atlases

Positive overlaps

All transition maps have positive Jacobian determinant.

Consistency

$\det D(\varphi_\beta\circ\varphi_\alpha^{-1})>0$

Equivalent encoding

A positively-oriented atlas is just another way to name the orientation.

Printed pp. 377-399Oriented Atlases
The chart-based criterion: an oriented atlas is one whose transition maps all have positive Jacobian determinant on overlaps. The sign tracker contrasts identity and positive-shear overlaps, which preserve orientation, with reflection and coordinate swap, which reverse it and cannot belong to a consistently oriented atlas.

Non-Orientable: The Mobius Band

Monodromy

Transport a frame around the core curve; it returns reflected.

Cylinder contrast

On the cylinder the same loop returns the frame unchanged: orientable.

Sign after one loop

$\text{Mobius}:-1 \qquad \text{cylinder}:+1$

Printed pp. 377-399The Mobius Band
The Mobius band is the standard non-orientable surface. Transport a transverse frame once around the central loop and it returns reflected, so no continuous global choice of orientation exists. The cylinder, by contrast, returns the frame unchanged, and so it is orientable.

Klein Bottle and Projective Spaces

Klein bottle

Contains a Mobius band, so it inherits the orientation flip and is non-orientable.

Antipodal map

On $S^n$ the antipodal map preserves orientation iff $n$ is odd.

Projective space

$\mathbb{RP}^n$ is orientable exactly when $n$ is odd.

Sign of the antipode

$\det(-I_{n+1})=(-1)^{\,n+1}$

Same bookkeeping

Each example reduces to whether a covering symmetry preserves or reverses sign.

Printed pp. 377-399Klein Bottle and RP^n
Other non-orientable spaces share this monodromy. The Klein bottle embeds a Mobius band and inherits the flip. For real projective space the antipodal map on the sphere preserves orientation exactly when the dimension is odd, so RP^n is orientable precisely when n is odd.

The Induced Boundary Orientation

Stokes orientation

An oriented $M$ with boundary induces an orientation on $\partial M$.

Outward-normal-first

A boundary frame is positive when prefixing the outward normal is positive for $M$.

Purpose

This is the convention that makes Stokes' theorem sign-clean.

Rule

$(E_1,\dots,E_{n-1})\;\text{positive}\iff(N,E_1,\dots,E_{n-1})\;\text{positive}$

N points out

$N\in T_pM,\;\; N\;\text{outward}$

Printed pp. 377-399Boundary Orientation
An oriented manifold-with-boundary induces a Stokes orientation on its boundary by the outward-normal-first rule: a boundary frame is positive when prefixing the outward normal yields a positive ambient frame. This is the convention that makes Stokes' theorem hold without stray signs.

Boundary Orientation on the Disk

Forced direction

The rule selects the counterclockwise tangent everywhere.

Positivity check

$\det[\,N\;\;T\,]>0$ on $\partial D$

Picture meets sign

The intuitive boundary direction and the determinant agree.

Printed pp. 377-399Boundary on the Disk
On the oriented disk the rule forces the counterclockwise tangent. At each boundary point the outward normal then the induced tangent form a positively oriented pair, so the determinant of that ordered pair is positive everywhere. The picture and the sign computation agree exactly.

The Riemannian Volume Form

Metric plus orientation

Together they pick out one canonical top form.

Coordinate coefficient

In oriented coordinates the coefficient is $\sqrt{\det g}$, always positive.

Reversal

Flip the orientation and the form changes sign; the density stays positive.

Volume form

$dV_g=\sqrt{\det g}\;dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n$

Printed pp. 377-399Riemannian Volume Form
Add a Riemannian metric to an oriented manifold and you get a canonical volume form. In oriented coordinates its coefficient is the square root of the determinant of the metric, always positive. Reversing the orientation reverses the form while the underlying density stays positive.

The Orientation Double Cover

Points are orientations

The cover collects every pointwise orientation of $M$.

Connectedness test

Connected iff $M$ is non-orientable; splits in two iff orientable.

Two-to-one

$\widetilde M_{\mathrm{or}}\to M,\quad \text{fiber}=\{\pm\}$

Printed pp. 377-399Orientation Double Cover
Every manifold carries an orientation double cover whose points are pointwise orientations. It is connected exactly when the base is non-orientable, and the base is orientable exactly when the cover splits into two sheets. Deck transformations that preserve orientation are the obstruction being measured.
