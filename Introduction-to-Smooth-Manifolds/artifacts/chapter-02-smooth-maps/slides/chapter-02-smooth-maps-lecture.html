Smooth Functions: Coordinates Make the Test Ordinary

Definition

$f:M\to\mathbb{R}^k$ is smooth when its coordinate representation is ordinary smooth on a Euclidean open set.

No intrinsic derivative

The abstract set $M$ has no partial derivatives. The chart supplies them.

Coordinate test

$\hat f = f \circ \varphi^{-1} \in C^\infty(\varphi(U))$

Example

$f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ becomes $\hat f(r,\theta)=r^2$ in polar coordinates — plainly smooth.

Printed pp. 32-49Smooth Functions in Charts
A scalar function is smooth when its coordinate representation, the composite with an inverse chart, is ordinary smooth on a Euclidean open set. Stress that the manifold has no intrinsic notion of partial derivative; the chart supplies it. The example, x squared plus y squared becoming r squared in polar coordinates, shows the test in action.

Why the Chart Choice Does Not Matter

Two overlapping charts

On the overlap, $\varphi$ and $\tilde\varphi$ differ by a smooth transition map.

Well-defined

Composing a smooth function with a smooth transition stays smooth, so the test is chart-independent.

Transition argument

$f\circ\tilde\varphi^{-1} = \big(f\circ\varphi^{-1}\big)\circ\big(\varphi\circ\tilde\varphi^{-1}\big)$

Why it works

The smooth atlas guarantees the transitions are smooth — this is the whole point of demanding them.

Printed pp. 32-49Independence of Charts
Walk students through why the chart choice does not matter: any two overlapping charts differ by a smooth transition map, so composing with it preserves ordinary smoothness. This is the well-definedness argument that justifies the whole definition. Without smooth transitions on the atlas, the word smooth would have no meaning on the manifold.

Smooth Maps: Two Translations

Source and target charts

Pick $\varphi$ around $p$ and $\psi$ around $F(p)$, with $F$ landing inside the target chart.

Coordinate representation

The downstairs map $\hat F$ is the only thing we ever compute with.

Definition

$\hat F = \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}$

Target-chart landing

Requiring $F(U)\subseteq$ target chart is exactly what forces a smooth map to be continuous.

Printed pp. 32-49Smooth Maps Between Manifolds
Now there are two translations, one on the source and one on the target. The coordinate representation psi after F after phi inverse must be ordinary smooth. Emphasize the target-chart landing condition: the image of the small neighborhood must sit inside the target chart domain. That requirement is exactly what forces smooth maps to be continuous.

A Concrete Coordinate Square: The Circle

The wrapping map

$\varepsilon(t)=e^{2\pi i t}$ sends the real line onto the unit circle.

Angle chart

Away from a chart cut, an angle chart unwraps a short arc to an interval.

Coordinate expression

$\hat\varepsilon(t)=2\pi t + c$ — linear, hence smooth

Local, point by point

Smoothness is verified near each point, never globally all at once.

Printed pp. 32-49The Circle Example
The exponential parametrization t maps to e to the two pi i t is the cleanest coordinate-square example. Around any point avoiding a chart cut, an angle chart unwraps a short arc and the coordinate expression is simply linear in t, hence smooth. Use it to show that smoothness is checked locally, point by point, never all at once.

Local Tests and Their Traps

Trap 1: hidden discontinuity

A step function looks locally constant on each side of its jump, but it is not continuous — the target-chart condition forbids it.

Trap 2: the cusp

$|x|$ is smooth on each closed half-line, yet the pieces meet in a non-smooth corner.

Printed pp. 32-49Failure Modes
Two honest warnings. A step function looks locally constant on each side of its jump, yet it is not continuous, so it is not smooth; the target-chart condition rules it out. And absolute value, smooth on each closed half-line, develops a cusp where the pieces meet. Locality is real, but continuity and overlap agreement do the bookkeeping.

Diffeomorphisms: Smooth Sameness

Definition

A smooth bijection whose inverse is also smooth. Diffeomorphic manifolds are "the same" smooth manifold.

Radial example

$F(x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-|x|^2}}$ maps the open ball onto all of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Smooth inverse

$G(y)=\dfrac{y}{\sqrt{1+|y|^2}},\quad G\circ F=\mathrm{id}$

Invariants

Diffeomorphisms preserve dimension and boundary; radii near $1$ are flung to infinity, so the boundary is excluded.

Printed pp. 32-49Diffeomorphisms
A diffeomorphism is the smooth notion of sameness: a smooth bijection with smooth inverse. The radial map sending the open ball onto all of Euclidean space is the standard example; radii near one are flung to infinity, which is why the boundary cannot belong to the domain. Diffeomorphic manifolds share dimension and every smooth invariant.

The Algebra $C^\infty(M)$

One object

Collect all smooth real-valued functions on $M$ into a single set.

Algebra structure

Pointwise sum and product make it a commutative $\mathbb{R}$-algebra containing the constants.

Operations

$(f+g)(p)=f(p)+g(p),\ (fg)(p)=f(p)g(p)$

Why it matters

Tangent vectors will reappear as derivations of $C^\infty(M)$ — treat the algebra as first-class now.

Printed pp. 32-49The Algebra C-infinity(M)
Collect all smooth real-valued functions into one object. Pointwise sum and product make it a commutative algebra over the reals, with the constants sitting inside. Much of manifold theory is encoded here: later we recover tangent vectors as derivations of this algebra, so it pays to treat C-infinity of M as a first-class structure now.

Cutoff and Bump Functions

The flat seed

$f(t)=e^{-1/t}$ for $t>0$ and $0$ otherwise — every derivative vanishes at $0$, yet $f>0$ nearby.

Cutoff to bump

From the flat seed build a function that is $1$ on a set and $0$ outside a slightly larger ball.

Printed pp. 32-49Bump and Cutoff Functions
Here is the analytic miracle that makes everything global possible. A function can be flat at a point, every derivative vanishing, yet positive nearby. From that flat seed we build a cutoff that slides from one to zero, and from the cutoff a radial bump equal to one on a set and supported in a slightly larger ball. No analytic function can do this.

Partitions of Unity Subordinate to a Cover

Theorem

Every open cover admits a smooth partition of unity subordinate to it: weights $0\le\rho_\alpha\le1$, locally finite, $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha\subseteq U_\alpha$.

Recipe

Bumps in refined coordinate balls, add, then normalize.

Normalization

$\rho_\alpha=\dfrac{\psi_\alpha}{\sum_\beta \psi_\beta},\qquad \sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \equiv 1$

Local finiteness

Near each point only finitely many $\psi_\beta$ are nonzero, so the sum is smooth and positive.

Printed pp. 32-49Partitions of Unity
State the theorem and the recipe. Given an open cover, there is a locally finite family of smooth weights, each supported in one cover set, all between zero and one, summing to one. The construction is mechanical: bumps inside refined coordinate balls, add them, local finiteness keeps the sum smooth and positive, then divide to normalize.

Proof View: Where the Power Comes From

Each link is necessary

Paracompactness gives local finiteness; coordinate balls give room; positivity makes division legal; reindexing returns to the original cover.

Order matters

Refine first, place bumps, sum, then normalize — the steps cannot be reshuffled.

Printed pp. 32-49Proof Dependencies
Make the logical scaffolding explicit so the proof stops feeling like a trick. Paracompactness supplies a locally finite refinement; regular coordinate balls supply room for bump supports; local finiteness makes the sum smooth; positivity of the total makes division legal; reindexing returns us to the original cover. Each link is necessary and the order matters.

Local-to-Global: Glue, Blend, Extend

Gluing (rigid)

When local maps already agree on overlaps, they assemble into a unique global smooth map.

Blending (flexible)

When they disagree, partition weights average them — provided the codomain supports weighted sums.

Extension lemma

$f\in C^\infty(A)\ \Rightarrow\ \tilde f\in C^\infty(M),\ \tilde f|_A=f$

Payoff

Extend off a closed set, build positive exhaustion functions, realize prescribed zero sets — local data becomes global geometry.

Printed pp. 32-49Extension and Local-to-Global
Close with the payoff. Gluing is rigid, used when local maps already agree on overlaps; blending is flexible, using partition weights when the codomain supports averages. The extension lemma then guarantees any smooth function on a closed set extends smoothly to the whole manifold, vanishing outside a chosen neighborhood. Local data becomes global geometry.
