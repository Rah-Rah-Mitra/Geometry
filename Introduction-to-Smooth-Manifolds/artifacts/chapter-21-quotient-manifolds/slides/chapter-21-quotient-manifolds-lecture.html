When is an orbit space a smooth manifold?

The question

A Lie group $G$ acts on $M$. Is the orbit space $M/G$ smooth?

The two controls

Free tames stabilizers; proper tames the topology.

The payoff

One theorem builds homogeneous spaces, coverings, and quotient Lie groups.

Group Actions, Orbits, Stabilizers

Smooth action

$\theta:G\times M\to M$ is smooth with $e\cdot p=p$ and $(gh)\cdot p=g\cdot(h\cdot p)$.

Orbit

$G\cdot p=\{g\cdot p:g\in G\}$ is the image of the whole group at $p$.

Stabilizer

$G_p=\{g:g\cdot p=p\}$ measures how much of $G$ fixes $p$.

Orbit relation

$p\sim q \iff q=g\cdot p$ for some $g$

Printed pp. 540-563Group Actions, Orbits, Stabilizers
Remind students that an action partitions the manifold into orbits, the level sets of the equivalence relation collapsed by the quotient map. The stabilizer measures how much of the group fixes a point. Emphasize that the orbit through a point is the image of the whole group acting on it.

The Orbit Space $M/G$

Definition

$M/G$ is the set of orbits with the quotient topology; $\pi:M\to M/G$ sends $p\mapsto G\cdot p$.

Always open

For a continuous action $\pi$ is an open map: $\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))=\bigcup_g g\cdot U$.

Not yet enough

Openness gives neither Hausdorff nor locally Euclidean structure.

Saturated sets

$U$ saturated $\iff U=\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))$

Invariants

$\pi$ keeps exactly the data constant along orbits: a function on $M/G$ is an invariant function on $M$.

Printed pp. 540-563The Orbit Space M/G
Stress that the orbit space always exists as a topological space and that the quotient map is always open for a continuous action. The catch is that openness alone guarantees neither the Hausdorff property nor local Euclidean structure, which is exactly why naive quotients can fail.

When Quotients Go Wrong

Fixed point

Rotations of $\mathbb{C}$: the origin is fixed, becoming a singular endpoint class in the half-line quotient.

Non-closed orbits

$\mathbb{R}^\times$ scaling $\mathbb{R}^n$: every nonzero vector is one orbit; $\{0\}$ is another. The quotient is two non-separable points.

Symptom

orbit not closed $\Rightarrow$ $M/G$ not Hausdorff

Printed pp. 540-563When Quotients Go Wrong
Walk through the gallery of failures. Rotation has a fixed origin that becomes a singular endpoint class; the full linear action glues all nonzero vectors into one orbit producing a non-Hausdorff two-point quotient. Each failed hypothesis leaves a visible geometric scar in the orbit space.

Free Actions

Definition

The action is free if every stabilizer is trivial: $g\cdot p=p$ forces $g=e$.

Dimension payoff

Each orbit is an injective immersion of $G$, so all orbits have $\dim G$.

Scope

Freeness controls isotropy only — it says nothing about separation of orbits.

Equivalent form

$G_p=\{e\}$ for every $p\in M$

Example

$\mathbb{Z}^2$ translating $\mathbb{R}^2$ is free: only $(0,0)$ fixes any point.

Printed pp. 540-563Free Actions
Define freeness as triviality of every stabilizer: only the identity can fix any point. Explain the dimensional payoff, that a free action makes every orbit an immersed copy of the group, so orbits all share the dimension of G. Freeness controls isotropy but says nothing yet about topology.

Proper Actions

Action map

$\Theta:G\times M\to M\times M,\ (g,p)\mapsto (g\cdot p,\,p)$.

Definition

The action is proper if $\Theta$ is a proper map: preimages of compact sets are compact.

Sequence test

$p_i\to p,\ g_i\cdot p_i\to q \ \Rightarrow\ (g_i)$ has a convergent subsequence

Consequence

Properness forces the orbit relation closed and separates orbit classes — the missing topological control.

Printed pp. 540-563Proper Actions
Introduce properness through the action map sending a pair to the moved point and the original point; the action is proper when this map is proper, meaning preimages of compact sets are compact. Give the sequence test as the working criterion students will actually apply in examples.

Properness Stops Escape

Dilation on $\mathbb{R}$

$g\cdot x = gx$ with $g>0$. Take $p_i=\tfrac1i$ and $g_i=i$.

Both converge

$p_i\to 0$ and $g_i\cdot p_i = 1 \to 1$, yet $g_i=i\to\infty$ escapes.

Verdict

Sequence test fails $\Rightarrow$ the action is not proper, and the quotient is non-Hausdorff.

Printed pp. 540-563Properness Stops Escape
Use the dilation example to make properness concrete. Take points one over i and group elements i; both the points and their images converge to zero, yet the group elements escape to infinity. This failure of properness is exactly what lets a non-Hausdorff quotient sneak in.

Free Does Not Mean Proper

Irrational flow

$t\cdot(x,y)=(x+t,\,y+\alpha t)\bmod 1$ on the torus $\mathbb{T}^2$, with $\alpha$ irrational.

Free

No nonzero time returns both coordinates exactly, so every stabilizer is trivial.

Not proper

Each orbit is dense; only $\varnothing$ and $\mathbb{T}^2$ are saturated open, so $M/G$ cannot separate points.

Printed pp. 540-563Free Is Not Proper
The irrational torus flow is the cautionary tale: it is free because no nonzero time returns a point to itself, yet every orbit is dense. The only saturated open sets are empty or everything, so the quotient cannot separate points. Free without proper is not enough.

The Quotient Manifold Theorem

Theorem

If a Lie group $G$ acts smoothly, freely, and properly on a smooth manifold $M$, then $M/G$ is a topological manifold of dimension $\dim M-\dim G$, and it has a unique smooth structure for which $\pi:M\to M/G$ is a smooth submersion.

Uniqueness

The smooth structure is forced, not chosen — it is canonical.

Free

$\Rightarrow$ orbits have constant dimension $\dim G$.

Proper

$\Rightarrow$ orbits are closed, embedded, and separated.

Submersion

$d\pi_p$ is surjective everywhere; orbits are the fibers.

Printed pp. 540-563The Quotient Manifold Theorem
State the central theorem carefully. A smooth, free, and proper Lie group action yields an orbit space that is a topological manifold with a unique smooth structure making the quotient map a smooth submersion. Underline the uniqueness clause, which makes the smooth structure canonical rather than a choice.

Adapted Charts: The Local Model

Proof engine

Near $p$ choose coordinates $(x,y)$: $x$ runs along the orbit, $y$ is transverse.

One hit

Properness shrinks the chart so each orbit meets the slice $\{x=0\}$ exactly once.

Free

keeps the limiting group element from fixing the base point.

Quotient map reads

$\pi:(x,y)\mapsto y,\quad \operatorname{rank} d\pi = \dim M-\dim G$

Printed pp. 540-563Adapted Charts
Sketch the proof engine. Properness shrinks a transverse slice so each orbit meets it once; freeness keeps the limiting group element from fixing the base. In the resulting adapted chart the quotient map reads as projection onto the slice coordinates, giving rank equal to dim M minus dim G.

Descending Maps to the Quotient

Characteristic property

If $\pi$ is a surjective submersion and $F:M\to N$ is smooth and constant on orbits, then $F$ descends to a unique smooth $\tilde F:M/G\to N$ with $\tilde F\circ\pi=F$.

Why it matters

We define smooth maps on $M/G$ without building charts by hand — invariance is enough.

Printed pp. 540-563Descending Maps to the Quotient
Explain the characteristic property of submersions: a map out of M that is constant on orbits descends to a unique smooth map on the quotient. This is the everyday tool that lets us define smooth functions and maps on orbit spaces without building charts by hand each time.

Homogeneous Spaces $G/H$

Transitive action

If $G$ acts transitively on $M$, pick $p$; then $M\cong G/G_p$ as smooth manifolds.

Closed subgroup

For closed $H\le G$, the coset space $G/H$ is a smooth manifold and $G\to G/H$ a submersion.

Examples

$S^n=SO(n{+}1)/SO(n)$, plus Grassmannians and $\mathbb{RP}^n$.

Printed pp. 540-563Homogeneous Spaces G/H
Show that a transitive action realizes a manifold as cosets of the isotropy subgroup of a base point. The sphere as SO(n+1) over SO(n) is the model: spinning the frame about the north pole changes the matrix but not its image, so the quotient forgets exactly the isotropy directions.

Coverings and Quotient Lie Groups

Discrete actions

A discrete group acting freely and properly gives a normal covering map $\pi:M\to M/G$.

Model

$\mathbb{Z}^2\curvearrowright\mathbb{R}^2$ has quotient the torus; $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}=S^1$ via $t\mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$.

Quotient Lie groups

Closed normal $K\trianglelefteq G$ makes $G/K$ a Lie group; discrete kernel $\Rightarrow$ covering homomorphism.

One mechanism

free + proper $\Rightarrow$ smooth $M/G$, submersion $\pi$

Printed pp. 540-563Coverings and Lie Quotients
Close by collecting applications. Discrete free proper actions make quotient maps into normal covering maps, with the lattice quotient of the plane giving the torus. The line over the integers gives the circle, and closed normal subgroups yield quotient Lie groups, all from one mechanism.
