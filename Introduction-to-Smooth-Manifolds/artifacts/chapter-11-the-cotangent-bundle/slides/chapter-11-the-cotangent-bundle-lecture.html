Covectors and the Dual Space

Definition

A tangent covector at $p$ is a linear map $\omega:T_pM\to\mathbb{R}$.

Cotangent space

$T_p^*M$ is the dual vector space; it has the same dimension $n$ as $T_pM$.

No metric required

A covector eats a vector and returns a scalar — no inner product needed.

Cotangent space

$$T_p^*M = \operatorname{Hom}(T_pM,\mathbb{R})$$

Pairing

$$\langle \omega, v\rangle = \omega(v)\in\mathbb{R}$$

Picture

Parallel level sets the vector crosses, not an arrow.

Printed pp. 272-303Covectors and the Dual Space
A covector at a point is just a linear functional on the tangent space, so the cotangent space is the dual vector space. Stress that no metric is needed: a covector eats a vector and returns a number. Picture it as a stack of parallel level hyperplanes that a vector pierces.

The Coordinate Coframe

Dual basis

Each chart $(x^i)$ gives covectors $dx^1,\dots,dx^n$ dual to the coordinate vectors.

Duality relation

$dx^i\big(\partial/\partial x^j\big)=\delta^i_j$.

Components

Pair against $\partial_j$ to read off each component $\omega_j$.

Expansion in the coframe

$$\omega_p = \omega_i\, dx^i\big|_p,\qquad \omega_i=\omega_p\!\left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right)$$

Printed pp. 272-303The Coordinate Coframe
Each chart gives a basis of covectors, the differentials of the coordinate functions. They are dual to the coordinate vector fields: dx-i applied to partial-j is the Kronecker delta. Any covector expands in this coframe, and its components are read off by pairing against the coordinate vectors.

The Pairing: Counting Crossings

Why crossings?

Level sets of $\omega$ are evenly spaced; a vector twice as long crosses twice as many.

Linearity, visually

$$\omega(av+bw)=a\,\omega(v)+b\,\omega(w)$$

Different species

Vectors are arrows; covectors are rulings. The pairing marries them.

Printed pp. 272-303Pairing as Counting Crossings
The most useful mental image: a covector is a ruled stack of hyperplanes, and its value on a vector counts how many sheets that vector crosses. This makes linearity obvious and shows why covectors and vectors are different species. Walk through the dual coframe acting on coordinate vectors here.

How Components Transform

Covariant transform

Covector components carry the Jacobian transpose — contravariant to vector components.

Polar example

On the right half-plane, $\dfrac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}=d\theta$ locally.

Coframe change of basis

$$d\tilde x^j=\frac{\partial \tilde x^j}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i$$

Pairing is invariant

$$\tilde\omega_j\,\tilde v^j=\omega_i\,v^i$$

Inverse transpose

The two rules are inverse-transpose, so the scalar pairing cancels coordinates.

Printed pp. 272-303Change of Coordinates
Covector components transform by the Jacobian transpose, contravariantly to vector components. Derive dx-tilde from the chain rule on coordinate functions. Emphasize that the pairing of a covector with a vector is coordinate independent: the two transformation rules are inverse-transpose and cancel exactly.

The Cotangent Bundle $T^*M$

Total space

$T^*M=\bigsqcup_{p\in M}T_p^*M$ is a smooth rank-$n$ vector bundle.

Trivializations

Coordinate coframes give local trivializations on each chart.

One-forms

A smooth covector field is a smooth section $\omega:M\to T^*M$.

Dual transition functions

$$\tau_{\alpha\beta}=(g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$$

Printed pp. 272-303The Cotangent Bundle T*M
Assembling all cotangent spaces gives a smooth rank-n vector bundle, the cotangent bundle. Its local trivializations come from coordinate coframes, and the transition functions are the inverse-transpose duals of the tangent bundle's. A smooth covector field, or one-form, is a smooth section of this bundle.

The Differential of a Function

Definition

For $f\in C^\infty(M)$, $df$ is the one-form with $df(X)=Xf$.

In coordinates

$df=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i$ — the row of partials.

Coordinate-free

No chart needed: apply $X$ to $f$ as a derivation.

Differential as a covector field

$$df_p(v)=v f,\qquad df=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i$$

Consistency check

$$d(x^i)=dx^i$$

Not a gradient

$df$ is a covector; a metric is needed to make it a vector.

Printed pp. 272-303The Differential df
For a smooth real function, the differential df is the one-form whose value on a vector is that vector applied as a derivation: df(X) equals Xf. In coordinates df is the row of partial derivatives. This is the coordinate-free first derivative, living naturally as a covector, not a vector.

$df$ and Level Sets

Linear part

$df_p(hv)$ is the first-order part of $f(p+hv)-f(p)$.

Quadratic residual

$$f(p+hv)-f(p)=h\,df_p(v)+O(h^2)$$

Kernel

$\ker df_p$ is the tangent space to the level set of $f$ at $p$.

Printed pp. 272-303df as Best Linear Approximation
The differential is the linear part of the change in f. Its kernel is the tangent plane to the level set through the point, and the level hyperplanes of df are level sets of f to first order. The residual of the linear approximation decays quadratically with step size, as the notebook checks.

Pullback of Covector Fields

Always works

Unlike vector fields, covector fields pull back along any smooth $F:M\to N$.

Dualizing $dF$

$(F^*\omega)_p(v)=\omega_{F(p)}\big(dF_p(v)\big)$.

Restriction

Inclusion $\iota:S\hookrightarrow M$ restricts forms by testing $T_pS$ only.

Naturality of the differential

$$F^*(dh)=d(h\circ F)$$

Printed pp. 272-303Pullback of Covectors
Unlike vector fields, covector fields always pull back along any smooth map. The pullback dualizes the differential of the map, sending covectors on the target back to covectors on the source. The fundamental identity is that the pullback of dh equals d of h composed with the map. Restriction to submanifolds is a special case.

Line Integrals of One-Forms

Definition

Pull $\omega$ back to the interval, then integrate the resulting function of $t$.

Reparametrization

Invariant under orientation-preserving reparametrizations.

Line integral along a curve

$$\int_\gamma \omega=\int_a^b \omega_{\gamma(t)}\big(\gamma'(t)\big)\,dt$$

Printed pp. 272-303Line Integrals of One-Forms
A line integral of a one-form along a curve is ordinary integration after pulling the form back to the parameter interval. It is reparametrization invariant up to orientation. This is the natural pairing of a one-form against a curve, and it is the bridge to the conservative-versus-exact discussion.

Conservative and Exact Forms

Conservative

Line integral depends only on endpoints; equivalently every loop integral is zero.

Exact

$\omega=df$ for some smooth potential $f$.

Equivalence

On a connected manifold, conservative $\iff$ exact.

Fundamental theorem for line integrals

$$\int_\gamma df = f(\gamma(b))-f(\gamma(a))$$

Building a potential

$$f(q)=\int_{p_0}^{q}\omega$$

Direction

Exact $\Rightarrow$ conservative; integrate a conservative form to recover $f$.

Printed pp. 272-303Conservative and Exact Forms
A one-form is conservative when its line integral depends only on endpoints, equivalently when every loop integral vanishes. Exact means it is the differential of a function. The fundamental theorem for line integrals shows exact implies conservative, and integrating from a base point recovers a potential for any conservative form.

Closed Versus Exact

Closed

A local derivative test. In the plane, $P\,dx+Q\,dy$ is closed when $\partial_x Q=\partial_y P$.

Exact $\Rightarrow$ closed

$d(df)=0$ because mixed partials commute.

Converse fails

Topology can obstruct closed $\Rightarrow$ exact.

Closedness condition (plane)

$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=0$$

Poincare lemma

$$\text{star-shaped}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{closed}\Rightarrow\text{exact}$$

Local vs global

Closedness is local; exactness sees the whole domain.

Printed pp. 272-303Closed Versus Exact
Closed is the local derivative test: in the plane, P dx plus Q dy is closed when the mixed partials match. Every exact form is closed. The converse fails globally: the angular form on the punctured plane is closed but has loop integral two pi, so topology obstructs exactness. Poincare's lemma rescues us on star-shaped domains.

The Angular Form on the Punctured Plane

Angular one-form

$$\omega=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}$$

Winding obstruction

$$\oint_{\gamma_k}\omega = 2\pi k$$

First cohomology

The integer $k$ detects the missing point — a preview of de Rham theory.

Printed pp. 272-303The Angular Form and Winding
The angular one-form is the canonical closed-but-not-exact example. Around a loop winding k times about the origin its integral is two pi k. That single integer is the obstruction: it measures the hole the punctured plane carries. This is the first taste of de Rham cohomology to come.
