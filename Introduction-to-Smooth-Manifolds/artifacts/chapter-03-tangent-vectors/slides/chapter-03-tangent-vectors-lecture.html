Chapter 3: Tangent Vectors

What does a vector mean on a manifold with no ambient space? We build the tangent space three ways, identify its coordinate basis, push it forward through smooth maps, and assemble it into the tangent bundle. Source span: printed pages 50-76.

Chapter 3Tangent Vectors
Frame the chapter as answering a single embarrassing question: in calculus a vector at a point is a little arrow, but an arrow points somewhere in the ambient space, and an abstract manifold has no ambient space to point into. The resolution is to stop thinking of vectors as arrows and start thinking of them as objects that differentiate functions. By the end of the lecture students should hold three equivalent pictures at once and be able to switch between them depending on what is easiest to compute.

Three Equivalent Views of a Tangent Vector

Velocity of a Curve

$\dot\gamma(0)$

A tangent vector is the velocity at $t=0$ of some smooth curve $\gamma$ through $p$. Concrete and visual.

Derivation

$v(fg)=v(f)g(p)+f(p)v(g)$

A linear operator on smooth functions that obeys the product rule at $p$. Needs no ambient space.

Equivalence Class

$[\gamma]\sim[\sigma]$

Curves agreeing to first order in every chart. Geometric, chart-independent once verified.

The Punch Line

On a smooth manifold these three constructions produce the same vector space $T_pM$. We work mainly with the derivation definition because it makes the differential coordinate-free, then translate to curves and components when we compute.

One object, three languagesSlide 2 / 10
Lay out all three definitions side by side before committing to one. The velocity-of-curves picture is the most intuitive and is where most students start. The derivation picture is the most powerful because it never mentions an ambient space and makes the differential fall out of the definition. The equivalence-class picture is the bridge: it is geometric like curves but already quotiented to first order. Stress that proving these agree is real work done in the source, and that we will exploit whichever description is cheapest for a given task.

Tangent Vectors as Derivations at a Point

Definition

A derivation at $p$ is a linear map $v:C^\infty(M)\to\mathbb{R}$ with

$v(fg)=f(p)\,v(g)+g(p)\,v(f).$

Consequence

Derivations kill constants: $v(c)=0$. The arrow does not move the point; it differentiates functions there.

Euclidean model

$D_v f = \sum_i v^i\,\partial_i f(a)$

Product rule replaces the arrowSlide 3 / 10
This is the technical heart of the chapter. Walk through why the product rule is exactly the property that captures differentiation: it forces linearity over evaluation at the point and immediately implies derivations annihilate constants, since v of one equals v of one-times-one equals two v of one. Emphasize the picture: the contours are level sets of a test function, and v reports the instantaneous rate of change of that function. The Euclidean formula on the right is the prototype every coordinate computation reduces to. In Euclidean space one proves these directional-derivative operators are all of them, which is what licenses the abstract definition.

The Tangent Space $T_pM$ and Its Coordinate Basis

Vector Space Structure

The derivations at $p$ form a real vector space $T_pM$ with $\dim T_pM = \dim M = n$.

Coordinate Basis

A chart $(U,x)$ gives the basis

$\left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right|_p,\quad i=1,\dots,n.$

Expansion

$v=\sum_i v^i\left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right|_p,\quad v^i=v(x^i).$

A chart turns abstract derivations into componentsSlide 4 / 10
Now collect the derivations into a vector space and pin down its dimension equals the dimension of the manifold, which is the payoff theorem of the section. The coordinate vectors are the derivations that take a function, express it in the chart, and differentiate with respect to the i-th coordinate slot. Make the recovery formula explicit: the i-th component of a vector is just the vector applied to the i-th coordinate function. The surface picture is an honest model only because the embedded chart partials genuinely lie in the tangent plane and are perpendicular to the normal; warn that the normal direction is not part of the intrinsic tangent space.

The Differential $df_p$: Pushing Vectors Forward

Definition

For smooth $F:M\to N$, the differential at $p$ is the linear map $df_p:T_pM\to T_{F(p)}N$,

$df_p(v)(h)=v(h\circ F).$

In coordinates

The matrix of $df_p$ is the Jacobian $J_F(p)$ of partial derivatives.

Why it works

Pulling $h$ back along $F$, then differentiating, is automatically a derivation at $F(p)$.

The Jacobian is the differential in coordinatesSlide 5 / 10
This slide pays off the derivation definition. The differential is defined with no coordinates at all: to apply the pushed-forward vector to a function on N, pull that function back along F and let the original vector differentiate it. Check that the result satisfies the product rule, so it really is a tangent vector at F of p. Then reveal that once charts are chosen on both ends, this abstract map is exactly the Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives. The single formula df_p(v)(h)=v(h composed with F) is the one students should memorize; everything coordinate-based is bookkeeping on top of it.

The Tangent Bundle $TM$ as a Smooth Manifold

Definition

$TM=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{p\in M}T_pM$

All tangent vectors at all points, with charts $(x^i, v^i)$ built from base charts.

Smooth structure

$TM$ is a smooth $2n$-manifold; the projection $\pi:TM\to M$, $\pi(p,v)=p$, is smooth.

Warning label

Locally $TM\cong U\times\mathbb{R}^n$, but globally there need not be a canonical product splitting.

Local product, no canonical global splittingSlide 6 / 10
Assemble all the tangent spaces into one object. The key construction is the chart: a point of TM is recorded by the base coordinates plus the component coordinates of the vector, giving 2n numbers, and these natural charts make TM a smooth manifold of dimension 2n. The projection forgets the velocity coordinate. Stress the warning: every fiber bundle looks like a product locally, but the global twisting can be nontrivial, which is exactly why bundle language exists. The circle is a clean example because each fiber is one-dimensional, so a bundle point is just an angle plus a velocity coefficient.

Velocity Vectors of Curves

Velocity as derivation

$\gamma'(0)\,f=\dfrac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}(f\circ\gamma)$

Realization

Every $v\in T_pM$ is $\gamma'(0)$ for some curve; in a chart take $\gamma(t)=x^{-1}(x(p)+tv)$.

Boundary subtlety

On a manifold with boundary, two-sided curves force the normal component to vanish; one-sided curves recover inward/outward vectors.

Curves realize every tangent vectorSlide 7 / 10
Close the loop back to the velocity-of-curves definition from slide two. A curve differentiates test functions by composition, and this operator is a derivation, so it is a tangent vector. The realization theorem is the converse and is constructive: pick the straight line in a chart and pull it back, and its velocity is the prescribed vector. The boundary discussion is the genuinely subtle part of this section: if you insist on an honest open two-sided interval the curve cannot leave the half-space, so the outward normal component is unreachable; allowing a parameter endpoint with one-sided derivatives restores those vectors.

Computations in Coordinates: Change of Basis

$\left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial \tilde x^j}\right|_p=\sum_i\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial \tilde x^j}(p)\left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right|_p,\qquad \tilde v^j=\sum_i\dfrac{\partial \tilde x^j}{\partial x^i}(p)\,v^i$

Transition Jacobian

Bases transform by the Jacobian of the chart transition; components transform by its inverse.

Polar example

The angular basis vector $\partial/\partial\theta$ has length $r$; the chart degenerates at $r=0$.

Common mistake

Copying the ordered pair of components into a new chart is wrong: components are coordinates, and coordinates have transition laws.

Components are chart-dependentSlide 8 / 10
This is the operational slide that students must drill. The coordinate basis vectors transform by the transition Jacobian, and the components transform by the inverse Jacobian, so that the abstract vector itself stays fixed; the two transformation laws are paired compensation. Polar coordinates make the stakes concrete: the angular basis vector is not a unit vector, its length grows with radius, and the chart genuinely breaks at the origin. End on the named misconception from the chapter lab: reusing the same component pair across charts is the single most common error, and it ignores that components are coordinates with their own transformation rule.

Preview: The Cotangent Space $T_p^*M$

Dual space

$T_p^*M=(T_pM)^*$

The covectors at $p$: linear maps $T_pM\to\mathbb{R}$.

Dual basis

$dx^i\!\left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\right)=\delta^i_j$

Differential of a function

For $f\in C^\infty(M)$, $df_p\in T_p^*M$ acts by $df_p(v)=v(f)$. This launches the next chapter on covectors and 1-forms.

Vectors get pushed forward; covectors get pulled backSlide 9 / 10
Set up the next chapter by dualizing. A covector is a linear functional on the tangent space, drawn here as a stack of level sets that a vector pierces; the value is how many sheets the vector crosses. Introduce the dual coordinate basis dx-i, which is defined by the Kronecker delta pairing against the partial-with-respect-to-x-j basis. The crucial conceptual flip to foreshadow: tangent vectors push forward along smooth maps, while covectors pull back, with the opposite variance. The differential of a scalar function is the first natural covector and connects directly to the pushforward formula we already proved.

Recap: The Grammar of Tangent Vectors

1

Three Views Agree

Velocity of a curve, derivation, and first-order equivalence class all define the same $T_pM$.

2

Charts Give Components

The coordinate basis $\partial/\partial x^i$ makes vectors computable; components obey transition laws.

3

Bundles & Maps

$TM$ packages all fibers; $df_p$ pushes vectors forward and is the Jacobian in coordinates.

Diagnostic Question

Given any tangent-vector computation, ask: which definition is cheapest here, what does the vector do to a test function, and how do its components transform when I switch charts?

Chapter 3 recapSlide 10 / 10
Consolidate the chapter into three durable ideas. First, the three definitions are interchangeable and the skill is choosing the cheapest one for the task at hand. Second, charts are what make tangent vectors computable, but the components they produce are coordinate-dependent and carry a transition law. Third, the global picture is the tangent bundle and the natural maps between tangent spaces are differentials, which are Jacobians in coordinates. Leave students with the diagnostic question, because it is the habit that turns this machinery into fluent computation in later chapters on vector fields, the cotangent bundle, and tensors.
