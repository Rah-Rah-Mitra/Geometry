Open Sets and the Operations They Generate

Topology axioms

$\varnothing,\,X \in \tau;\;\; \bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha \in \tau;\;\; U_1\cap\cdots\cap U_n \in \tau$

Derived operations

Interior $\operatorname{int} A$, closure $\overline A$, boundary $\partial A = \overline A \setminus \operatorname{int} A$.

Closed = complement of open

A set is closed when $X\setminus A$ is open.

Boundary identity

$\partial A = \overline A \cap \overline{X\setminus A}$; a point of $\partial A$ has every neighborhood meeting both $A$ and its complement.

Printed pp. 596-616Open Sets and Derived Operations
Stress that a topology is not a distance but a declared family of open sets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. Interior, closure, exterior, and boundary are all derived from that family. The finite non-Hausdorff example in the notebook makes every one of these operations checkable by hand.

Bases: Generating a Topology Economically

Basis

A family $\mathcal B$ whose unions are exactly the open sets.

Open set from a basis

$U \in \tau \iff U = \bigcup_{i} B_i,\quad B_i \in \mathcal B$

Standard example

Open balls $B_r(x)$ are a basis for the metric topology on $\mathbb R^n$.

Why it matters

A countable basis is second countability, a manifold axiom met on slide 7.

Printed pp. 596-616Bases and Generating a Topology
A basis lets us specify a topology economically: name a few open sets and let unions do the rest. Second countability, one of the manifold axioms, is precisely the demand that some basis be countable. Remind students that metric balls form the standard basis of Euclidean space.

Continuity Is a Backward Test

Definition

$f:X\to Y$ continuous $\iff$ $f^{-1}(V)$ open for every open $V\subseteq Y$

Sierpiński test

$f:X\to\{0,1\}$ with $\{1\}$ open is continuous iff $\{x: f(x)=1\}$ is open.

Printed pp. 596-616Continuity by Preimages
This is the conceptual pivot of the appendix. Continuity is a backward test: preimages of open sets are open. The Sierpinski example shows a map into two points is continuous exactly when the level set of the open point is open, which is why inverse images, not formulas, are the native language here.

Homeomorphism vs. Mere Continuous Bijection

Definition

$f$ homeomorphism $\iff$ $f$ bijective, $f$ and $f^{-1}$ both continuous

Topological equality

Homeomorphic spaces share every topological property.

The trap

A continuous bijection need not be a homeomorphism: the inverse can fail to be continuous.

Printed pp. 596-616Homeomorphisms
A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection whose inverse is also continuous; only then are the two spaces topologically identical. Warn loudly that a continuous bijection need not be a homeomorphism. The half-open interval wrapping onto the circle is the canonical counterexample, and it returns when we build quotient manifolds.

Three Constructions, Three Continuity Tests

Subspace

Open sets are $U\cap S$. Test maps into $S$ after the inclusion.

Product

Generated by boxes $U\times V$. A map is continuous iff every coordinate projection is.

Quotient

$\pi:X\to X/{\sim}$. A set $U$ downstairs is open iff $\pi^{-1}(U)$ is open upstairs.

Saturation warning

$U$ open in $X$ alone is not enough — $\pi^{-1}\pi(U)$ must be open

Printed pp. 596-616Subspace, Product, Quotient
Each construction redefines the continuity test. Subspace topology tests after the inclusion, product topology tests every coordinate projection, and quotient topology tests after the projection. Emphasize the quotient subtlety: a set downstairs is open exactly when its saturated preimage is open, so open is not enough unless it is saturated.

The Two Manifold Axioms

Hausdorff (T₂)

$x\neq y \;\Rightarrow\; \exists\,U\ni x,\, V\ni y$ open with $U\cap V=\varnothing$

Payoff

Limits are unique; compact sets are closed.

Second countable

There is a countable basis $\mathcal B$ for $\tau$

Payoff

Partitions of unity, compact exhaustions, embeddability.

Printed pp. 596-616Hausdorff and Second-Countable
These are the two topological conditions baked into the definition of a smooth manifold. Hausdorff separates distinct points by disjoint open sets, killing pathologies like limits that are not unique. Second countability gives a countable basis, enabling partitions of unity and compact exhaustions. Both fail in the deliberately broken finite models.

Compactness: A Finite Certificate

Definition

Every open cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ has a finite subcover $U_{\alpha_1},\dots,U_{\alpha_n}$

Stability

Continuous images of compact sets are compact; finite products stay compact.

Printed pp. 596-616Compactness
Compactness is the finite-certificate principle: every open cover admits a finite subcover. Walk through the picture of an arbitrary cover collapsing to finitely many sets. Note the two stability facts we reuse constantly: continuous images of compact sets are compact, and finite products of compact spaces are compact by the tube lemma.

Connectedness and Path-Connectedness

Connected

No split $X = U\sqcup V$ into disjoint nonempty opens

Path-connected

Every pair joined by a continuous path $\gamma:[0,1]\to X$. This implies connected.

The bridge

Local path-connectedness: components are open and equal path components — the manifold regime.

Printed pp. 596-616Connected and Path-Connected
Connectedness forbids splitting the space into two disjoint nonempty open sets; path-connectedness asks for actual paths. Path-connected implies connected, but not conversely. Local path-connectedness is the bridge: there components are open and equal the path components, which is exactly the regime manifolds live in.

Convergence and the Role of Countability

Convergence

$x_n \to x$ iff every open $U\ni x$ contains all but finitely many $x_n$

Without separation

In the trivial topology a constant sequence converges to every point.

With Hausdorff

Limits are unique — the value the sequence "should" have.

Why countability

First/second countability let sequences replace nets, justifying the sequential arguments used throughout.

Printed pp. 596-616Convergence and Countability
Sequences only fully characterize topology when countability is present. In the trivial topology a constant sequence converges to every point; in a Hausdorff space limits are unique. First and second countability are what let sequential arguments replace net or filter arguments, which is why we lean on them throughout the manifold theory.

A Glimpse: Fundamental Group and Covers

Fundamental group

$\pi_1(X,x_0)$ = path-homotopy classes of loops at $x_0$

Universal cover of $S^1$

$\mathbb R\to S^1,\ \theta\mapsto e^{i\theta}$. A loop lifts to the line; its endpoint shift is the winding number.

Degree-n cover

$S^1\to S^1,\ z\mapsto z^n;\quad \pi_{1*}(\mathbb Z) = n\mathbb Z$

Lifting criterion

A loop of winding $k$ lifts to a closed loop iff $n \mid k$.

Printed pp. 596-616Fundamental Group and Coverings
Homotopy turns loops into algebra. The fundamental group records path-homotopy classes of loops at a basepoint. The universal cover of the circle, the exponential map from the real line, makes winding number an integer lift. The degree-n cover sends a loop to a closed lift exactly when its winding is a multiple of n.

Synthesis: A Checklist for Manifold Steps

1. Open sets or map?

Name the topology, or the map that defines it.

2. Continuity

Verify by inverse images of open sets.

3. Quotients

Are the projected sets saturated?

4. Hypotheses

Hausdorff, second-countable, locally path-connected, locally compact?

5. Surviving invariant

components, compactness, $\pi_1$, or covering sheets

Printed pp. 596-616Synthesis for Manifolds
Pull the threads together into a diagnostic checklist for any later construction: name the open sets or the defining map, test continuity by preimages, check saturation for quotients, verify Hausdorff and second-countable, and ask which invariant survives. This is the lens through which every manifold-building step in the book should be read.
