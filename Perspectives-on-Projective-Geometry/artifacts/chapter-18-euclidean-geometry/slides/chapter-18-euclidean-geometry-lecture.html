The Two Circular Points $I$ and $J$

Complex anchors

$I$ and $J$ are conjugate complex points at infinity, not ordinary real points.

Their job

They mark the two ideal directions shared by every circle after projective completion.

Also called

The absolute points; the conic through them is the absolute conic of the plane.

The circular points

$$I=(1:i:0),\qquad J=(1:-i:0)$$

Why they vanish on circles

$$1^2+i^2=0,\qquad 1^2+(-i)^2=0$$

Caution

Do not look for $I$ or $J$ on a drawn circle; they are invisible anchors.

Printed pp. 329-348The Two Circular Points I and J
Introduce the absolute points. They live on the line at infinity, they are complex conjugates, and they satisfy the imaginary relation that makes every circle vanish on them. Stress that they are anchors, never sampled real points, so students do not look for them on a drawn circle.

Every Circle Passes Through $I$ and $J$

Homogeneous circle equation

$$x^2+y^2+a\,xz+b\,yz+c\,z^2=0$$

Substitute the anchors

At infinity $z=0$, so only $x^2+y^2$ survives. With $x=1,\,y=\pm i$ it vanishes.

Consequence

Both $I$ and $J$ satisfy every circle, regardless of centre $(a,b)$ or radius.

Printed pp. 329-348Every Circle Passes Through I and J
Show the substitution explicitly. A general circle in homogeneous coordinates has the same leading quadratic part, and feeding in either circular point kills the equation because the imaginary unit squares to minus one. This is why all circles share the two absolute points and form a pencil-like family through them.

Circles Are Conics Through $I$ and $J$

Definition

A circle is exactly a real conic constrained to pass through both circular points.

Degree-of-freedom count

$$5\ (\text{conic}) - 2\ (I,J) = 3\ (\text{centre} + \text{radius})$$

Dictionary entry

This is the bridge: projective conic $\leftrightarrow$ Euclidean circle.

Printed pp. 329-348Circles Are Conics Through I and J
Reframe a circle as a conic constrained to pass through the absolute pair. A conic has five degrees of freedom; forcing it through I and J consumes two, leaving three, exactly the centre and radius of a circle. This is the key dictionary entry connecting projective conics to Euclidean circles.

Cocircularity Is a Six-Point Conic Test

Translation

$$A,B,C,D \text{ cocircular} \iff A,B,C,D,I,J \text{ on one conic}$$

Bracket certificate

$$[ACI][BDI][ADJ][BCJ]=[ACJ][BDJ][ADI][BCI]$$

Why it matters

The drawing is suggestive; the bracket residual is the actual proof.

Printed pp. 329-348Cocircularity Is a Six-Point Conic Test
Translate the Euclidean notion that four points lie on a circle into the projective statement that those four points together with I and J lie on one conic. Present the bracket identity as the computable certificate, and emphasize that a drawing is suggestive but the bracket residual is the actual proof.

Perpendicularity Is Harmonic Conjugacy

Setup

Two finite lines meet the ideal line in two direction points $L$ and $M$.

Right-angle criterion

$$(M,L;I,J)=-1$$

Reading

$\{L,M\}$ and $\{I,J\}$ are in harmonic position: perpendicular lines.

Special case

This is Laguerre's formula at a right angle.

Printed pp. 329-348Perpendicularity Is Harmonic Conjugacy
Two finite lines hit the line at infinity in two ideal direction points. The lines are perpendicular exactly when that pair is harmonic with respect to I and J, giving cross-ratio minus one. Make clear this is the right-angle special case of the general angle formula that follows.

Laguerre's Formula for Angle

Laguerre's formula

$$\angle(l,m)=\frac{1}{2i}\,\log\,(M,L;I,J)\ \ (\mathrm{mod}\ \pi)$$

The half and the $i$

They convert a multiplicative cross-ratio into an additive real angle.

Modulo $\pi$

The logarithm is multivalued; unoriented lines are measured mod $\pi$.

Ingredients

  • $l,m$: two finite lines
  • $L,M$: their ideal directions on the line at infinity
  • $I,J$: the circular points

Right-angle check

When $(M,L;I,J)=-1$, $\log(-1)=i\pi$, so $\angle=\tfrac{1}{2i}\,i\pi=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$.

Printed pp. 329-348Laguerre's Formula for Angle
State Laguerre's formula: the angle between two lines is one over two i times the logarithm of the cross-ratio of their ideal directions with the circular points. Explain the half and the i convert a multiplicative cross-ratio into an additive real angle, and why measurement is modulo pi for unoriented lines.

Reading the Angle as a Cross-Ratio

Construction

From the vertex, draw four lines: to $L$, $M$, $I$, and $J$. Their pencil cross-ratio encodes the angle.

One formula, two cases

Cross-ratio $-1$ gives a right angle; the general log gives every angle.

Invariance

Cross-ratio is projective, so the angle reading survives any projectivity fixing $\{I,J\}$.

Printed pp. 329-348Reading the Angle as a Cross-Ratio
Walk through the construction geometrically. From the intersection of the two lines, draw the four lines to L, M, I, and J; the cross-ratio of that pencil encodes the angle. Connect the harmonic right-angle case to the general logarithmic case so students see one formula specializing to the other.

Similarities Preserve the Pair $\{I,J\}$

Orientation-preserving

$$\begin{pmatrix}c&s&a\\-s&c&b\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{fixes } I,J$$

Orientation-reversing

$$\begin{pmatrix}c&s&a\\s&-c&b\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{swaps } I,J$$

Not a similarity

A shear keeps the ideal line but moves $\{I,J\}$: affine, not Euclidean.

Clean characterization

Similarities are precisely the real projectivities that preserve the unordered pair $\{I,J\}$.

Printed pp. 329-348Similarities Preserve the Pair I J
Characterize Euclidean transformations projectively. Orientation-preserving similarities fix each circular point; reflections swap them; either way the unordered pair is preserved. A shear keeps the line at infinity but moves I and J, which is exactly why it is affine but not a similarity.

The Transformation Hierarchy

Projective

Preserves cross-ratio. Largest group.

Affine

Preserves the line at infinity (parallelism).

Similarity

Preserves the pair $\{I,J\}$ (angles, ratios).

Isometry

Adds an absolute unit (distance).

Printed pp. 329-348The Transformation Hierarchy
Lay out the nested hierarchy from projective down to Euclidean motions, keyed by what each level preserves. Projective keeps cross-ratio, affine keeps the line at infinity, similarity keeps the pair I and J, and isometry adds an absolute unit. This makes the taxonomy concrete rather than just a list.

Distance Needs a Reference Segment

The catch

Similarities rescale, so the pair $\{I,J\}$ alone cannot fix absolute distance.

The fix

Choose a reference segment $AB$ with $|AB|=1$ as an absolute unit.

Result

With the unit fixed, length is again written entirely in projective brackets.

Bracket distance formula

$$|PQ|=\sqrt{\dfrac{[PQI][PQJ][AIJ][BIJ]}{[ABI][ABJ][PIJ][QIJ]}}$$

The square root

Records the sign choice; the positive branch is ordinary Euclidean length.

Printed pp. 329-348Distance Needs a Reference Segment
Explain why distance is the one Euclidean quantity the circular pair alone cannot recover, since similarities rescale. Introduce a unit reference segment, then present the bracket distance formula. With the unit fixed, even absolute length is written entirely in projective brackets through I and J.

What Survives Which Transformation

Euclidean motion

Preserves distances and angles. Fixes $\{I,J\}$ and the unit.

Scaling similarity

Preserves angles and ratios, not absolute distance. Still fixes $\{I,J\}$.

Affine shear

Keeps the ideal line, but moves $\{I,J\}$: angles and circles distort.

Recap: The Projective Memory of Euclid

Anchors

$I=(1:i:0)$ and $J=(1:-i:0)$ are the projective memory of Euclidean structure.

Circles

A circle is a conic through $I,J$; cocircularity is a six-point conic test.

Angles

Perpendicularity is harmonic position; Laguerre recovers all angles by cross-ratio.

Maps

Similarities are exactly the projectivities preserving $\{I,J\}$.

One move, all of Euclid

$$\text{Projective plane}+\{I,J\}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{Euclidean geometry}$$

Distance

Needs only one more datum: a reference unit segment.
