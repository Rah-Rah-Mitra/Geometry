Homogeneous Coordinates

A point is a ray, a line is a triple, and the exceptions disappear

One move

Replace the pair $(x,y)$ by a ray through the origin of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

One payoff

Incidence, join, and meet each become a single algebraic operation.

Guiding question

How do three coordinates remove the special cases of plane geometry?

Printed pp. 47-66Homogeneous Coordinates
This chapter, printed pages 47 through 66, rebuilds the plane from three coordinates. The single idea to carry through is that a point is no longer a pair but a ray through the origin in three-space, and that this one move erases the exceptional cases that plague ordinary coordinate geometry.

Points as Rays in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Definition

$\mathbb{RP}^2=\big(\mathbb{R}^3\setminus\{0\}\big)\big/\sim,\quad p\sim\lambda p\ (\lambda\neq0)$

Representative

A nonzero triple $(x:y:w)$ names the point; the colons recall the scaling freedom.

Invariance

$(2:4:2)$ and $(1:2:1)$ are the same point on the same ray.

Printed pp. 47-66Points as Rays
Stress that a homogeneous point is the whole nonzero scalar class, not any one representative. Scaling a triple by a nonzero lambda changes the representative but not the point. The ray picture in three-space is the mental model students should keep returning to all chapter.

The Affine Chart $w=1$

Read a finite point

$w\neq0:\quad (x:y:w)\;\longmapsto\;\left(\dfrac{x}{w},\,\dfrac{y}{w}\right)$

Embedding

The Euclidean plane sits inside $\mathbb{RP}^2$ as the rays piercing $w=1$.

Chart, not plane

A point missing from one chart can be finite in another, e.g. dividing by $x$ instead.

Printed pp. 47-66The Affine Chart
The plane w equals one is just a screen that most rays pierce exactly once. Reading a finite point means dividing by w. Emphasise that the chart is a view of the projective plane, never the whole thing, so different charts reveal different points cleanly.

Points at Infinity: $w=0$

Directions, not places

A representative $(r_x:r_y:0)$ records a direction; its ray lies flat in $w=0$ and never reaches the chart.

Limit

$\big(p+\alpha r\big)\big/\alpha\;\xrightarrow{\ \alpha\to\infty\ }\;(r_x:r_y:0)$

Closure

Adding these directions completes the affine plane into $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

Printed pp. 47-66Points at Infinity
Rays lying flat in the plane w equals zero never reach the chart, so they record directions rather than locations. Walk through the limit: moving farther along p plus alpha r sends the affine point to infinity while the rescaled homogeneous vector converges to the direction triple.

Lines as Triples, Incidence as a Dot Product

Incidence

$P=(x:y:w)$ lies on $\ell=(a:b:c)\iff a x+b y+c w=0$

A line is a covector

$(a:b:c)$ is the normal of the plane through the origin whose rays are the line's points.

Symmetric form

Incidence is exactly $P\cdot\ell=0$ — same scaling freedom on both sides.

Printed pp. 47-66Lines as Triples
A line is also a nonzero triple, read as a plane normal or covector. Incidence is the single symmetric equation a x plus b y plus c w equals zero. Point that this is literally the dot product vanishing, the first hint of the point-line symmetry developed next.

Point–Line Duality

Identical spaces

Point triples and line triples live in the same $\mathbb{RP}^2$; incidence treats them symmetrically.

Dual swap

$\text{point}\leftrightarrow\text{line},\qquad \text{join}\leftrightarrow\text{meet}$

Free theorems

Any statement in point, line, incidence, join, meet has a valid dual.

Printed pp. 47-66Point-Line Duality
Points and lines live in algebraically identical spaces and incidence treats them symmetrically. Therefore any theorem phrased only with point, line, incidence, join, and meet has a valid dual obtained by swapping those words. This is the structural payoff of the homogeneous model, not a coincidence.

Join and Meet by Cross Product

Join of two points

$\ell = P\times Q,\qquad \ell\cdot P=\ell\cdot Q=0$

Meet of two lines

$R = \ell\times m,\qquad R\cdot\ell=R\cdot m=0$

One operation

The cross product, read two ways, builds both constructions.

Printed pp. 47-66Join and Meet
The cross product does double duty. The join of two points is their cross product, a vector orthogonal to both, hence the line through them. Dually the meet of two lines is again a cross product. One operation, read two ways, captures both incidence constructions.

Parallel Lines Meet at Infinity

No special case

The meet formula $\ell\times m$ returns a finite point or a point at infinity automatically.

Parallel test

$\ell\parallel m \iff (\ell\times m)_w = 0$

Worked check

Two affine lines of equal slope cross-product to a triple with $w=0$ — their direction at infinity.

Printed pp. 47-66Parallels Meet at Infinity
Run the worked example: two affine lines that look parallel have a meet whose w coordinate is zero. The same cross-product formula returns a finite intersection or a point at infinity with no special casing. This is the concrete reward for the homogeneous bookkeeping.

Projective Transformations

Definition

$P\;\longmapsto\;MP,\quad M\in GL_3(\mathbb{R})\ \text{up to scale}$

Scale freedom

$M$ and $\lambda M$ act identically — matrices carry the same projective ambiguity as triples.

Four-point rule

Four source and four target points (no three collinear) fix $M$ up to scale.

Printed pp. 47-66Projective Transformations
A projectivity is an invertible three by three matrix taken up to scale, acting on point representatives by left multiplication. Make clear that scalar multiples of the matrix give the same map, mirroring how scalar multiples of a triple give the same point.

How Lines Transform

Contravariant rule

$P\mapsto MP\implies \ell\mapsto M^{-\top}\ell$

Why incidence survives

$(M^{-\top}\ell)\cdot(MP)=\ell^{\top}M^{-1}MP=\ell\cdot P$

Take-away

Points and lines must transform oppositely so that incidence is preserved.

Printed pp. 47-66Lines Transform Contravariantly
To keep incidence true under a map M, lines cannot transform the same way as points. They transform by the inverse transpose, since M-inverse-transpose l dotted with M p equals l dot p. Walk through that one-line algebra; it justifies the contravariant rule completely.

Finite Projective Planes over $\mathrm{GF}(q)$

Same recipe, finite field

$P\cdot\ell\equiv 0 \pmod q,\qquad \#\text{points}=\#\text{lines}=q^2+q+1$

Normalise mod scalars

Canonical triples for both points and lines, exactly as over $\mathbb{R}$.

Combinatorial law

Each line holds $q+1$ points; every two points meet in exactly one line.

Printed pp. 47-66Finite Projective Planes
The same recipe works over a finite field GF of q. Normalise nonzero triples modulo scalars for both points and lines, and declare incidence by the dot product vanishing mod q. The count q squared plus q plus one and the unique-line property fall out automatically.

Recap and Outlook

Three moves

  • Points and lines are rays / triples in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  • Incidence is one dot product $P\cdot\ell=0$.
  • Join and meet are one cross product.

The infinity line is a choice

Declaring which line is $w=0$ decides what "parallel" means.

What it powers

Projective transformations as $3\times3$ matrices, and the conics and cross-ratios studied next.

Printed pp. 47-66Recap and Outlook
Recap the three moves: points and lines are rays, incidence is one dot product, joins and meets are one cross product. Remind students the line at infinity is a choice, not intrinsic, and that this coordinate machinery powers the projective transformations and conics studied next.
