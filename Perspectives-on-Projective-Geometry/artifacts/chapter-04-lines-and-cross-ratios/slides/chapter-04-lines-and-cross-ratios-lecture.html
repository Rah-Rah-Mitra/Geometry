Lines and Cross-Ratios

The fundamental projective invariant, built from determinants

The question

Distance changes under projection. What number does not?

The answer

The cross-ratio $(A,B;C,D)$ of four collinear points: a ratio of ratios that survives every projective map.

The tool

Two-by-two brackets (determinants) of homogeneous representatives.

Printed pp. 67-78Lines and Cross-Ratios
This chapter spans printed pages 67 through 78 and introduces the cross-ratio, the first genuinely projective number we meet. Frame it as the payoff of homogeneous coordinates: a quantity built from determinants that survives every projective map. Promise students that distance and ratio of distances will fail, but this ratio of ratios will not.

Joins and Meets via the Cross Product

Join of two points

$\ell = P \times Q$

Meet of two lines

$X = \ell \times m$

Duality

One operation, two readings. Points and lines obey the same algebra.

Printed pp. 67-78Joins and Meets by Cross Product
Stress that the cross product is the single algebraic engine of the projective plane. The join of two points and the meet of two lines are the same operation, which is the cleanest expression of point-line duality. Have students notice that incidence reduces to a vanishing triple determinant, setting up the bracket algebra ahead.

Putting Coordinates on a Line

Span of two points

Every point on line $PQ$ is $[\lambda P + \mu Q]$; the pair $[\lambda:\mu]$ is itself a point of $\mathbb{RP}^1$.

Two charts

$t=\dfrac{\lambda}{\mu},\qquad u=\dfrac{\mu}{\lambda}=\dfrac{1}{t}$

Point at infinity

The chart $t$ misses exactly $[1:0]$; the reciprocal chart $u$ recovers it.

Printed pp. 67-78Coordinates on a Projective Line
Emphasize that a coordinate on a line is a chart choice, not the point itself. The pair lambda and mu is itself a point of the projective line RP one. Walk through how the affine value t equals lambda over mu loses exactly one point, the point at infinity, recovered by the reciprocal chart u equals one over t.

Defining the Cross-Ratio

Ratio of ratios

$(A,B;C,D)=\dfrac{\;\overline{AC}/\overline{BC}\;}{\;\overline{AD}/\overline{BD}\;}$

As two-by-two brackets

$(A,B;C,D)=\dfrac{[A,C]\,[B,D]}{[A,D]\,[B,C]}$

Order matters

Four ordered points, one number. No metric, no chart, only determinants.

Printed pp. 67-78Defining the Cross-Ratio
Define the cross-ratio carefully as a ratio of two ratios, then rewrite it with two by two brackets. Insist on the order of the four points, because permuting them changes the value. Point out that the bracket form needs no metric and no chart, only determinants of homogeneous representatives, which is why it generalizes the naive distance ratio.

Why the Bracket Form Wins

Scale-free

Replace $A$ by $\sigma A$: each bracket scales by $\sigma$, and all factors cancel.

Handles infinity

If $D=[1:0]$, the signed-length form breaks but the determinant quotient computes straight through.

Bracket

$[u,v]=\det\!\begin{pmatrix}u_1 & v_1\\ u_2 & v_2\end{pmatrix}$

Printed pp. 67-78Why Brackets Beat Distances
Contrast the two formulas side by side. The signed length form is intuitive on a Euclidean line, but it breaks when a point goes to infinity. The bracket form computes straight through infinity and ignores how each representative is scaled. This robustness is the whole reason the cross-ratio, not the simple ratio, is the projective invariant.

Invariance: the Determinant Cancellation

Theorem

$(MA,MB;MC,MD)=(A,B;C,D)$

Proof in one line

$[Mu,Mv]=\det(M)\,[u,v]$

Why it cancels

Each of the four brackets gains the same factor $\det(M)$; the quotient leaves it untouched.

Printed pp. 67-78Invariance Under Projective Maps
Here is the central theorem. A two by two matrix acting on homogeneous coordinates multiplies every bracket by the same determinant of M. Because the cross-ratio is a quotient of products of brackets, that common factor cancels exactly. Walk the algebra slowly; the cancellation is the entire proof and students should see it line by line.

Perspectivities Preserve the Cross-Ratio

Perspectivity

Project $A,B,C,D$ from a center $O$ onto a second line, giving $A',B',C',D'$.

Projectivity

A chain of perspectivities is a projective map, so the value is carried along unchanged.

Result

$(A,B;C,D)=(A',B';C',D')$

Printed pp. 67-78Perspectivities Preserve It
Connect the algebra to geometry. Projecting four collinear points from a center onto a second line is a perspectivity, and chaining perspectivities gives projectivities. Each is realized by a projective matrix, so the cross-ratio is preserved. This is the result a Renaissance painter implicitly used and the reason photographs preserve cross-ratios of collinear features.

The Dual: Cross-Ratio of a Pencil

Four concurrent lines

By duality a pencil of four lines through a point has a cross-ratio $(\ell_1,\ell_2;\ell_3,\ell_4)$.

Read off by a transversal

Any line crossing the pencil cuts four points with the same cross-ratio.

Equality

$(\ell_1,\ell_2;\ell_3,\ell_4)=(A,B;C,D)$

Printed pp. 67-78The Dual Cross-Ratio of Lines
By duality the four concurrent lines through a point carry a cross-ratio equal to that of the four points they cut on any transversal. Explain that this is why a perspectivity preserves the value: the pencil of projecting lines has a fixed cross-ratio, and any line crossing the pencil reads off that same number.

The Harmonic Value $-1$

Harmonic condition

$(A,B;C,D)=-1$

Harmonic conjugates

$C$ and $D$ are harmonic with respect to $A$ and $B$; the relation is symmetric in the two pairs.

Midpoint case

If $D$ is at infinity, $C$ is the Euclidean midpoint of $A$ and $B$.

Printed pp. 67-78The Harmonic Value -1
Single out the value minus one as the most important special case. When the cross-ratio is minus one the pair C and D is harmonic with respect to A and B, and the relation is symmetric between the two pairs. Mention the midpoint and point at infinity example, since harmonicity quietly encodes the Euclidean midpoint inside projective language.

The Complete-Quadrilateral Construction

Given

Three collinear points $A$, $B$, $C$ on a line $g$.

Construct

Build a complete quadrilateral on $A,B,C$; its diagonal structure meets $g$ at the unique fourth harmonic point $D$.

Straightedge only

No measurement is used, yet $(A,B;C,D)=-1$ is forced.

Printed pp. 67-78Constructing the Fourth Harmonic
Walk the complete quadrilateral construction step by step. Given three collinear points, draw a quadrilateral whose diagonal structure meets the line again at the unique fourth harmonic point. Emphasize that this construction uses only a straightedge, needs no measurement, and yet pins down a point defined by the value minus one entirely projectively.

Six Values Under Permutation

From 24 to 6

Permuting the four entries gives only six distinct values, an orbit under two generators.

Generators

$\lambda\mapsto\dfrac{1}{\lambda},\qquad \lambda\mapsto 1-\lambda$

Degeneracy

At $\lambda=-1$ the six values collapse to three: $-1,\ 2,\ \tfrac12$.

Printed pp. 67-78Six Values Under Permutation
Explain that the cross-ratio depends on the order, but not arbitrarily. The twenty four permutations collapse to at most six values generated by lambda going to one over lambda and lambda going to one minus lambda. Show the orbit and note the degenerate coincidences at the harmonic value minus one, where the six values fall to three.

Recap and Outlook

The chain

  • Joins and meets are the cross product.
  • A line carries $\mathbb{RP}^1$ coordinates.
  • The cross-ratio is a quotient of brackets.

The invariance

$\det(M)$ cancels, so projectivities and perspectivities preserve the value.

Special structure

  • Harmonic value $-1$ and its straightedge construction.
  • Six permutation values from two generators.

Outlook

The cross-ratio becomes the measuring instrument for Cayley-Klein distances and the projective route to Euclidean geometry.

Printed pp. 67-78Recap and Outlook
Recap the chain: joins and meets by cross product, coordinates on a line, the bracket definition, invariance by determinant cancellation, perspectivities, the harmonic case, and the six permutation values. Preview that the cross-ratio becomes the measuring tool for the rest of the book, underpinning Cayley-Klein distances and projective constructions of Euclidean structure.
