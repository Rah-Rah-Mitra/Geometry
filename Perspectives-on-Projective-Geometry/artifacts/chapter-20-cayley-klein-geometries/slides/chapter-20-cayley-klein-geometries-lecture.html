Cayley-Klein Geometries

One projective recipe that produces every classical geometry

The idea

Fix an absolute conic. Measure distance and angle by the logarithm of a cross-ratio against it.

The payoff

Euclidean, hyperbolic, elliptic, Minkowski, and Galilean planes are one construction with different absolutes.

The habit

Never insert Euclidean distance by hand. Every metric quantity is recovered from projective data.

The Absolute: Fixed Data for Measurement

Primal form

A symmetric quadratic form $F$, e.g. $x^2+y^2-z^2=0$. A line cuts $F$ in two reference points.

Dual form

A pencil through a point sees two reference tangents from that point to $F$.

Invariance

Only projective intersections are used, so the metric is preserved by projectivities fixing $F$.

Fundamental conic

$$\mathbf{x}^{\top} F\, \mathbf{x} = 0,\qquad F = F^{\top}$$

Distance via Cross-Ratio with the Absolute

Cayley-Klein distance

$$\operatorname{dist}(P,Q)=\tfrac{c}{2}\,\big|\log(P,Q;X,Y)\big|$$

Recipe

Join $P$ and $Q$; intersect the line with $F$ to get $X,Y$; form the cross-ratio; take the logarithm.

Multiplicative to additive

Cross-ratio multiplies along a line; the logarithm turns that into additive distance.

Printed pp. 375-398Distance by Cross-Ratio
State the core distance formula. The joining line of two points meets the absolute in X and Y, and half the constant times the logarithm of the cross-ratio gives the distance. Walk through why multiplicativity of cross-ratio becomes additivity of distance once a logarithm is applied.

Proof Scaffold: An Oriented Scale

Zero distance

$(P,P;X,Y)=1 \Rightarrow \log = 0$

Antisymmetry

$(P,Q;X,Y)\,(Q,P;X,Y)=1$

Additivity

$(P,Q;X,Y)\,(Q,R;X,Y)=(P,R;X,Y)$

What the identities prove

Coincident points have distance zero, swapping reverses sign, and three collinear points add. These are the metric axioms, derived from cross-ratio algebra alone.

Branch caution

The logarithm is multivalued; choose a consistent branch. Elliptic measurements live on the unit circle, so a factor $1/(2i)$ extracts a real value.

Before any model

No geometry is named yet. The absolute supplies only the two reference elements $X,Y$.

One Line, Three Measurement Behaviors

Real distinct $X,Y$

Hyperbolic. Open interval between them; the boundary is infinitely far, equal steps shrink in the drawing.

Complex conjugate $X,Y$

Elliptic. The cross-ratio sits on the unit circle; $\tfrac{1}{2i}\log$ gives a real bounded scale.

Coincident $X=Y$

Parabolic. Raw cross-ratio is $1$; measure relative to a chosen unit, then take the coalescing limit.

Hyperbolic line distance

$d_H(p,q)=-\tfrac12\log(p,q;-1,1)$

Elliptic line distance

$d_E(p,q)=\tfrac{1}{2i}\log(p,q;-i,i)$

Printed pp. 375-398Three Behaviors on One Line
On a fixed line the two absolute points fall into three cases. Real distinct points give a hyperbolic interval with an infinitely far boundary; complex conjugate points give a bounded elliptic scale; a coincident pair gives the parabolic limit requiring a chosen unit. One formula, three root types.

Angle: The Dual Construction

Dual recipe

At a point, take the two tangents from its pencil to $F$. Measure two lines $\ell,m$ by their cross-ratio against those tangents.

Cayley-Klein angle

$$\angle(\ell,m)=\tfrac{c'}{2}\log(\ell,m;t_1,t_2)$$

Laguerre recovered

With the circular points as absolute tangents, this is exactly Laguerre's Euclidean angle formula.

Euclidean angle from $I,J$

$\theta=\tfrac{1}{2i}\log(m_\ell,m_m;-i,i)$

Euclidean Geometry Is Cayley-Klein

Degenerate absolute

Primal: the doubled line at infinity $z^2=0$. Dual: the circular points from $a^2+b^2=0$, directions $-i$ and $i$.

Distance is parabolic

The two absolute points coincide on the line at infinity, so length must be calibrated by a chosen unit.

Angle is elliptic

Measured against $I,J$, exactly Laguerre. So Euclid's angle was a Cayley-Klein measurement all along.

Euclidean geometry is not outside the scheme. Its absolute is the degenerate doubly counted line at infinity carrying the imaginary circular points I and J. Distance becomes parabolic and needs a calibrated unit, while angle is elliptic, measured against I and J exactly as Laguerre prescribed.

A Real Planar Absolute: The Unit Circle

Fundamental conic

$$F:\; x^2+y^2-z^2=0$$

Many behaviors, one plane

  • Secant line → real $X,Y$ → hyperbolic interval.
  • Missing line → complex $X,Y$ → elliptic.
  • Tangent line → $X=Y$ → parabolic limit.
  • Separated $P,Q$ → negative cross-ratio → imaginary part.

The Two Nondegenerate Planes

Real conic → Hyperbolic

The absolute is a real conic. Its interior is the Klein disk; the boundary lies at infinite real distance. This is the interior substructure of type II.

Imaginary conic → Elliptic

The absolute is imaginary, so there is no real boundary. The elliptic plane is modeled by the sphere with antipodal points identified (type I).

Printed pp. 375-398Hyperbolic and Elliptic Models
Contrast the two nondegenerate planes. A real absolute conic gives the hyperbolic Klein disk, whose boundary sits at infinite real distance. An imaginary conic leaves no real boundary and gives the elliptic plane, modeled by a sphere with antipodal points identified. Both come from the same formula.

Synthesis: One Construction, Many Worlds

The reference elements

The absolute conic supplies the two reference points (distance) and two reference tangents (angle) for every measurement.

Log of cross-ratio

Multiplicativity becomes additivity through the logarithm. Hyperbolic, elliptic, parabolic are root-type cases, not separate theories.

Where the classics land

  • Euclidean = type V (parabolic distance, elliptic angle).
  • Hyperbolic = interior of type II.
  • Elliptic = type I.
  • Minkowski / Galilean = degenerate ranks VI, VII.
