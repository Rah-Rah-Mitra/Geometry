The Line at Infinity

One point per direction

Each parallel class gets a single ideal point shared by all its representatives.

One horizon

All ideal points together form one new line, the line at infinity $\ell_\infty$.

Completion

$\mathbb{RP}^2 = \mathbb{R}^2 \,\cup\, \ell_\infty$

Axioms of a Projective Plane

Axiom A1 — join

Two distinct points lie on a unique line.

Axiom A2 — meet

Two distinct lines meet in a unique point.

Axiom A3 — richness

There exist four points, no three collinear.

Just incidence

A plane is a set of points, a set of lines, and a relation $I$ saying which are incident. No metric appears anywhere.

Why A3?

The “four points in general position” axiom forbids degenerate, collapsed structures with too few elements.

Printed pp. 35-46Axioms of a Projective Plane
State the three incidence axioms carefully. A1 and A2 are exact mirror images under swapping point and line. A3 forbids degenerate collapses by demanding four points in general position. Make clear that no metric appears anywhere; the plane is defined purely by an incidence relation between two kinds of object.

No Parallels

Euclid vs. projective

Euclid's parallel postulate is replaced by A2: every two distinct lines meet. Parallel lines simply do not exist.

Lemma 2.1

Two distinct points share at most one line — no double incidence. This small invariant keeps later counting honest.

Meet is total

$\forall\,\ell \neq m:\; |\ell \cap m| = 1$

Printed pp. 35-46No Parallels
Contrast Euclid's parallel postulate with axiom A2: in a projective plane every two distinct lines meet, so parallel lines simply do not exist. Walk through why the join and meet are unique, citing the no-double-incidence lemma that stops two points from sharing two lines and keeps the counting honest.

The Principle of Duality

Swap the roles

Trade the words point ↔ line and join ↔ meet throughout a statement.

Why it works

A1 and A2 are exact duals of each other; A3 is self-dual. So the axiom set survives the swap.

Dual theorem

$\text{Theorem} \;\rightsquigarrow\; \text{Theorem}^{\ast}$

Printed pp. 35-46The Principle of Duality
Introduce duality as the deepest structural symmetry of the subject. Because A1 and A2 swap into each other and A3 is self-dual, any theorem stated in incidence terms yields a valid dual theorem by trading the words point and line, join and meet. Promise that later chapters exploit this to halve their work.

Counting in a Finite Plane

Order $n$

Define the order by the points per line minus one: every line carries $n+1$ points.

Symmetric count

By duality every point lies on $n+1$ lines. The no-double-incidence lemma blocks overcounting.

Totals

$$\#\text{points} = \#\text{lines} = n^2 + n + 1$$

Smallest case

Order $n=2$ gives $2^2+2+1 = 7$ points and $7$ lines — the Fano plane.

Next cases

$n=3 \to 13$, $\; n=4 \to 21$, $\; n=5 \to 31$ points.

Printed pp. 35-46Counting in a Finite Plane
Derive the order-n counting result. Fix a point, count lines through it, count points per line, and the no-double-incidence lemma prevents overcounting. Every line carries n+1 points and every point meets n+1 lines, giving n squared plus n plus one points and equally many lines. Order two yields seven.

Building the Fano Plane

Step 1 — seed

Take the four general-position points that A3 guarantees.

Step 2 — join

Their pairwise joins give $\binom{4}{2}=6$ lines.

Step 3 — meet

Opposite line pairs meet in $3$ new points.

Step 4 — close

A2 forces one final line through those three points.

Result

$4 \to 6 \;\text{lines},\;\; +3 \;\text{points},\;\; +1 \;\text{line}$

Closes at

$$7 \text{ points}, \quad 7 \text{ lines}$$

The construction terminates: no further joins or meets create anything new. This minimality is exactly order two.

Printed pp. 35-46Building the Fano Plane
Show the construction concretely. Start from the four general-position points A3 guarantees; their joins give six lines; opposite line pairs meet in three new points; the meet axiom forces one final line through those three. Counting closes at seven points and seven lines, the smallest projective plane, of order two.

Orders and Existence

Known planes

Planes of order $2,3,4,5,7,8,9,\dots$ all exist. Every known finite plane has prime-power order.

Forbidden order

No plane of order $6$ exists — ruled out by the Bruck-Ryser theorem.

Open frontier

Whether a non-prime-power order is ever possible is famously unsolved.

Printed pp. 35-46Orders and Existence
Survey the landscape of finite planes. Planes of order two, three, four, and five exist; order six does not, by Bruck-Ryser. The existence question for general orders is open and famously hard. Mention that all known finite projective planes have prime-power order, which students should treat as a striking unsolved pattern.

Recap & Outlook

Five takeaways

  • Keep incidence; let measurement become optional.
  • Perspective explains why lines and incidences survive.
  • Ideal points remove parallels onto one line at infinity.
  • Point and line are dual; transpose the table to see it.
  • The Fano plane is the minimal model, order two.
Master formula

$$N = n^2 + n + 1$$
