One Plane, Two Disk Models

Klein disk

Geodesics are straight chords. Angles are distorted, but incidence and parallelism are easy to read.

Poincare disk

Geodesics are circular arcs orthogonal to the boundary. The model is conformal, so angles are true.

Same metric

Both arise from the absolute conic; switching models is just changing coordinates.

Printed pp. 505-524The Absolute Conic and Two Disk Models
Contrast the two faces of the same Cayley-Klein metric. The Klein disk draws geodesics as straight chords but distorts angles; the Poincare disk bends geodesics into orthogonal arcs but keeps angles honest. Both live inside the absolute unit circle, and the model choice is just a choice of convenient coordinates on one hyperbolic plane.

Distance from a Cross-Ratio

Construction

The line through interior $P,Q$ meets the absolute in ideal endpoints $A,B$.

Cayley-Klein distance

$$d(P,Q)=\tfrac12\,\big|\log(A,B;P,Q)\big|$$

Why it works

Cross-ratio is a projective invariant, so the metric is invariant under conic-preserving maps and additive along a line.

Printed pp. 505-524Hyperbolic Distance as a Cross-Ratio
Derive the metric the projective way. A line through two interior points meets the absolute in two ideal endpoints; the cross-ratio of the four points is a projective invariant, and its logarithm, halved, is the hyperbolic distance. Emphasize additivity along a line and the blow-up to infinity as either point slides toward the boundary.

Lines, Ideal Points, Ultraparallels

Intersecting

Chords that cross inside the absolute meet at a finite hyperbolic point.

Asymptotic parallel

Chords sharing one boundary endpoint approach but never meet inside.

Ultraparallel

Disjoint chords have a unique common perpendicular: the polar of their meeting pole.

Printed pp. 505-524Lines, Ideal Points, and Ultraparallels
Classify how two hyperbolic lines can relate. Chords that cross inside are intersecting; chords sharing a boundary endpoint are asymptotic parallels; disjoint chords are ultraparallel. Make the projective punchline explicit: ultraparallel lines have a unique common perpendicular, found as the polar of their pole with respect to the absolute conic.

Angle Sum Less Than $\pi$: the Defect

Defect equals area

$$\text{area}=\pi-\alpha-\beta-\gamma$$

Consequence

No similar-but-larger triangles: angles fix the area, so shape determines size.

Extreme case

An ideal triangle with three boundary vertices has all angles $0$ and the maximal area $\pi$.

Printed pp. 505-524Triangles, Angle Sum, and the Defect
State the signature hyperbolic theorem: a triangle's angles sum to strictly less than pi, and the missing amount, the defect, equals the area in the standard normalization. Walk through why this forbids similar-but-not-congruent triangles and why an ideal triangle with three boundary vertices attains the maximal area pi.

Pythagoras and Thales, Corrected

Hyperbolic Pythagoras

$$\cosh a\,\cosh b=\cosh c$$

Small-length limit

Expanding $\cosh$ and $\sinh$ recovers $a^2+b^2\approx c^2$ only near zero length.

Hyperbolic Thales

In the Klein disk, polarity sends a pencil of lines to a pencil, so the right-angle locus is a conic, not a circle.

Printed pp. 505-524Hyperbolic Pythagoras and Thales
Show that classical theorems survive only in corrected form. Right triangles obey a multiplicative law in cosh rather than an additive square law, and expanding sinh recovers the Euclidean version in the small-length limit. For Thales, polarity in the Klein disk sends a pencil of lines to a pencil, so the right-angle locus traces a conic.

Cycles in the Poincare Disk

Proper circle

Locus equidistant from an interior center; drawn as a Euclidean circle, but its metric center is shifted.

Horocycle

Limit of circles whose center is pushed onto a single ideal point on the absolute.

Hypercycle

The curve at constant distance from a line; a chord-symmetric arc, not a geodesic.

Printed pp. 505-524Cycles: Circles, Horocycles, Hypercycles
Catalogue the constant-curvature curves. A proper circle is the locus equidistant from an interior center; a horocycle is its limit with center pushed to a single ideal point; a hypercycle is the equidistant curve of a line. In the Poincare disk all three look like Euclidean circles or arcs, distinguished only by where the center sits relative to the absolute.

Isometries Preserve the Absolute

Definition

A hyperbolic isometry is a projectivity fixing the absolute conic, i.e. a disk-preserving Mobius map.

Elliptic

One interior fixed point; a rotation.

Parabolic

One boundary fixed point; a limit-rotation.

Hyperbolic

Two boundary fixed points; translation along their axis.

Printed pp. 505-524Isometries as Projective Automorphisms
Define hyperbolic isometries as the projective transformations fixing the absolute conic, equivalently the disk-preserving Mobius maps. Classify them by fixed points: elliptic maps fix one interior point and rotate, parabolic maps fix one boundary point, and hyperbolic maps fix two boundary points and translate along the geodesic joining them.

Regular n-Gons and Tilings

Build from right triangles

A regular $n$-gon with vertex angle $2\pi/m$ is $2n$ congruent triangles with angles $\pi/n$ and $\pi/m$.

Circumradius

$$\sinh^2 c=\frac{1}{\tan^2(\pi/n)\,\tan^2(\pi/m)}-1$$

Tiling exists when

$$\tfrac1n+\tfrac1m<\tfrac12$$

Printed pp. 505-524Regular n-Gons and the Tiling Condition
Turn angle freedom into construction. A regular n-gon with vertex angle two-pi-over-m splits into right triangles with angles pi-over-n and pi-over-m, and the circumradius is real exactly when one-over-n plus one-over-m is below one-half. Equality marks the Euclidean boundary case; strict inequality opens the whole catalogue of hyperbolic tilings.

Symmetry Orbits and Exponential Area

Orbits, not decoration

A tessellation is the orbit of a fundamental cell under disk-preserving Mobius generators.

Isometry, not size

Generators preserve hyperbolic distance even as Euclidean spacing collapses near the absolute.

Area grows like

$$\text{area}(r)=2\pi\,(\cosh r-1)$$

Printed pp. 505-524Symmetry Orbits and Exponential Growth
Read a tessellation as a group orbit, not decoration. Disk-preserving Mobius generators move points by genuine isometries even though Euclidean spacing collapses near the boundary. Emphasize that the area inside radius r grows like the hyperbolic cosine, so orbits and tilings expand exponentially, which is why so many regular tilings exist.

Worked Reading: Measuring a Defect

Step 1

Place three vertices in the Poincare disk; sides are arcs orthogonal to the boundary.

Step 2

Read the conformal Euclidean angle between tangent directions at each vertex.

Step 3

$$\text{area}=\pi-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)$$

Watch

Drag one vertex to the boundary: its angle $\to 0$, area $\to\pi$.

Printed pp. 505-524Worked Reading: Defect of a Disk Triangle
Work a concrete defect computation. Place three vertices in the Poincare disk, draw the orthogonal-arc sides, measure the conformal Euclidean tangent angles, and subtract their sum from pi. The result is both the defect and the area; push one vertex toward the boundary and watch the angle shrink while the area climbs toward the ideal maximum.

Synthesis: One Conic, Many Theorems

Metric structure

Distance is a halved log cross-ratio; the absolute supplies the two ideal endpoints.

Curvature signature

Area equals defect; angle equalities relax into inequalities.

Symmetry

Isometries are conic-preserving projectivities, classified by fixed points.

Looking ahead

The same Cayley-Klein device, with a different absolute, yields elliptic and Euclidean geometry, unifying all three classical planes.

Printed pp. 505-524Synthesis and Takeaways
Close by collecting the throughline: one absolute conic generates distance by cross-ratio, area by defect, parallels by boundary contact, and symmetry by conic-preserving projectivities. Remind students that hyperbolic equalities relax into inequalities, leaving room for infinitely many tilings, and point ahead to the unified Cayley-Klein treatment of all three classical geometries.
