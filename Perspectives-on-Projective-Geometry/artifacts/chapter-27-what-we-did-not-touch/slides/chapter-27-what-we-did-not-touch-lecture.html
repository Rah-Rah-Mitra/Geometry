Projective Geometry Over Finite Fields

Finite, complete, combinatorial

The plane $\mathbb{P}^2(\mathbb{F}_q)$ has $q^2+q+1$ points and the same number of lines.

Smallest example: the Fano plane

$q=2:\quad 7\text{ points},\ 7\text{ lines},\ 3\text{ per line}$

Why it matters

Links to coding theory, combinatorial designs, and packing problems. The cross-ratio survives over any field.

Printed pp. 525-556Finite Fields and Coding
Projective planes over finite fields are tiny, complete, and combinatorial. The Fano plane has seven points and seven lines with three points on each. Connect this to coding theory and designs, and note that the cross-ratio still makes sense over any field. This is the most accessible frontier for a first independent project.

Higher Dimensions and Grassmannians

Beyond points and planes

In $\mathbb{P}^3$, lines are the first genuinely new object. A Grassmannian is the space of such subspaces.

Plucker coordinates

A line through points $p,q$ gets six brackets, one per coordinate pair: $p_{ij}=p_iq_j-p_jq_i$.

Brackets again

The same determinant machinery from the course, now indexing lines instead of triangles.

Printed pp. 525-556Higher Dimensions and Grassmannians
Lines in space are the first object beyond points and planes. Plucker coordinates turn a line into six numbers, one bracket per coordinate pair, satisfying a single quadratic relation. That relation is the Klein quadric, so the set of lines becomes a four-dimensional quadric in five-dimensional projective space. Emphasise that brackets are doing the work.

Teaser: Lines as Points of the Klein Quadric

The Plucker relation

$$p_{01}p_{23}-p_{02}p_{13}+p_{03}p_{12}=0$$

What it says

Not every six-tuple is a line. The valid ones lie on one quadric surface in $\mathbb{P}^5$ — the Klein quadric $\mathcal{K}$.

Incidence as one equation

$$\langle \ell,\ell'\rangle = 0 \iff \ell \text{ meets } \ell'$$

Printed pp. 525-556The Klein Quadric Teaser
This is the concrete teaser. Spell out the Plucker map and the alternating relation that the six coordinates must satisfy. Two lines meet exactly when a bilinear pairing of their coordinates vanishes, which is incidence rewritten as a single equation. Have students verify the relation on the two example lines drawn on the slide.

Algebraic Curves and Bezout's Theorem

Past the conic

A plane curve of degree $d$ is just a homogeneous polynomial equation in $[x:y:z]$.

Bezout

$$\#(C_m \cap C_n) = m\,n$$

counting complex points, points at infinity, and multiplicity.

The catch

A tangent contact is a double root. Multiplicity is not optional bookkeeping.

Printed pp. 525-556Algebraic Curves and Bezout
Past conics, a curve is just a homogeneous polynomial of some degree. Bezout says two curves of degrees m and n meet in exactly m times n points once you count complex points, points at infinity, and multiplicity. Use the line-against-cubic check: three roots generically, a double root at a tangency. Multiplicity is non-negotiable.

Cubics: Nine Inflections, a Group Law, a Dual Curve

Hessian curve

$$H=\det\!\big(\partial_i\partial_j F\big),\quad \deg H = 3d-6$$

Nine inflections

Common zeros of $F$ and $H$. For a smooth cubic that is exactly nine complex points — the seed of Cayley-Bacharach.

Group law

Three collinear points of an elliptic cubic sum to the identity, turning the curve into an abelian group.

Printed pp. 525-556Cubics, Hessian, Duality
The smooth cubic is the richest small example. Its nine inflection points are the common zeros of the curve and its Hessian, and they organise the Cayley-Bacharach phenomenon and the group law on an elliptic curve. The gradient at each point gives a tangent line, and tracing those lines sweeps out the dual curve. Connect duality back to brackets.

Oriented and Discrete Projective Geometry

Keep the sign

Projective geometry forgets the sign of the bracket; oriented matroids remember it.

Chirotope

$$\chi(a,b,c)=\operatorname{sign}\,[abc]\in\{+,0,-\}$$

Constraints & hardness

Grassmann-Plucker relations restrict the signs; deciding realizability is genuinely hard.

Printed pp. 525-556Oriented and Real Projective Geometry
Oriented projective geometry keeps the sign of the determinant that the projective version throws away. An oriented matroid records, for every triple, whether the bracket is positive, negative, or zero. Zeros still mean incidence; signs remember which side. The Grassmann-Plucker relations constrain those signs, and deciding realizability is genuinely hard.

Complex Points, Quantum States, Invariant Theory

The complex points were always there

The absolute conic and the circular points live off the real chart. Make them explicit and physics appears.

A qubit is projective

$$|\psi\rangle \sim \lambda|\psi\rangle \;\Rightarrow\; \text{qubit}=\mathbb{CP}^1,\ \ 2\text{ qubits}=\mathbb{CP}^3$$

Entanglement = invariant

$\det\!\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{psmallmatrix}=0$ on product states; preserved by local unitaries.

Printed pp. 525-556Complex Points and Quantum States
The complex points were always there, hiding the absolute conic and circular points. Made explicit, a qubit is a point of CP1 on the Bloch sphere, two qubits live in CP3, and entanglement is a projective invariant: the determinant of the amplitude matrix vanishes exactly on product states and is preserved by local unitaries. Invariant theory is the doorway.

Tropical and Dynamic Geometry

Tropical semiring

$$a\oplus b=\min(a,b),\quad a\otimes b=a+b$$

Curves become graphs

Polynomials turn into piecewise-linear shapes, yet Bezout-style intersection counts persist.

Dynamic geometry

A construction step is multivalued: line-meets-circle has two solutions; choices depend on motion history.

Printed pp. 525-556Tropical and Dynamic Geometry
Two motion-and-degeneracy frontiers. Tropical geometry replaces plus and times with min and plus, turning curves into piecewise-linear graphs while keeping Bezout-style counts. Dynamic geometry treats construction primitives as multivalued relations; a line meets a circle in two points, and following branches through complex detours avoids the tangency where they collide.
