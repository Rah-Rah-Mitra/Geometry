The Circular Points $I$ and $J$

Definition

$$I=(1,\,i,\,0),\qquad J=(1,\,-i,\,0)$$

Conjugate pair

Complex conjugates lying on the line at infinity $\ell_\infty:\;x_3=0$.

On every circle

Each real circle $x^2+y^2+\dots$ meets $\ell_\infty$ exactly at $I$ and $J$.

Why they matter

An affine map may move the pair; a similarity must fix $\{I,J\}$. That single invariance is the seed of Euclidean structure.

Computational stance

We join, meet, and bracket with $I,J$ exactly as with any real point, accepting complex intermediate coordinates.

Printed pp. 349-374The Circular Points I and J
Define the circular points as the complex intersections of every circle with the line at infinity. Write their coordinates and emphasize they are conjugate. The pair is what an affine transformation may move but a similarity must fix; that invariance is exactly why they carry Euclidean structure. Tell students to treat them as legitimate points over C.

Isotropic Lines and Degenerate Length

Minimal lines

The lines joining a finite point to $I$ or to $J$ are the isotropic (minimal) lines.

Length degenerates

$$\text{dir }(1,\pm i)\;\Rightarrow\;1^2+(\pm i)^2=0$$

Hidden singularity

Squared Euclidean length vanishes along them: the metric collapses inside the absolute.

Printed pp. 349-374Isotropic Lines and Degenerate Length
Introduce isotropic, or minimal, lines as the lines joining a finite point to I or J. Along them the quadratic form measuring squared length vanishes, so Euclidean distance degenerates to zero. This is the metric singularity hidden inside the absolute. Connect it to why these lines are invisible in any real drawing yet decisive algebraically.

Laguerre's Angle Formula

Angle as cross ratio

$$\angle(g,h)=\tfrac{1}{2i}\,\log\,(L,M;I,J)$$

Infinite directions

$L,M$ are where the real lines $g,h$ meet $\ell_\infty$.

Multiplicative $\to$ additive

The logarithm turns a cross ratio into an additive angle; the $\tfrac{1}{2i}$ normalizes it.

Printed pp. 349-374Laguerre's Angle Formula
Present Laguerre's formula as the centerpiece: the angle between two real lines is a logarithm of the cross ratio that their infinite points form with I and J. Walk through why a multiplicative cross ratio becomes an additive angle through the logarithm, and why the factor one over two i normalizes it. This is angle as pure incidence data.

Bracket Construction of Bisectors

From brackets to bisectors

$$\lambda=\sqrt{[P,L,J]\,[P,M,J]},\quad \mu=\sqrt{[P,L,I]\,[P,M,I]}$$

Two directions

$A_\pm=\lambda\,I\pm\mu\,J$ are the infinite points of the internal and external bisectors.

Free fact

The two come out perpendicular automatically, recovering a classical theorem from algebra.

Printed pp. 349-374Bracket Construction of Bisectors
Show the angle bisector emerging directly from brackets involving I and J. The square-root combination of the four brackets gives lambda and mu, and the points lambda I plus or minus mu J are the two infinite directions of the internal and external bisectors. Note they come out automatically perpendicular, recovering a classical fact from pure algebra.

Distance up to a Similarity Factor

Same machinery

Circular-point brackets recover Euclidean length, paralleling the Laguerre treatment of angle.

Only up to scale

$$d(A,B)\;\sim\;\kappa\cdot d_{\text{Eucl}}(A,B)$$

Why a factor remains

Similarities rescale distance and are exactly the maps fixing $\{I,J\}$, so $\kappa$ is undetermined.

Angle vs. distance

Angle is absolutely determined by $(L,M;I,J)$; distance is determined only modulo the scale $\kappa$.

The projective fingerprint

The unfixable factor is precisely the trace of similarity invariance left in the projective picture.

Reading

$$\text{angle}\;\text{exact},\qquad \text{length}\;\bmod\;\text{scale}$$

Printed pp. 349-374Distance up to a Similarity Factor
Explain the analogue for distance: the same circular-point brackets recover Euclidean length, but only up to an overall scale because similarities, which rescale distance, are exactly the maps fixing I and J. Contrast this with angle, which is absolutely determined. The unfixable factor is the projective fingerprint of similarity invariance.

Euclidean Maps Fix the Pair $\{I,J\}$

Group definition

$$\mathrm{Sim}=\{\,\varphi\in PGL_3:\;\varphi\{I,J\}=\{I,J\}\,\}$$

Fix each point

Orientation-preserving similarities: rotations, translations, scalings.

Swap the pair

Adds reflections; conjugation $I\leftrightarrow J$ flips orientation.

Printed pp. 349-374Euclidean Maps Fix the Pair {I,J}
State the group-theoretic punchline: the projectivities that fix the unordered pair I, J are precisely the similarities. Fixing each point separately gives orientation-preserving maps; swapping them adds reflections and conjugation. Translations and rotations sit inside as the subgroup also fixing the line at infinity pointwise structure. This is the cleanest definition of Euclidean motion available.

Circles Pass Through $I$ and $J$

Projective definition

$$\text{conic }Q\text{ is a circle}\iff I,J\in Q$$

Center as a meet

Tangents $QI$ and $QJ$ at the circular points are complex; their meet is the real center.

Real from complex

No real tangent is drawn at $I$ or $J$, yet the construction yields a visible real point.

Printed pp. 349-374Circles Pass Through I and J
Define a circle projectively as any conic through both circular points. Then the center is the meet of the two complex tangents drawn at I and J, a real point even though the tangents are complex. Emphasize the diagram shows the real consequence of a complex construction, and that this characterizes circles among all conics.

Foci from Tangents Through $I$ and $J$

Generalize the center

From each of $I$ and $J$ draw the complex tangents to a conic $C$.

Foci = tangent meets

$$\text{foci}=\big(\text{tangents from }I\big)\cap\big(\text{tangents from }J\big)$$

Two of four are real

For a real ellipse, exactly two of the four pairings are the classical foci.

Printed pp. 349-374Foci from Tangents Through I and J
Generalize the circle center idea to arbitrary conics: draw the complex tangents from I and from J, and their intersections are the foci. For a real ellipse exactly two of the four pairings are real, matching the classical foci. This unifies center and focus as one circular-point construction and explains the reflection law of conics.

Mirror Image as Joins and Meets

Pure incidence

Join $P$ to $I$ and $J$, meet those lines with the mirror, then cross-join with $J$ and $I$.

Reflection recovered

$$P'=(\,bI\,)\wedge(\,aJ\,),\quad a=l_{PI}\wedge m,\;b=l_{PJ}\wedge m$$

Exact check

For mirror $y=0$, SymPy confirms $(x,y,1)\mapsto(x,-y,1)$ exactly.

Printed pp. 349-374Mirror Image as Seven Joins and Meets
Reconstruct reflection projectively: join the point to I and J, meet those lines with the mirror, then cross-join with J and I. Seven incidence operations reproduce the ordinary reflection, with the midpoint landing on the mirror and the segment perpendicular to it. Highlight that a SymPy check confirms the identity exactly for the horizontal mirror.
