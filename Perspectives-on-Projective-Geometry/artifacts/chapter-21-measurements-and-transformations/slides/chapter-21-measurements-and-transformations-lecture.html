The Measurement Problem

No intrinsic metric

Projective space alone has no distances or angles; cross-ratio is the only invariant.

Fix an absolute

Choose one conic, the absolute. Every measurement is read relative to it.

From recipe to formula

The cross-ratio recipe of Chapter 20 becomes a closed coordinate formula here.

Cross-ratio measurement

$\operatorname{dist}(P,Q)=c\,\log(P,Q;X,Y)$

where $X,Y$ are the absolute points on line $PQ$ and $c$ depends on the geometry.

Goal

Replace this with an algebraic expression in $P$, $Q$, and a matrix $A$ only.

The Bilinear Form $A$

Absolute as a matrix

$A$ is the symmetric matrix whose quadratic form vanishes on the absolute: $X^{T}AX=0$.

Three scalars

From two points $P,Q$ the form yields exactly three numbers.

The pairings

$\Omega_{PP}=P^{T}AP,\quad \Omega_{QQ}=Q^{T}AQ$

$\Omega_{PQ}=P^{T}AQ$

Printed pp. 399-422The Bilinear Form A
Introduce the matrix A as the symmetric bilinear form whose zero set is the absolute conic. Stress the three scalars it produces from two points: the self-pairings P-transpose-A-P and Q-transpose-A-Q and the mixed pairing P-transpose-A-Q. These three numbers carry all metric information the chapter needs.

The Distance Formula

Central invariant

$\displaystyle \kappa(P,Q)=\frac{(P^{T}AQ)^{2}}{(P^{T}AP)(Q^{T}AQ)}$

Wrapped into a distance

$\operatorname{dist}(P,Q)=f\!\big(\kappa(P,Q)\big)$

$f$ is an inverse cosine, inverse hyperbolic cosine, or logarithm.

Why a ratio

Scaling $P$ or $Q$ rescales numerator and denominator equally, so $\kappa$ is projectively well defined.

Angles too

The dual form measures angles between lines by the identical recipe applied to line coordinates.

Reading $\kappa$

$\kappa=1$ means the points coincide on the conic in the limiting sense; $f$ converts $\kappa$ to additive length.

Printed pp. 399-422The Distance Formula
State the central formula. The squared mixed pairing divided by the product of the two self-pairings is a projective invariant, and an inverse trigonometric or hyperbolic or logarithmic wrapper turns it into additive distance. Emphasize that the geometry type selects which wrapper appears.

Eliminating the Reference Points

The nuisance

Naive distance needs $X,Y$, the intersections of line $PQ$ with the absolute. Constructing them each time is wasteful.

Discriminant

$\Delta=\Omega_{PQ}^{2}-\Omega_{PP}\,\Omega_{QQ}$

Endpoint cross-ratio, reference-free

$\displaystyle \alpha=\frac{\Omega_{PQ}+\sqrt{\Delta}}{\Omega_{PQ}-\sqrt{\Delta}}$

Printed pp. 399-422Eliminating the Reference Points
Explain that naively the distance needs the two points where the line meets the absolute. The chapter eliminates them. With the discriminant Delta built from the three Omega pairings, the cross-ratio of the endpoints is recovered directly, so X and Y never have to be constructed.

Orientation and the Reciprocal Class

No canonical order

The two absolute points $X,Y$ on a generic line are not intrinsically ordered.

Reversal sends $\alpha\mapsto 1/\alpha$

$(P,Q;X,Y)\cdot(P,Q;Y,X)=1$

The honest datum

The unoriented measurement is the class $\{\alpha,\,1/\alpha\}$, not a single number.

Printed pp. 399-422Orientation and the Reciprocal Class
Warn students that the two absolute intersections have no canonical order. Swapping them sends the cross-ratio alpha to its reciprocal. So the honest projective datum is the unordered pair alpha and one over alpha, and the logarithm only fixes a sign once a direction is chosen.

The $\Psi$ Comparison Formula

Dual-conic measurement

$\displaystyle \Psi(P,Q)=\frac{(P\times Q)^{T}B\,(P\times Q)}{(P^{T}AP)(Q^{T}AQ)}$

Survives degeneration

Where $\Delta$ fails because the primal form is degenerate, $\Psi$ still returns a meaningful comparison.

Euclidean embedding

$A=\mathrm{diag}(0,0,1),\;B=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,0)$

$\Psi$ is exactly $dx^{2}+dy^{2}$.

Pseudo-Euclidean

$B=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,0)\;\Rightarrow\;\Psi=dx^{2}-dy^{2}$

Role

$\Psi$ is the robust comparator used to test that a motion preserves measurement.

Printed pp. 399-422The Psi Comparison Formula
Introduce the dual-conic expression Psi, built from the join of the two points paired through the dual matrix B and normalized by the self-pairings. Psi survives degeneration. For the Euclidean embedding it equals squared Euclidean distance, and a sign flip in B yields the pseudo-Euclidean square.

Isometries Preserve the Absolute

Defining condition

$T^{T}A\,T=\lambda\,A$

$T$ maps the absolute conic onto itself.

Why distances survive

$T$ permutes the conic, so the endpoints $X,Y$ map among themselves, and projective maps preserve cross-ratio.

Looks can deceive

The affine grid may bend dramatically; the absolute and every $\Psi$ value stay fixed.

Printed pp. 399-422Isometries Preserve the Absolute
Define a Cayley-Klein motion as a projective map whose matrix preserves the bilinear form, T-transpose-A-T proportional to A. Such a map sends the absolute conic to itself, hence permutes the line endpoints and preserves every cross-ratio, so all distances and angles are unchanged even when the affine grid distorts.

The Orthogonal Group of $A$

Group structure

The isometries form the projective orthogonal group $\mathrm{PO}(A)$ of the form, closed under composition and inverse.

Specializations

$A=I\;\Rightarrow\;$ elliptic motions

$A=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1)\;\Rightarrow\;$ hyperbolic

$A$ degenerate $\Rightarrow\;$ Euclidean

Hyperbolic case

$\mathrm{PO}(1,1,-1)$ acts on the disk; these are the rigid motions of the Klein model.

Euclidean case

The degenerate absolute on the line at infinity recovers rotations, translations, and reflections of the plane.

Elliptic case

A definite $A$ has no real conic; the orthogonal group becomes the elliptic rotation group.

Printed pp. 399-422The Orthogonal Group of A
Frame the isometry group as the projective orthogonal group of the form A. Choosing A as the identity gives the elliptic motions, a diagonal form with one negative sign gives the hyperbolic motions on the disk, and the degenerate Euclidean absolute recovers the familiar rigid motions of the plane.

Reflections Generate Motions

Reflection from a pole/polar pair

$m=A\,o,\quad o=B\,m$

$T=\langle o,m\rangle\,I-2\,o\,m^{T}$

Involution

$T^{2}$ is a scalar multiple of the identity, and $T^{T}AT\propto A$, so each reflection is a motion.

Two reflections

Their product is again a motion, a rotation, translation, or hyperbolic analogue.

Printed pp. 399-422Reflections Generate Motions
Build motions from reflections. A center and its polar mirror form a pole-polar pair under A, giving an involution that preserves the absolute. Composing two reflections yields a rotation by twice the mirror angle when the mirrors meet inside the disk, and a hyperbolic translation when they meet outside.
