One Machine, Many Geometries

Objects

Points $p$ and lines $l$ are homogeneous vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$; incidence is $p^T l = 0$.

Primal & dual absolute

$p^T A p = 0$ on the absolute conic; $l^T B l = 0$ for its tangents.

Every measurement

A cross-ratio against the two fundamental points or tangents of the absolute.

Distance & angle

$$\mathrm{dist}(p,q)=\tfrac{c}{2}\,\big|\log(p,q;f_1,f_2)\big|$$

$$\angle(l,m)=\tfrac{1}{2i}\,\log(l,m;t_1,t_2)$$

Tune one matrix

Distances read off the primal conic $A$, angles off the dual conic $B$. Replace the matrix — the formulas never change.

Printed pp. 423-442One Machine, Many Geometries
Recall the single recipe. Points and lines are homogeneous vectors, the absolute is a symmetric matrix A with dual B, and every measurement is a cross-ratio against the absolute. Distances use the primal conic, angles use the dual conic. Walk through how this one apparatus specializes by tuning A.

Distance From the Absolute

Cross-ratio distance

$$d(p,q)=\tfrac{c}{2}\log\,(p,q;f_1,f_2)$$

Hyperbolic

$f_1,f_2$ are real points of the disk boundary — finite log, the Klein distance.

Elliptic

$f_1,f_2$ are conjugate imaginary — the log becomes an arc-length angle.

Euclidean limit

The absolute collapses to a repeated line; the cross-ratio degenerates to the ordinary additive length.

Printed pp. 423-442Distance From the Absolute
Derive the distance measurement. The line through two points meets the absolute in two fundamental points; the cross-ratio of the four points, fed through a logarithm, gives distance. Show how the elliptic, hyperbolic and Euclidean constants emerge from the same cross-ratio as the absolute changes.

Orthogonality Is Pole-Polar

The test replaces intuition

Two lines are orthogonal exactly when the dual bilinear form vanishes — no Euclidean appeal needed.

Orthogonality

$$l^T B\,m = 0$$

Pole gives the perpendicular

When $Bl \neq 0$ it is the pole of $l$. The perpendicular to $l$ through $p$ is $\operatorname{join}(p,\,Bl)$.

Printed pp. 423-442Orthogonality Is Pole-Polar
This is the chapter's keystone. A right angle is not Euclidean intuition but the algebraic test l-transpose B m equals zero. When Bl is nonzero it is the pole of l, so the perpendicular to l through p is the join of p with Bl. Stress the constructive flavor.

Altitudes: Constructive vs Implicit

Constructive theorem

Join each vertex to the pole $Bl_i$ of the opposite side. The poles exist, the altitudes concur.

Implicit version

Merely require $l_i^T B\,g_i = 0$. Looks equivalent — but is weaker.

Where it breaks

If $B=qq^T$ is rank one and two sides pass through $q$, the equations constrain nothing; chosen $g_i$ need not concur.

Printed pp. 423-442Altitudes: Constructive vs Implicit
Warn students about two versions of every theorem. The constructive altitude joins a vertex to a genuine pole and concurs reliably. The implicit version only asks for a line satisfying the bilinear equation, and when B drops to rank one those equations stop constraining anything. Degeneracy breaks the implicit theorem.

Same Test, Different Pictures

$l^T B\,m=0$ wears three faces

Euclidean

Perpendicular finite slopes multiply to $-1$ — the familiar quarter-turn.

Pseudo-Euclidean

Orthogonal lines have inverse slopes; reflection is across the diagonal, not a rotation.

Hyperbolic disk

A regular pentagon can be right-angled: adjacent sides satisfy $l_i^T B\,l_{i+1}=0$.

Printed pp. 423-442Same Test, Different Pictures
The one equation l-transpose B m equals zero shows three faces. Euclidean eyes see slopes multiplying to minus one. The pseudo-Euclidean absolute reflects slopes across the diagonal so orthogonal lines have inverse slopes. In the hyperbolic Klein disk a regular pentagon can have five right angles. Same algebra, different geometry.

Midpoints and Bisectors Are Dual

Midpoint pair (points)

$$m_\pm=\sqrt{\Omega_{qq}}\,p \pm \sqrt{\Omega_{pp}}\,q$$

Bisector pair (lines)

$$b_\pm=\sqrt{\Theta_{ll}}\,g \pm \sqrt{\Theta_{gg}}\,l$$

Harmonic invariant

$(p,q;\,m_+,m_-)=-1$; the two bisectors are $B$-orthogonal.

Printed pp. 423-442Midpoints and Bisectors Are Dual
Midpoints and angle bisectors are the same square-root construction, one on points, one on lines. Each delivers a pair, not a single object, and the pair is harmonic with cross-ratio minus one. In the Euclidean limit one midpoint slides to the point at infinity. Highlight the primal-dual symmetry.

Trigonometry as a Projective Identity

Vertex ratio

$\Psi_{PQ}=\dfrac{(P\times Q)^T B (P\times Q)}{(P^TAP)(Q^TAQ)}$

Side ratio

$\Psi^*_{pq}=\dfrac{(p\times q)^T A (p\times q)}{(p^TBp)(q^TBq)}$

Law of sines

$$\frac{\Psi_{PQ}}{\Psi^*_{pq}}=\frac{\Psi_{QR}}{\Psi^*_{qr}}=\frac{\Psi_{RP}}{\Psi^*_{rp}}$$

Printed pp. 423-442Trigonometry as a Projective Identity
The law of sines is one projective statement. Build the Psi and Psi-star ratios from the primal and dual conics, take their quotient on each side, and the three quotients agree. The Euclidean, elliptic and hyperbolic sine laws are just specializations obtained by fixing the absolute.

Minkowski: The Light Cone Absolute

Absolute = light cone

The real cone $x^2+y^2-t^2=0$ is the Cayley-Klein absolute of relativistic geometry.

Rapidity is hyperbolic angle

The cross-ratio measure between timelike rays gives $\varphi$, a hyperbolic angle.

Velocity addition

$$\varphi_{13}=\varphi_{12}+\varphi_{23},\quad \beta=\tanh\varphi$$

Reachability

The cone is the unreachable absolute; rapidities simply add along a worldline.

Printed pp. 423-442Minkowski: The Light Cone Absolute
Relativistic geometry is Cayley-Klein with the light cone as absolute. The cross-ratio measure of angle between timelike directions becomes rapidity, a hyperbolic angle that adds. Velocity addition is rapidity addition; the cone is the unreachable absolute. Connect this to the hyperbolic-distance picture from earlier slides.

Translating a Theorem

Write it once in pole-polar language, read it off in every geometry

Projective statement

"The three altitudes $\operatorname{join}(V_i, B l_i)$ are concurrent." Invariant: it mentions only poles and joins.

Euclidean reading

$B$ degenerate — altitudes meet at the orthocenter.

Elliptic / hyperbolic

Swap $B$ for the definite or disk absolute — same concurrence, curved triangle.

Printed pp. 423-442Translating a Theorem
Demonstrate the translation discipline. Write a theorem once in pole-polar language, then read off its elliptic, Euclidean and hyperbolic instances by swapping the absolute. The projective construction is invariant; only the metric reading changes. This is the payoff of the unified machinery.

Recap and Cautions

Unified test

Orthogonality is the pole-polar statement $l^T B\,m=0$; perpendiculars are joins to poles.

Dual pairs

Midpoints and angle bisectors come in harmonic / $B$-orthogonal pairs.

Sine law

One $\Psi/\Psi^*$ ratio identity specializes to Euclidean, elliptic and hyperbolic trigonometry.

Minkowski fits

The light cone is the absolute; rapidity is the hyperbolic angle and velocities add as rapidities.

Prefer constructive

$$Bl\neq 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{pole pins the line}$$

Watch degeneracy

Rank-deficient absolutes leave implicit equations underdetermined — constructions can silently fail.

Printed pp. 423-442Recap and Cautions
Close by consolidating. Orthogonality is pole-polar, midpoints and bisectors are dual pairs, the sine law is a Psi ratio identity, and Minkowski geometry fits the same mold with rapidity as angle. Prefer constructive theorems; watch degenerate absolutes where implicit equations lose their grip.
