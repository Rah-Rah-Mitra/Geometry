The Configuration

Inputs

Points $A,B,C$ on one line; $A',B',C'$ on another.

Crossed joins meet at

$P = AB' \cap A'B$
$Q = AC' \cap A'C$
$R = BC' \cap B'C$

Conclusion

$P, Q, R$ are collinear — not by accident, by theorem.

Printed pp. 3-32The Configuration
Fix two lines, place three points on each. Cross the joins: A to B-prime and A-prime to B, and the two other crossed pairs. Those three intersection points are the claim. Stress that nothing forces collinearity by eye; it is a genuine theorem we must prove, not a drawing accident.

Incidence as Linear Algebra

Join (line through two points)

$\ell = p \times q$

Meet (point on two lines)

$x = g \times h$

Collinearity = vanishing bracket

$[p,q,r] = \det\,[\,p\;\; q\;\; r\,] = 0$

Homogeneous points

A point is a nonzero vector $(x,y,z)$ up to scale; finite points divide by $z$.

Printed pp. 3-32Incidence as Linear Algebra
Here is the computational dictionary the notebook builds on. Points are homogeneous vectors up to scale, a line through two points is their cross product, two lines meet at a cross product, and three points are collinear exactly when their bracket determinant vanishes. This single dictionary powers most of the nine proofs.

Projective Completion Removes the Parallel Exception

Euclidean view

Parallel lines have no finite intersection — an annoying special case.

Projective view

They meet at a point at infinity, $z = 0$.

Limiting meet

$\{y=0\}\cap\{y=mx+1\}\to(\,1:0:0\,)$ as $m\to 0$

Printed pp. 3-32Projective Completion
Euclidean geometry treats parallels as an exception with no meeting point. Projective completion removes that exception: parallel lines meet at infinity, a homogeneous point with third coordinate zero. As slope tends to zero the finite intersection escapes like one over slope, but the homogeneous limit stays well defined on the line at infinity.

Proof I — Menelaus and Segment Ratios

Menelaus condition

$\dfrac{\overline{XY}}{\overline{YZ}}\cdot\dfrac{\overline{ZU}}{\overline{UV}}\cdot\dfrac{\overline{VW}}{\overline{WX}} = -1$

The cancellation

Multiply Menelaus ratios around the figure; auxiliary factors cancel, leaving the collinearity condition for $P,Q,R$.

Cost

Depends on an auxiliary triangle and careful sign bookkeeping.

Printed pp. 3-32Proof I: Menelaus
The first classical proof is Euclidean. Apply the theorem of Menelaus to a triangle cut by transversals, multiply the signed segment ratios around the figure, and watch the auxiliary factors cancel. What survives is the ratio condition that forces the three points onto one line. Note the dependence on sign cases and an auxiliary triangle.

Proof II — Oriented Areas

Idea

Replace fragile ratios with signed-area polynomials that absorb every sign case.

Tile identity

$\mathrm{Area}_{\square} = T_1 + T_2 + Q_1 + Q_2$

Forcing

Hypotheses make four signed terms vanish, so the fifth area must vanish: collinearity.

Printed pp. 3-32Proof II: Oriented Areas
The area proof replaces fragile ratios with signed polynomials. A surrounding quadrangle area equals a signed sum of triangle and quadrangle tiles. When the hypotheses force four of those signed areas to vanish, the remaining area must vanish too, which is precisely the collinearity. Signed areas handle every sign case in one stroke.

Proof III — The Determinant / Bracket Proof

Build each meet

$P = (A\times B')\times(A'\times B)$

Collinearity is one bracket

$[\,P,\,Q,\,R\,] = 0$

Computer-checked

SymPy proves the bracket vanishes identically, not in one drawing.

Printed pp. 3-32Proof III: Bracket
Now the projective heart. Build each intersection point as a meet, itself a cross product of two joins. Collinearity of the three results is one determinant, the bracket of their homogeneous vectors. The notebook verifies symbolically that this bracket vanishes identically, not merely in a single drawing, with SymPy confirming the exact zero.

Why the Bracket Vanishes: Grassmann–Plücker

Brackets are not free

Three-by-three determinants satisfy quadratic syzygies.

A Grassmann–Plücker relation

$[abc][ade]-[abd][ace]+[abe][acd]=0$

Pappos as instance

The starting incidences make all but one bracket known; the relation forces the last to zero.

Printed pp. 3-32Grassmann-Plucker
Why does the bracket vanish? Because brackets are not independent: they satisfy the Grassmann-Plucker relations, quadratic identities among three-by-three determinants. Pappos is a single instance where these relations force one bracket to zero given that the others encode the points lying on the two starting lines. This is the algebra the whole book exploits.

Variation I — Pascal's Theorem

Pascal

Six points on any conic: the three crossed meets of opposite sides lie on the Pascal line.

Degenerate conic

A pair of lines is a conic. Let the conic split into two lines.

Limit

$\text{Pascal} \xrightarrow{\;\text{conic}\to\text{2 lines}\;} \text{Pappos}$

Printed pp. 3-32Variation I: Pascal
A pair of lines is a degenerate conic. Pascal's theorem says six points on any conic give a collinear hexagon of crossed meets, the Pascal line. Let the conic degenerate into two lines and Pascal becomes Pappos exactly. The Pascal line survives the degeneration, so Pappos is Pascal wearing a two-line costume.

Variation II — Cayley–Bacharach–Chasles

Nine-point principle

Two cubics meet in nine points; if six lie on a conic, the other three lie on a line.

Factored pencil cubic

$p(x,y,z) = C(x,y,z)\,L(x,y,z)$

Residual line

Once the conic $C$ divides out, a linear factor $L$ remains — its zeros are collinear.

Printed pp. 3-32Variation II: Cayley-Bacharach
The second conic variation is stronger. Two cubics meet in nine points; if six lie on a conic, the remaining three lie on a line. The proof factors a pencil cubic as conic times residual line. Once the conic divides out, a linear factor remains, so its residual zeros are forced collinear. Pappos is a shadow of this principle.

Variation III — Miquel and the Cross-Ratio

Different completion

Lines are circles through one extra point; circles are pinned by the complex circular points at infinity.

Cocircularity test

$(A,B;C,D)\in\mathbb{R}\iff A,B,C,D\text{ cocircular}$

Cancellation

Each assumed circle gives a real cross-ratio, so the final four points are cocircular.

Printed pp. 3-32Variation III: Miquel
The circle variation changes the completion. Treat lines as circles and characterize ordinary circles by the two complex circular points at infinity. Four points are cocircular exactly when a cross-ratio is real. In Miquel's configuration each assumed circle contributes a real cross-ratio, so the final four points are cocircular by the same cancellation logic.
