Circles and Cycles

What is a circle when distance comes from an absolute conic?

From metric to conic

A circle is the locus at fixed Cayley-Klein distance from a center, realized as a conic tied to the absolute.

From circle to cycle

Circles, lines, horocycles, and hypercycles are members of one wider family of cycles.

Printed pp. 443-464Circles and Cycles
This chapter spans printed pages 443 through 464 and asks one question: when distance is induced by a Cayley-Klein absolute rather than primitive, what should count as a circle? The answer reframes every circle as a conic tied to the fundamental conic, and widens the word circle into the more flexible word cycle.

Distance Circles Are Conics

The literal definition

Fix a center $m$, fix a distance, collect every $p$ at that distance. The Cayley-Klein distance condition is quadratic in $p$.

So the locus is a conic

No round shape is assumed: the conic matrix is assembled from the absolute matrices $A$ (primal) and $B$ (dual).

Distance condition

$$ (m\times p)^{\!\top} B\,(m\times p) \;\propto\; p^{\!\top}\! A\, p $$

Reading the absolute

$A$ is the measuring instrument, not background decoration. Its dual $B$ encodes distances between points.

Printed pp. 443-464Distance Circles Are Conics
Fix a center and collect all points at a fixed distance from it. Because the Cayley-Klein distance condition is quadratic in the moving point, the locus is automatically a conic, not a hand-drawn round shape. We never start from a Euclidean circle; we build the conic matrix directly from the absolute.

The Circle Pencil

Pencil of circles

$$ p^{\!\top}\!\big(\lambda\, M^{\!\top} B\,M + \mu\, A\big)\,p = 0 $$

$M$ is the skew matrix of $m\times(\,\cdot\,)$.

Limiting members

$\mu=0$: a degenerate zero-radius tangent pair through $m$. The absolute $A$ itself is the infinite-radius member.

Printed pp. 443-464The Circle Pencil
Every distance circle with center m lives in the pencil spanned by the skew zero-radius matrix M-transpose B M and the primal absolute A. Sliding the parameter sweeps from radius zero, a degenerate tangent pair through m, up to the absolute itself as the infinite-radius member. One formula generates the whole family.

Circles Touch the Absolute

Exceptional points

The polar of $m$ meets the absolute in two points. Every finite circle around $m$ has double contact there.

The circular points

In Euclidean geometry these are the complex points $I=(1,i,0)$, $J=(1,-i,0)$ that every circle contains.

Double contact

$C\,e \parallel A\,e$ at each contact $e$

Printed pp. 443-464Touching the Absolute
All circles sharing a center meet the absolute at the same two exceptional points, where the polar of the center cuts the fundamental conic. There the tangent of the circle and the tangent of the absolute are proportional line vectors. In the Euclidean case those contacts are the two complex circular points every circle passes through.

Coaxial Pencils & Orthogonality

Coaxial system

A pencil of circles shares a common radical structure; members combine linearly through their conic matrices.

Orthogonality via the absolute

$$ \langle C_1, C_2\rangle_A = 0 $$

apolarity expressed through $A$ and $B$.

Conjugate families

Each coaxial pencil has an orthogonal partner crossing every member at right angles.

Printed pp. 443-464Coaxial Pencils and Orthogonality
Two circles are orthogonal exactly when each is apolar to the other through the absolute, a symmetric bilinear pairing of their matrices. Coaxial pencils and their orthogonal partners are conjugate families: one shares a radical structure, the other crosses every member at right angles, all expressed through the same fundamental conic.

Centers at Infinity

Let $c\to 0$

A homogeneous center $m=(a,b,c)$ moves to infinity as its last coordinate vanishes.

Circle flattens

Keep one finite point fixed: the finite trace tends to a straight line with normal $(a,b)$.

Projective view

$\text{line} = \text{circle} \;+\; \ell_\infty$

Printed pp. 443-464Centers at Infinity
Push the homogeneous center toward the line at infinity by letting its last coordinate vanish. Keeping one finite point fixed, the circle flattens: its finite trace becomes a straight line, and the missing component is the line at infinity. Projectively a line is just a circle whose center ran off to the horizon.

Circle, Horocycle, Hypercycle

Inside center

Ordinary circle: no real boundary contact with the absolute.

Center on absolute

Horocycle: one real contact, counted with higher multiplicity.

Outside center

Hypercycle: two real contacts with the absolute.

Printed pp. 443-464Circle, Horocycle, Hypercycle
In hyperbolic geometry the center's position relative to the absolute classifies the cycle. An interior center gives an ordinary circle with no real boundary contact, a center on the absolute gives a horocycle with one real contact of higher multiplicity, and an exterior center gives a hypercycle meeting the boundary twice.

Dual Cycles & Oriented Contact

Center plus signed parameter

A cycle carries an orientation: a center $m$ and a signed parameter $\beta$ give a primal and a dual conic.

Primal-dual product

$$ X_{\text{primal}}\,X_{\text{dual}} = \kappa\, I $$

Lie / Laguerre flavor

Oriented circles and contact make tangency, inversion, and pencils all cohere.

Printed pp. 443-464Dual Cycles and Contact
Cycles also have a dual life: a center together with a signed parameter defines a primal conic and a dual conic that multiply to a scalar matrix. This Laguerre-flavored oriented-contact picture is why pencils, tangency, and inversion all behave coherently, and why the cross-ratio of four members stays invariant under projective change of chart.

Galilean: Circles Split from Cycles

Galilean distance

$\operatorname{dist}(p,q)=x_p-x_q$ reads only the $x$-coordinate.

Distance circle

Fixed $x$-distance from a center is a pair of vertical lines — too small for a circle theory.

The robust cycle

The limiting-conic viewpoint gives vertical parabolas; line pairs survive as degenerate cycles.

Printed pp. 443-464Galilean Circles vs Cycles
Galilean geometry is the warning case. Its distance reads only the x-coordinate difference, so a finite-distance circle collapses to a pair of vertical lines. That is far too small to carry a circle theory. The robust cycle objects are instead vertical parabolas, with the line pairs surviving as special degenerate members.

Galilean Cycle Theorems

Peripheral angle

$$ \text{slope}_{AX}-\text{slope}_{BX}=a-b $$

independent of the viewpoint $X$ on $y=x^2$.

Power of a point

$$ (a-x)(b-x)=x^2-y $$

secant product depends only on $P=(x,y)$.

Checked symbolically

Both identities verified exactly with SymPy on the standard cycle.

Printed pp. 443-464Galilean Cycle Theorems
Familiar circle theorems survive on the standard cycle y equals x squared. The chord slope from parameter a to x simplifies to a plus x, so the peripheral angle becomes a slope difference independent of the viewpoint. The power of a point reappears as a secant product that depends only on the point, both checked symbolically.

Cycles as a Deformation Limit

One-parameter family

$$ x^2 + k\,y^2 + ux + vy + w = 0 $$

three finite points held fixed for every $k$.

$k<0$

Two real ideal directions: hyperbola-like cycle.

$k>0$

Complex ideal directions: ellipse-like cycle.

$k=0$

Ideal points coalesce: the Galilean vertical parabola.

Printed pp. 443-464Deforming Through Three Points
Hold three finite points fixed and vary the ideal quadratic part x squared plus k y squared. Negative k gives two real ideal directions, positive k makes them complex, and k equal to zero coalesces them into the Galilean point at infinity. That middle conic is the vertical parabola, the cycle sitting exactly between hyperbola and ellipse.

Takeaways

Circle = conic

Constant Cayley-Klein distance is quadratic, so a circle is a conic in a pencil with the absolute.

The absolute decides contact

Circles around a center share exceptional contacts from the center's polar; orthogonality is apolarity through $A$.

Lines are circles too

A center at infinity gives a line plus the line at infinity — the limiting member of the family.

Cycle is the right word

Distance circles, limit conics, and dual curves agree generically but split in the Galilean plane: vertical lines versus parabolas.

Printed pp. 443-464Takeaways
Carry away four ideas: a Cayley-Klein circle is a conic because constant distance is quadratic; the absolute fixes the shared contact points; a center at infinity yields a line plus the ideal line; and cycle bundles distance circles, limit conics, and dual curves that agree generically but split in degenerate geometries like the Galilean plane.
