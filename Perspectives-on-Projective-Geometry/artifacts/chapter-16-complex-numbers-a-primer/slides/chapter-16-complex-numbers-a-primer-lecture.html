Historical Pressure: Cubics and Quartics

Real answers, complex detours

The cubic formula passes through non-real quantities even when every root is real.

Worked case

$x^3-15x-4=0$ has a real root assembled from two conjugate complex cube roots.

Cardano's bridge

$$x=\sqrt[3]{2+11i}+\sqrt[3]{2-11i}=4$$

The lesson

Quartics reduce to a cubic auxiliary. We want a field where roots count without changing rules midstream.

Complex numbers entered mathematics through polynomial solving, not through a wish for a new plane. The Complex Plane

Identification

$a+ib \leftrightarrow (a,b)$ in the Argand plane: real and imaginary parts are coordinates.

Two coordinate systems

Cartesian $(a,b)$ and polar $(r,\theta)$ with $a=r\cos\theta$, $b=r\sin\theta$.

Addition is translation

$$(a+ib)+(c+id)=(a+c)+i(b+d)$$

Vector picture

Adding $w$ slides every point by the vector $w$ — the easy half of the arithmetic.

Printed pp. 297-310The Complex Plane
We identify the number a plus i b with the point of coordinates a and b, the Argand plane. Real and imaginary parts are Cartesian coordinates, while modulus and argument are polar coordinates. Addition is plain vector addition, a translation of the plane, which is the easy half of the arithmetic.

Polar Form and Multiplication

Polar form

$z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ with modulus $r=|z|$ and argument $\theta=\arg z$.

Multiply by $w$

Rotate by $\arg w$, then scale by $|w|$: a similarity of the plane.

Moduli multiply, arguments add

$$zw=rs\,\big(\cos(\theta+\phi)+i\sin(\theta+\phi)\big)$$

Matrix of $\times w$

$$\begin{pmatrix} s\cos\phi & -s\sin\phi \\ s\sin\phi & s\cos\phi \end{pmatrix}$$

Printed pp. 297-310Polar Form and Multiplication
Multiplication is the structured operation. Writing each number in polar form, the product multiplies the two moduli and adds the two arguments. Multiplication by a fixed w is therefore a similarity, a rotation by its argument composed with a scaling by its modulus, captured by a real two by two matrix.

Multiplication as Rotation and Scaling

Central identity

$$|zw|=|z|\,|w|, \quad \arg(zw)=\arg z+\arg w$$

Read the figure

$z$ and $w$ start as separate spokes; their product $zw$ rotates to the summed angle and stretches to the product radius.

Printed pp. 297-310Multiplication as Rotation-Scaling
Here is the central picture. Point z sits at radius r and angle theta, point w at radius s and angle phi. Their product lands at radius r times s and angle theta plus phi. Trace the arguments adding on the diagram so students see rotation and scaling acting together as one similarity.

Conjugation: Reflection and Automorphism

Field automorphism

$$\overline{z+w}=\bar z+\bar w, \quad \overline{zw}=\bar z\,\bar w$$

Conjugate root rule

For real coefficients, if $z$ is a root then $\bar z$ is too — non-real roots arrive in pairs.

Printed pp. 297-310Conjugation as Reflection
Conjugation sends a plus i b to a minus i b, a reflection in the real axis. It preserves both addition and multiplication, so it is a field automorphism of C. For real-coefficient polynomials this forces non-real roots to arrive in conjugate pairs, a rule we use repeatedly when reading factorizations.

Roots of Unity as Regular Polygons

The n-th roots

$$z_k=\sqrt[n]{|w|}\;e^{\,i(\arg w + 2\pi k)/n}, \;\; k=0,\dots,n-1$$

Geometry of polar form

Equal modulus, arguments spaced by $2\pi/n$: the roots are vertices of a regular $n$-gon.

Printed pp. 297-310Roots of Unity
The n-th roots of unity solve z to the n equals one. Their common modulus is one and their arguments are equally spaced multiples of two pi over n, so they sit as vertices of a regular n-gon on the unit circle. Solving a power equation is angle division plus a branch choice.

Euler's Formula

Exponential of imaginary input

$e^{i\theta}$ moves on the unit circle at angle $\theta$.

Angle addition, restated

$e^{i\theta}e^{i\phi}=e^{i(\theta+\phi)}$ makes "arguments add" a one-line consequence.

Euler's identity

$$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$$

Periodicity

$\theta$ and $\theta+2\pi$ name the same point, so argument is only defined modulo $2\pi$.

Printed pp. 297-310Euler's Formula
Euler's formula says e to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta, a point on the unit circle at angle theta. It makes multiplication's angle addition obvious through the exponential law, and it explains why argument is periodic: theta and theta plus two pi name the same point.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Statement

Every degree-$n$ polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ has $n$ roots, counted with multiplicity.

Algebraically closed

$\mathbb{C}$ leaves no equation unsolved — factorization is always complete.

Complete factorization

$$p(z)=a\prod_{k=1}^{n}(z-z_k)$$

Why it matters here

Closure is the bookkeeping that makes intersection counting in projective geometry uniform and predictable.

Printed pp. 297-310Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
Over C every degree n polynomial factors completely into n linear factors, counting multiplicity. The field is algebraically closed. Conjugate symmetry organizes the real-coefficient case, and this closure is exactly the bookkeeping that makes intersection counting in projective geometry uniform and predictable.

Uniform Intersection Counting

Same equation, hidden roots

$$y^2=1-x^2 \;\Rightarrow\; y=\pm\sqrt{1-x^2}$$

The principle

A line and a conic always meet in two points over $\mathbb{C}$, counted with multiplicity, even when the real drawing shows none.

Printed pp. 297-310Uniform Intersection Counting
Take the unit circle and a vertical line. At x equals 0.65 the line cuts two real points; at x equals 1.35 it misses the real picture, yet the equation still has two complex solutions for y. Complex closure records the hidden intersection, so a line and a conic always meet twice with multiplicity.

Bridge to Projective Measurement

Complexify coordinates

Homogeneous coordinates over $\mathbb{C}$ count intersections uniformly — the habit behind conics.

Circular points

Two complex points at infinity, $I$ and $J$, will encode Euclidean angle and distance.

Semilinear branch

$$x \mapsto A\,\bar x \quad (\text{matrix} + \text{conjugation})$$

Invariant that survives

The cross-ratio works over $\mathbb{C}$ exactly as over $\mathbb{R}$; Möbius maps preserve it.

Printed pp. 297-310Bridge to Projective Measurement
Two consequences carry forward. First, complexifying coordinates lets projective constructions count intersections uniformly, the habit behind conics and the circular points at infinity. Second, conjugation is an automorphism, so the fundamental theorem of projective geometry gains a semilinear branch combining a matrix with conjugation over C.
