Quotient construction

Partitions Become Points

Definition
Identification space

The points of \(Y\) are the parts of a partition of \(X\). The natural map \(q:X\to Y\) sends each \(x\) to its part.

\(U\subseteq Y\) is open exactly when \(q^{-1}(U)\subseteq X\) is open.

Teaching warning

A quotient is not the picture after glueing. It is the projection map plus the topology detected by full preimages.

Computational translation: equivalence classes plus a projectionInspection target: full preimages
Use this slide to separate two ideas students often merge. First, the quotient set is easy: every part of the partition becomes a point. Second, the quotient topology is the real mathematical content. It is defined through the projection map, so the class picture only becomes a topological space after preimages are used as the open-set test.

Universal continuity test

Continuity Out Of A Quotient

Theorem

If \(Y\) has the quotient topology from \(q:X\to Y\), then \(f:Y\to Z\) is continuous exactly when \(f\circ q:X\to Z\) is continuous.

How to use it

  1. Define the candidate map on classes.
  2. Check it is well-defined on representatives.
  3. Prove continuity after composing with the projection.
Proof move: preimage of \(f^{-1}(U)\) equals \((fq)^{-1}(U)\)Notebook section: proof scaffold
Explain this as the quotient's universal measuring device. To prove a function out of a quotient is continuous, we avoid guessing what open sets look like downstairs. We compose with the projection and test on the familiar source. Stress the extra well-definedness step: points identified by q must receive the same value under the proposed function.
Recognizing quotient maps in practice

Identification-Map Criteria

Identification Map

An onto map \(p:X\to Y\) is an identification map when the topology on \(Y\) is exactly the quotient topology forced by \(p\).

Why criteria matter

Many useful quotients are presented as concrete maps. Criteria prevent us from assuming a map creates the right topology just because it is continuous and onto.

Chapter tools: open-map, closed-map, compact-Hausdorff testsUsed for torus, cone, and projective models
Frame this slide as a practical checklist for examples. A continuous onto map need not automatically carry the quotient topology, so the chapter supplies criteria. If the map is open or closed, it is an identification map. If the source is compact and the target is Hausdorff, closedness comes for free, making many geometric quotient models legitimate.

Functions assembled from pieces

Glueing And Attaching

Glueing Lemma

If closed pieces cover a space and continuous maps agree on overlaps, their glued map is continuous.

Attaching Map

Given \(f:A\to X\) with \(A\subseteq Y\), the space \(X\cup_fY\) is formed from \(X+Y\) by identifying each \(a\in A\) with \(f(a)\).

Quotient viewpoint: disjoint union first, identify overlap secondApplied model: disk attached to a Mobius strip
Connect glueing to quotients rather than treating it as a separate lemma. The disjoint union keeps the pieces honest, and the identification map records exactly which boundary or overlap points are equal. The continuity statement then becomes another instance of the quotient continuity test: if each piece behaves correctly and the overlaps agree, the assembled map behaves correctly.
Same space, three quotients

Projective Space Descriptions

Real Projective Space

\(P^n\) is the quotient that remembers a line through the origin instead of a vector on that line.

  1. Antipodal points on \(S^n\) are identified.
  2. Nonzero vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\) are identified along the same line.
  3. Antipodal boundary points of \(B^n\) are identified.
Recognition tool: compact source to Hausdorff targetWarning: drawings in 3-space can mislead for \(P^2\)
Projective space is the deck's best example of comparing descriptions. The lecture should not ask students to memorize three definitions as unrelated facts. Each description has the same equivalence classes seen from a different source. Use the compact-to-Hausdorff criterion to explain why concrete maps can prove these descriptions produce homeomorphic quotient spaces.

Topological Groups And Quotient Groups

Topological Group

A group \(G\) is topological when multiplication \(G\times G\to G\) and inversion \(G\to G\) are continuous, with \(G\) Hausdorff.

Topological groups give quotients extra structure because algebraic translations are homeomorphisms. Begin with the real line modulo integer translation, since the exponential map shows exactly which points are identified. Then point out that the same map respects the group operation: addition modulo integers becomes multiplication on the circle. The displayed checks are numerical witnesses for that compatibility.
Homogeneous spaces

Matrix Groups And Spheres

Orbit Map

The map \(O(n)\to S^{n-1}\), \(A\mapsto Ae_1\), is onto. Matrices with the same image differ by a copy of \(O(n-1)\).

\(O(n)/O(n-1)\cong S^{n-1}\)

Chapter examples

\(SO(2)\cong S^1\), \(SO(3)\cong P^3\), and compactness of \(O(n)\) supports quotient recognition.

Orthogonality check: 3.93e-16First column norm check: 1.0000000000000002
Move from the circle quotient to matrix quotients. Orthogonal matrices act on the sphere by sending a unit vector to another unit vector. The action is transitive, so the orbit of e1 is the whole sphere. The stabilizer of e1 is a lower-dimensional orthogonal group. That stabilizer is precisely what is divided out in the homogeneous-space description.
Group actions as quotient machines

Orbit Space Definition

Group Action

A topological group \(G\) acts on \(X\) when each \(g\) gives a homeomorphism of \(X\), the action law holds, and \(G\times X\to X\) is continuous.

Orbit Space

\(X/G\) is the quotient whose points are orbits \(O(x)=\{g(x):g\in G\}\).

Examples: \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\), \(\mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}^2\), \(S^n/\mathbb{Z}_2\)Representatives are a display choice
Define orbit spaces after students have already seen several quotient examples. The important conceptual shift is that the equivalence relation is now generated by homeomorphisms. Representatives are useful for drawing, but the quotient point is the entire orbit. This distinction matters in later examples, where two actions of the same group on the same space can produce different orbit spaces.
Action matters, not just the group

Same Group, Different Quotients

Half-turn on a torus

A rotation can collapse a chosen half of the torus to a sphere-type quotient.

Different rotation axis

The same abstract group can preserve torus-type quotient data.

Central reflection

Reflection-style pairing can change the quotient to a Klein-bottle type.

Principle

The orbit space is determined by the action \(G\times X\to X\), not by \(G\) alone.

Source example: three \(Z_2\) actions on a torusInspection target: boundary identifications of a fundamental piece
Use this slide to prevent a common overgeneralization. Knowing the acting group is not enough to know the quotient. The source span describes several actions of a two-element group on a torus, producing different quotient spaces. The difference is how the generator moves the torus and how the chosen fundamental piece has its boundary identified.

Fundamental Regions And What Survives Forgetting

Fundamental Region

A region contains one representative from most orbits. Boundary points carry the quotient instructions.

  • Quotient topology is tested by saturated preimages.
  • Identification maps need recognition criteria.
  • Glueing and attaching are quotient constructions.
  • Group actions package equivalence classes as orbits.
  • Dense orbit behavior shows why representatives are not the quotient.
