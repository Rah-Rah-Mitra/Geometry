Open by making the chapter question concrete: the objects being compared may not have the same points, topology, or visible dimension. The lecture asks what it means for a circle, a finite net, a sphere with a shrinking handle, and a distance table to live near one another.
Problem setting

Why Compare Different Point Sets?

Finite Samples

A dense net should approach the continuum it samples, even though it has fewer points.

Vanishing Features

A small handle or spike may disappear in the limit while distances change only slightly.

Scaling Limits

Zooming in or out can reveal a cone, a norm, or a large-scale shape not visible at the original scale.

source span: printed 241-270comparison must survive missing or extra points
Use this slide to separate two habits. A map-based comparison expects a named point in one space to have a named partner in the other. Gromov-Hausdorff comparison instead asks whether the distance patterns can be placed near each other after allowing extra or missing sample points.

Fixed set versus variable space

From Uniform Metrics to Flexible Spaces

Uniform Metric Convergence

\(\sup_{x,y\in X}|d_n(x,y)-d(x,y)|\to 0\)

All metrics live on the same underlying set, so every pair of points has a stable name.

Gromov-Hausdorff Convergence

\(X_n \to X\)

The point set itself may change; only the finite distance patterns must become close.

Finite samples can gain points.
Small handles can collapse.
Lattices can converge to normed spaces.
Cones can appear under rescaling.
fixed-set control is not enoughnext: Lipschitz distance
This is the transition from fixed-set thinking to variable-space thinking. Uniform convergence of metrics is powerful when the underlying set is fixed, but the chapter needs a comparison that tolerates different cardinalities, disappearing pieces, and finite approximations.
Section 7.2

Lipschitz Distance: Relative Error

Definition

\(d_L(X,Y)=\inf_f \log \max\{\operatorname{dil}(f),\operatorname{dil}(f^{-1})\}\)

The infimum runs over bi-Lipschitz homeomorphisms \(f:X\to Y\).

relative distortion, topology-sensitivebest finite value below: 0.371564
Present Lipschitz distance as a relative-error instrument. It is excellent when a homeomorphism is part of the data and every distance should be distorted by a controlled factor. The logarithm turns multiplicative stretch in both directions into an additive nonnegative number.

Limitation

Why Lipschitz Is Too Strong

Needs a Homeomorphism

Spaces of different topology are outside the comparison, however small the feature may be.

Needs Matching Points

A triangle and the same triangle with one near-duplicate point cannot be bijectively matched.

Penalizes Ratios

Changing a very small distance by a factor of two may be a large Lipschitz error.

Desired replacement. Compare compact spaces by additive distance error after allowing a common ambient space, a relation, or an almost-isometric sampling map.

from relative map control to additive metric pattern controlnext: Hausdorff distance
Pause on the restrictions before introducing the new metric. Lipschitz distance demands a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism, so it cannot compare spaces with different topology or cardinality. A near-duplicate point or a collapsing handle is not a small Lipschitz perturbation of the original space.
Section 7.3

Hausdorff First, Then Gromov-Hausdorff

Same Ambient Space

\(d_H(A,B)=\inf\{\epsilon:A\subset B_\epsilon,\ B\subset A_\epsilon\}\)

Hausdorff distance compares subsets already living in one metric space.

Best Common Placement

\(d_{GH}(X,Y)=\inf_Z d_H(i(X),j(Y))\)

The infimum ranges over isometric embeddings into a common metric space \(Z\).

ambient embeddings are auxiliary, not part of the inputPDF pages 256-285
Build the definition in two steps. Hausdorff distance works after the spaces already sit inside one ambient metric space. Gromov-Hausdorff distance removes the ambient space from the input by allowing all common placements and taking the best Hausdorff comparison.
Relations instead of functions

Correspondences Replace Maps

Definition

A correspondence \(R\subset X\times Y\) is a relation whose projections cover both \(X\) and \(Y\).

One point may be paired with several points; no point may be ignored.

correspondences tolerate duplicate samplesevery row and column must be covered
Explain that a correspondence is deliberately more permissive than a function. Every point must appear, but a point may be paired with several points on the other side. This is exactly the structure needed for duplicate samples, collapsed fibers, and finite approximations of continua.

Distortion

\(\operatorname{dis}(R)=\sup |d_X(x,x')-d_Y(y,y')|\)

Read the heatmap as a pairwise stress test. A correspondence is judged not by one paired distance, but by the worst discrepancy between two paired pairs. The notebook check records distortion 0.16 and hence the displayed finite Gromov-Hausdorff upper bound 0.08.
Computable reformulation

The Correspondence Formula

Compact Metric Spaces

\(d_{GH}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)}\operatorname{dis}(R)\)

Embedding View

Place two spaces into a common ambient metric space and compare subsets.

Relation View

Pair points flexibly and measure the worst distance-table error.

Finite View

On tiny spaces, enumerate covering relations and compute the best distortion.

the factor 1/2 comes from turning distance-table error into ambient separationGH by correspondences
State the formula as the computational heart of the chapter. For compact metric spaces, Gromov-Hausdorff distance can be recovered as one half of the best correspondence distortion. This converts an existential embedding definition into finite distance bookkeeping.
Equivalent language up to constants

Epsilon-Isometries

Two Duties

  • Small distortion: \(|d_Y(fx,fx')-d_X(x,x')|\le \epsilon\).
  • Dense image: every point of \(Y\) lies within \(\epsilon\) of \(f(X)\).

What Is Relaxed?

The map may be discontinuous, non-injective, or non-surjective. It is judged by metric error and coverage, not topology.

artifact: gh-convergence-finite-net-lab.htmlfinite nets are epsilon-isometries in action
Use epsilon-isometries as the map-shaped version of the same looseness. The map need not be continuous or bijective; it only needs small distortion and an epsilon-dense image. That difference is what lets a finite net approximate an entire compact space.
Metric structure

GH Metric on Compact Spaces

Objects

Isometry classes of compact metric spaces.

Zero Distance

\(d_{GH}(X,Y)=0\) exactly when \(X\) and \(Y\) are isometric.

Triangle Inequality

Compose near-optimal correspondences and add their distortions.

compactness supports the metric theoremwork modulo isometry
Emphasize that compactness is doing real work. After quotienting by isometry, Gromov-Hausdorff distance is a genuine metric on compact metric spaces. The triangle inequality is believable because correspondences can be composed while their distortions add.

Compactness in the space of spaces

Precompactness Through Total Boundedness

Uniform Diameter

\(\operatorname{diam}(X_i)\le D\)

No sequence can escape by simply growing larger and larger.

Uniform Nets

\(N(X_i,\epsilon)\le N(\epsilon)\)

At each resolution, one finite size bound sees every space in the family.

Proof sketch. Choose compatible nets at scales \(1,1/2,1/3,\ldots\). Pass to subsequences until all named pairwise distances converge, then quotient and complete the limiting semi-metric.

finite distance tables form the diagonal spineprecompactness criterion
Use this slide to connect convergence to a uniform finite-resolution principle. Uniformly bounded diameter prevents spaces from drifting apart, while a shared bound on epsilon-net sizes prevents uncontrolled small-scale complexity. The diagonal proof then extracts limiting finite distance tables.
Section 7.5

Limits of Length Spaces

Theorem

A complete Gromov-Hausdorff limit of compact length spaces is again a length space.

Graph Approximation

Sampled shortest-path metrics remain slightly longer than Euclidean samples; the recorded maximum sampled excess is \(0.0891\).

Key Tool

Approximate midpoints pass through low-distortion correspondences.

artifact: length-space-graph-approximation.pngpath geometry survives in the limit
Shift from compactness of the universe to preservation of path geometry. Graph approximations show how sampled shortest-path metrics approach length behavior. The theorem says complete Gromov-Hausdorff limits of compact length spaces remain length spaces because approximate midpoints survive.
Proof flow

Midpoint Criterion Proof Flow

Choose Representatives

Use a small-distortion relation to lift \(x,y\in X\) to nearby \(x_n,y_n\in X_n\).

Take a Midpoint

Length spaces provide approximate midpoints \(z_n\) between the lifted points.

Return to the Limit

Bring \(z_n\) back through the relation; the distortion estimates prove it is an approximate midpoint for \(x,y\).

artifact: proof-flow-gh-to-length-spaces.pngmidpoint criterion closes the chapter
Close with the proof mechanism. Choose nearby representatives in approximating length spaces, take an approximate midpoint there, and send it back across a low-distortion correspondence. Letting the error shrink supplies approximate midpoints in the limit, which triggers the midpoint criterion.
