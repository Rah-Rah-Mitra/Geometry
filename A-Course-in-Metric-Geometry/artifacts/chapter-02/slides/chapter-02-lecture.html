The chapter question is a two-way bridge. A length structure gives a distance by taking infima over path lengths, while any metric gives a curve length by partition sums. The lecture tracks when this round trip changes the geometry and when it returns the same intrinsic metric.
Section 2.1

A Length Structure Is a Contract

Restriction

Subpaths remain admissible and inherit length.

Concatenation

Legal pieces glue and their lengths add.

Reparameterization

Changing speed cannot change the path length.

Exit Cost

Escaping a neighborhood costs a positive amount.

artifact: length-structure-axioms.pngcontract before metric
Use the contract language deliberately. A length structure is not just a curve with a number attached; it must behave well under restriction, concatenation, reparameterization, and local variation. These rules are what let path pieces assemble into a reliable metric.
Path distance

The Induced Metric Is the Cheapest Legal Route

Definition

\(d_L(x,y)=\inf\{L(\gamma):\gamma:x\to y\}\)

Accessibility

The path system partitions the space into finite-distance components, just as Chapter 1's infinite metrics did.

distance from admissible curvesinaccessible pairs have infinite induced distance
The infimum formula is where path geometry becomes distance. If two points can be joined by admissible curves, their distance is the cheapest length among those curves. If they cannot be joined, the induced distance is infinite, echoing the component picture from Chapter 1.

Partition sums make curve length from a metric without coordinates. As the partition is refined, the polygonal approximation sees more of the curve's oscillation. The key point is that the supremum over all partitions is the definition, not just a numerical approximation.
Key formula

The Supremum Over Partitions Is the Definition

Partition Data

Choose ordered times \(a=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_N=b\). Add the metric jumps between consecutive samples.

\(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} d(\gamma(t_i),\gamma(t_{i+1}))\)

Curve Length

Take the largest forced total over all finite partitions. Finer observations can only reveal more length.

\(L_d(\gamma)=\sup_Y \sum_i d(\gamma(t_i),\gamma(t_{i+1}))\)
ordered samples along one curvesupremum, not one preferred partition
This formula slide is intentionally quiet. Name the partition, walk through consecutive samples, and then stress the supremum. The formula says that length is the largest total distance forced by every finite observation of the path in order.
Section 2.4

Intrinsic Metrics Are Fixed Points of the Round Trip

metric \(d\)starting data
curve length \(L_d\)partition supremum
path metric \(d_{L_d}\)infimum over paths
compare\(d\le d_{L_d}\)
intrinsic\(d=d_{L_d}\)

When It Works

Distances can be recovered by arbitrarily fine path approximations.

When It Fails

The original metric allows jumps or chords that the legal path geometry cannot realize.

intrinsic means path-recoverablemetric-to-length-to-metric fixed point
Intrinsic metrics are fixed points of the metric-to-length-to-metric round trip. If arbitrarily fine paths recover exactly the original distance, then the metric already behaves as a path metric. If not, the induced intrinsic metric records extra route costs.
Operational intrinsicness

The Dotted-Line Test: Small Jumps, Nearly Correct Total

Test

Can \(x\) and \(y\) be joined by finite chains with very small jumps and total length close to \(d(x,y)\)?

Notebook Evidence

Dyadic midpoint chains keep total length equal to the endpoint distance while the maximum jump tends to zero.

This is a finite, checkable version of approximating a path by increasingly fine pieces.

artifact: midpoint-chain-intrinsic-characterization.pnglab: epsilon-midpoint-chain-lab.html
The dotted-line test makes intrinsicness operational. We do not need a smooth path immediately; we can ask whether points can be connected by chains with very small jumps whose total length is close to the claimed distance. The notebook's dyadic chain keeps total length exact while max jump shrinks.
Strict intrinsic spaces

Exact Midpoints Turn Chains Into Candidate Paths

Midpoint Property

For every pair \(x,y\), there is \(m\) with \(d(x,m)=d(m,y)=d(x,y)/2\).

Completeness Role

The dyadic choices need a limiting curve. Completeness prevents the candidate path from disappearing.

midpoints plus completeness build pathsfrom chains toward geodesics
A strict intrinsic space has exact midpoints, and repeated midpoint choices begin to look like a path. Completeness is what turns the dyadic limiting process into an actual curve, so midpoint constructions are also compactness and completeness tests in disguise.
Section 2.5

Shortest Path and Geodesic Are Not the Same Claim

Shortest Path

A path realizes the global distance between its endpoints.

Geodesic

A path may be locally minimizing while a different global route is shorter.

Failure Modes

Infimum not attained; local minimizer not global; compactness needed for subsequential limits.

artifact: shortest-paths-geodesics-compactness.pngglobal versus local minimization
Separate shortest paths from geodesics carefully. A shortest path realizes the global distance. A geodesic may only minimize locally. The punctured-plane model has an infimum that is not attained, while the circle model shows a locally minimizing long arc that is not globally shortest.
Existence theorem mechanism

Where Do Shortest Paths Come From?

Compact Case

Compactness turns minimizing sequences into convergent subsequences.

Local Compactness Route

In complete locally compact length spaces, closed bounded balls behave compactly enough for path existence.

teach the theorem as a mechanismnot just a named conclusion
The existence theorem should be taught as a mechanism. Compactness supplies convergent subsequences of almost-minimizing paths, lower semicontinuity prevents length from jumping down incorrectly, and completeness plus local compactness can produce compact closed balls where minimizers live.
Section 2.6

Simple Curve Length Equals One-Dimensional Hausdorff Measure

Simple Image

When a curve traces its image once, length and one-dimensional Hausdorff measure agree.

Two Inequalities

Cover the image by short curve intervals, and use connected-set diameter estimates in the other direction.

\(L(\gamma)=\mathcal{H}^1(\gamma([a,b]))\) for simple curves
artifact: hausdorff-length-and-multiplicity.pngsimple image means count once
For simple curves, length and one-dimensional Hausdorff measure agree because the image is traversed once. This is the bridge from the path language of Chapter 2 back to the scale-cover language introduced at the end of Chapter 1.
Warning

Multiplicity: Image Measure Forgets Traversal Count

Common Mistake

The image set alone does not remember how many times the curve traversed it.

Measure vs Length

\(\mathcal{H}^1\) measures the set. Length measures the path with multiplicity.

non-simple length counts multiplicityrecorded check: true
The multiplicity warning prevents a common mistake. Hausdorff measure sees the image set, but length counts how the curve travels through that image. Traversing a segment forward and then backward doubles length while the image measure remains the same.
Section 2.7

Lipschitz Speed Integrates Back to Length

Metric Speed

\(|\dot\gamma|(t)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{d(\gamma(t+h),\gamma(t))}{|h|}\)

Length Formula

\(L(\gamma)=\int_a^b |\dot\gamma|(t)\,dt\)

The notebook check records the speed-integral relative error below \(10^{-5}\).

artifact: lipschitz-speed-integral.pngmetric derivative exists almost everywhere for Lipschitz curves
Metric speed is the instantaneous version of path length. For Lipschitz curves the metric derivative exists almost everywhere, and integrating that speed recovers length. The speed graph turns the abstract derivative into a measurable rate.
Applied lab

Diagnose a Candidate Metric by Asking for Short Hops

Decision Tree

  • No finite chain with small jumps? Look for missing admissible paths.
  • Chains exist but total length stays too large? The metric may not be intrinsic.
  • Infimum exists but no curve attains it? Compactness or completeness may be missing.
  • Local minimizers exist but are not shortest? Use geodesic language carefully.

What to Try

Pick two points and a scale \(\epsilon\). Build a chain whose jumps are below \(\epsilon\), then compare the chain's total length to the claimed distance.

short jumps + near-correct total \(\Rightarrow\) intrinsic behavior
lab link: epsilon-midpoint-chain-lab.htmlfrom theorem to diagnostic workflow
The diagnostic lab is a practical checklist for unfamiliar spaces. If short chains fail, the space is not behaving intrinsically. If an infimum is never reached, compactness or completeness may be missing. If local minimizers stop being global, geodesic language must be used with care.
