Chapter question

How can a metric space have curvature when it has no tangent planes?

Available data

Distances between points and lengths of geodesic segments.

Replacement for derivatives

Compare every small triangle with a triangle in a model plane.

Curvature signal

Triangles are thinner, fatter, or exactly model-like.

Examples in range

Fans, normed planes, polyhedra, cones, and constant-curvature models.

Source-grounded in the assigned Chapter 04 notebook and pages 101-134.16-slide learning arc
Pose the chapter question before giving definitions. Students know geodesics and constructions; now the task is to decide when a space is no fatter or no thinner than a model plane. The important move is that every test uses distances among points on geodesic triangles, so it still makes sense in polyhedral, cone, and branching examples.
Comparison triangle dictionary

Translate a metric triangle into a model triangle

Dictionary

  • Original vertices: \(x,y,z\) in \(X\).
  • Model vertices: \(\bar x,\bar y,\bar z\) with matching side lengths.
  • Side points: \(p\in [xy]\) and \(\bar p\in[\bar x\bar y]\) with the same endpoint distances.
  • Test value: compare \(d(p,q)\) with \(|\bar p\bar q|\).
Residuals make the dictionary quantitative: zero means model-like, signed deviation means a curvature-side signal.
Treat the comparison triangle as a dictionary. A triangle in the metric space is translated into a Euclidean or model-space triangle with the same three side lengths, then points on corresponding sides are compared. Emphasize that the comparison triangle is not an embedding of the original triangle; it is a measuring instrument.
Two inequality directions

Upper and lower bounds ask opposite distance questions

CAT(\(\kappa\)): curvature \(\le \kappa\)

\(d(p,q)\le d_\kappa(\bar p,\bar q)\)

Geodesic triangles are no thicker than the model. Interior points are at most as far apart as their comparison points.

CBB(\(\kappa\)): curvature \(\ge \kappa\)

\(d(p,q)\ge d_\kappa(\bar p,\bar q)\)

Geodesic triangles are no thinner than the model. The comparison inequality reverses direction.

Memory hook: upper bound = thin; lower bound = thick.Same dictionary, reversed test.
Slow down the inequality directions. Upper curvature bounds and lower curvature bounds are easy to reverse by memory, so attach them to the visual language: CAT upper-bound triangles are not thicker than the model, while CBB lower-bound triangles are not thinner than the model. The same residual plot can be read as evidence for which side wins.
Local definitions as tests

Bounded curvature is inspected in small geodesic neighborhoods

1

Choose a ball

Work where geodesics exist and model comparison triangles are well-defined.

2

Test all triangles

Every small geodesic triangle must satisfy the chosen comparison inequality.

3

Record locality

The curvature claim is local until a globalization theorem supplies extra structure.

Local CAT(\(\kappa\)) or local CBB(\(\kappa\)) = comparison holds near each point.

Local definitions are tests carried out in sufficiently small balls. The point is not to demand one global model triangle for a whole space, but to make every small geodesic triangle pass the correct comparison. Mention that the chapter later discusses how local information can globalize under extra hypotheses.
Fan CAT(0) example

A nonsmooth hinge can still pass a thin-triangle test

Teaching point

A CAT(0) example need not be a smooth flat surface. What matters is whether every geodesic triangle is at least as thin as its Euclidean comparison triangle.

The fan model lets curvature be checked at the glued spine, where smooth coordinates are unavailable.

Use the fan as the first positive CAT example. A Euclidean fan glued along a spine can still make triangle distances contract relative to the flat comparison triangle, because geodesics can share a central hinge. The example helps students see why nonsmooth does not mean uncontrolled.
L1 plane warning

A familiar vector space can fail the comparison tests

What goes wrong?

  • The unit ball is a diamond, not a Euclidean circle.
  • Geodesics between two points need not be unique.
  • Triangle interiors do not behave like constant-curvature model interiors.
Normed geometry supplies useful counterexamples: topology and linear structure do not force comparison curvature.
The L1 plane is the warning label. It has straight-looking geodesics and a familiar topology, but the norm creates many geodesics and the comparison tests fail in characteristic ways. This prevents students from equating metric vector spaces with Euclidean local curvature behavior.

Angle existence

Metric angles are limiting comparison angles

Definition by limit

For geodesics \(\gamma\) and \(\eta\) from \(p\), compare the small triangle \(p,\gamma(s),\eta(t)\) in the model plane.

\(\angle(\gamma,\eta)=\lim_{s,t\to 0}\widetilde{\angle}_p(\gamma(s),\eta(t))\)

Angles have to be earned in a metric space. Explain that the angle between geodesics is recovered as a limiting comparison angle, not assumed from an inner product. The teaching emphasis is existence and compatibility: once comparison controls small triangles, the limiting angle becomes a stable local datum.
Distance functions as sensors

Watch one distance along a moving geodesic

Sensor principle

Fix \(a\in X\), move along a geodesic \(\gamma(t)\), and study \(t\mapsto d(a,\gamma(t))\) or \(d(a,\gamma(t))^2\).

Curvature bounds become convexity, concavity, and derivative statements for this one-variable signal.

The plot turns comparison geometry into readable function behavior.
Distance functions are sensors because they convert geometric curvature into one-variable inequalities along geodesics. Rather than staring at all triangles at once, measure how the distance to a fixed point changes along a moving geodesic. Convexity or concavity is then a readable trace of the curvature bound.
E-convexity and E-concavity

Error terms let model curvature enter a convexity test

Upper-bound flavor

Distance-squared behaves like a convex function along geodesics, up to a curvature-dependent error term.

f(t)\le (1-t)f(0)+tf(1)+E_\kappa(t)

Lower-bound flavor

The analogous comparison can reverse into a concavity-type inequality when triangles are model-thick.

f(t)\ge (1-t)f(0)+tf(1)-E_\kappa(t)

The symbol \(E_\kappa\) is the bookkeeping device: it reminds us that nonzero curvature changes the straight-line convexity rule.

Introduce E-convexity and E-concavity as error-corrected versions of ordinary convexity. The correction term accounts for the model curvature and the fact that distance squared is the natural smooth proxy. Students should hear that the sign of the inequality records the same upper-versus-lower curvature split from earlier.
First variation formula

Angles control the first-order change of distance

\(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0+} d(a,\gamma(t))=-\cos\angle_a(\gamma,a)\)

Metric reading

The formula says that a distance sensor has a one-sided derivative determined by the comparison angle at the base. It is the nonsmooth analogue of the Riemannian first variation calculation.

First variation connects the metric chapter back to the familiar differential-geometric formula. The rate of change of distance along a geodesic is controlled by the cosine of the angle at the base point. In this nonsmooth setting the statement is still about comparison angles and one-sided derivatives.
Numerical first variation sanity

Finite differences should match the angle prediction

Diagnostic

Sample \(d(a,\gamma(t))\) at small \(t\), estimate the right derivative, and compare the residual with \(-\cos\theta\).

A small residual does not prove the theorem, but it confirms that the model and the computation are speaking the same language.

Use the numerical residual as a sanity check rather than as a proof. The plot asks whether finite differences of distance agree with the angle predicted by the first-variation formula. It gives students a concrete diagnostic for when the theorem is saying something measurable.
Nonzero curvature bounds

Replace the Euclidean model by \(M_\kappa^2\)

Same comparison logic

  • \(\kappa=0\): Euclidean comparison.
  • \(\kappa>0\): spherical model, with diameter restrictions.
  • \(\kappa<0\): hyperbolic model, with thinner long-range behavior.
Angle sums are a visible way to remember the sign of the model curvature.
The zero-curvature story generalizes by replacing the Euclidean comparison triangle with a model triangle in the constant-curvature plane. Positive, zero, and negative model geometries change angle sums and distance behavior, but the logical structure of comparison remains the same.

Why this matters

Curvature bounds begin as small-triangle statements, but major geometric consequences require extending those inequalities across the space.

Instructor warning

Do not silently replace local CAT or CBB by global CAT or CBB. The chapter's globalization results explain exactly when that leap is justified.

Globalization should be framed as a roadmap, not a magic theorem. Local comparison requires completeness, geodesic control, and topological hypotheses before it can become a global statement. The chapter uses this to explain why curvature bounds are both local tests and global geometry theorems.

Takeaways

  • Cones make curvature visible as angle deficit or excess.
