Neutral Stance

Assume incidence, betweenness, distance, and angles; withhold parallels

Working Definition

Neutral geometry is the common core of plane geometry developed before adopting a Euclidean or hyperbolic parallel axiom.

Available Data
  • Segments, rays, angles, betweenness, and plane separation.
  • Distance and angle measure from earlier chapters.
  • SAS congruence as a controlled triangle axiom.
Pause on the word neutral and make it operational. The point is not vagueness; it is discipline. We are allowed to measure segments and angles, use separation, and invoke SAS. We are not allowed to use similarity, angle sums equal to two right angles, or unique parallels unless they have already been proved without the parallel postulate.
SAS Congruence

A local triangle can be copied by two sides and the included angle

Notebook visual target: compare two triangles after rigid alignment.

SAS Pattern

\(AB \cong A'B',\; AC \cong A'C',\; \angle BAC \cong \angle B'A'C'\)

Then every remaining corresponding side and angle agrees.

Why This Matters
  • It turns partial local measurements into a full triangle match.
  • It supports isosceles triangle arguments and right-triangle tests.
  • It is a proof primitive, not a parallel theorem in disguise.
Treat SAS as the chapter's first real lever. The diagram is deliberately an overlay: after the two side lengths and the included angle are matched, the second triangle has no remaining freedom. Invite students to say exactly which data are used and which conclusions are earned, because that habit prevents circular congruence proofs later.

Exterior Angle Theorem

An outside angle is larger than either remote interior angle

Notebook visual target: read an exterior angle against the two remote interior angles.

Theorem Pattern

Extend one side of a triangle. The exterior angle formed is greater than each nonadjacent interior angle.

\(m\angle BCD > m\angle A,\quad m\angle BCD > m\angle B\)

Proof Discipline

Do not replace this with the Euclidean angle-sum formula. The inequality is neutral and must be proved before angle sums become available in later chapters.

Use this theorem as the first major place where the no-parallel rule matters. In Euclidean geometry students may instinctively subtract from one hundred eighty degrees, but that is not neutral. The neutral proof instead builds a comparison triangle and uses congruence plus betweenness to force the exterior angle to dominate the remote interior angles.
Inequality Consequences

Exterior angles turn triangle shape into order information

Angle Order

Larger side, larger opposite angle

If one side of a triangle is longer, the angle facing it is larger.

Side Order

Larger angle, larger opposite side

The converse comparison follows by contradiction and the exterior-angle theorem.

Distance Order

Triangle inequality survives

A broken path through a third point cannot beat the direct segment.

Move slowly from theorem to consequences. The point is that neutral geometry can compare sizes without using similarity or coordinate slope. The exterior-angle theorem supplies the contradiction pattern: assume the wrong order, construct a comparison point or triangle, and watch an angle become both larger and not larger than another angle.
Right Triangles

Perpendicularity makes congruence tests sharper

Hypotenuse-Leg Idea

For right triangles, one right angle removes rotational ambiguity. Matching the hypotenuse and one leg forces the remaining leg and acute angles to match.

Proof Use
  • Drop a perpendicular to create a right triangle.
  • Use equal distances from a center, endpoint, or bisector.
  • Convert the comparison back into a statement about the original figure.
Right triangles are useful because perpendicularity adds a rigid angle for free. Emphasize that the hypotenuse-leg pattern is not a new magic axiom; it is a compressed congruence argument. The proof strategy is often to introduce an auxiliary perpendicular, prove two right triangles congruent, and then read off the hidden equality or angle relation.
Perpendicular Bisectors

Equidistance turns a line into a locus

Locus Statement

A point on the perpendicular bisector of segment \(AB\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\). Conversely, a point equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\) lies on that bisector.

Neutral Ingredients
  • Midpoint and perpendicular line structure.
  • Right-triangle congruence on the two halves.
  • No use of parallel lines or similarity ratios.
Use this slide to show a typical neutral proof template in miniature. A locus statement sounds global, but the proof is local: introduce the midpoint, draw the perpendicular, and compare the two right triangles. This is also a good moment to ask students which direction of the statement is easier and why the converse needs care.
Circles And Tangent Lines

A tangent is detected by a perpendicular radius

Notebook visual target: check that the tangent direction is perpendicular to the radius.

Tangent Criterion

If line \(\ell\) touches circle center \(C\) at \(T\), then \(CT \perp \ell\). Conversely, a line through \(T\) perpendicular to \(CT\) is tangent at \(T\).

Inspection Target

Every other point on the line must be farther from the center than \(T\), so it cannot lie on the same circle.

Connect circle geometry back to right triangles. The radius to the tangency point supplies the perpendicular, and the perpendicular supplies a shortest-distance comparison. The converse is equally important for constructions: once a radius is known, the perpendicular through the point gives the only possible tangent line at that point.
Synthetic Workflow

How a tangent proof usually pays its debts

Four Debts To Pay
  • Show the candidate point \(T\) is on the circle.
  • Show \(CT\) is perpendicular to the candidate line.
  • Use a right-triangle comparison for any other point \(X\) on the line.
  • Conclude \(CX > CT\), so \(X\) is outside the circle.
Common Error

Do not assume a tangent line is obvious from a picture. The proof must establish single-point contact using distance and perpendicularity.

This workflow is meant to make circle proofs less mystical. A tangent proof has obligations: put the point on the circle, establish a perpendicular radius, compare every other point on the line by right-triangle logic, and only then claim single contact. That order is what turns a convincing drawing into a neutral theorem.

What The Checks Are For

They do not replace proof. They make geometric claims inspectable, catch blank artifacts, and force each diagram to carry a measurable invariant.

What They Are Not For

A numeric example cannot prove a neutral theorem. It is a laboratory for finding the right synthetic dependency.

Explain why the computational checks belong in a proof-heavy chapter. They are not empirical substitutes for theorem proofs, but they help students inspect whether a constructed figure is meaningful. The chapter checks record betweenness, nonzero area, angle measurement, visual variation, and artifact count so the diagrams remain accountable to geometry rather than decoration.

Takeaway

Neutral geometry is not weak geometry. It is the portable core: congruence moves local data, exterior angles create inequalities, right triangles organize perpendiculars, and tangent proofs convert circle pictures into distance arguments.

Close by returning to the chapter question: how much geometry works before choosing a parallel axiom? The answer is more than students expect. A neutral proof can establish robust triangle congruence, inequality, right-triangle, and tangent facts. The exit problems ask students to practice the method, not to recite theorem names.
