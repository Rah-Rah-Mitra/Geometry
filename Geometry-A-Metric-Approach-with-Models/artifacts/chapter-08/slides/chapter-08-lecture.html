Poincare Disk: What Counts As A Line?

Definition 8.1 - Model Predicate
A disk geodesic is a diameter or a circle arc orthogonal to the unit boundary.

Its endpoints on the boundary are ideal endpoints. They organize limiting behavior but are not points of the hyperbolic plane.

Metric cue:
\(ds = \dfrac{2\,|dz|}{1-|z|^2}\)
Distances stretch without bound as \(|z| \to 1\).
The first technical point is that a Poincare disk picture is not ordinary Euclidean geometry with a decorative circle. A hyperbolic line is either a diameter or a circle arc meeting the unit boundary orthogonally. The metric factor explains why the boundary is infinitely far away even when the drawing looks bounded.

Asymptotic Rays Share An Ideal Endpoint

Definition 8.2 - Asymptotic
Two rays are asymptotic when they determine the same ideal endpoint.
  • They may have no ordinary point in common.
  • Near the shared endpoint, their hyperbolic separation can tend to zero.
  • The boundary supplies a direction at infinity.
Do not test asymptotic behavior with Euclidean slope. Test the ideal endpoint and the hyperbolic metric behavior.
Emphasize that asymptotic is a boundary statement, not a claim about Euclidean slopes. Two rays can fail to meet inside the disk and still be tied together by one ideal endpoint. This is the mental move students need before the many-parallels theorem feels natural rather than surprising.

Many Parallels Through One Exterior Point

Hyperbolic Parallel Phenomenon
For a line \(L\) and a point \(P \notin L\), there is more than one line through \(P\) that does not meet \(L\).

The limiting lines point toward the ideal endpoints of \(L\); the remaining non-meeting lines form a whole interval of directions.

Frame the fan through P as the direct failure of the Euclidean parallel postulate. The two limiting geodesics aim at the ideal endpoints of the reference line, while the geodesics between them also avoid the reference line. This gives many parallels, but it also gives a structured family rather than chaos.

Triangle Defect As A Measurable Signal

Definition 8.3 - Defect
For a hyperbolic triangle \(T\),
\(D(T)=\pi-(A+B+C)\)

In the hyperbolic plane, \(D(T)>0\). In curvature \(-1\), area and defect agree.

Notebook check: sample defect \(\approx 0.7416\) radians, with nonblank visual statistics recorded in the chapter checks.
This slide turns a qualitative theorem into a measurement. In the hyperbolic plane, triangles have angle sum below pi, and the missing amount is the defect. The notebook records a sample defect of about 0.7416 radians, which is useful because students can compare a drawing, a formula, and a numerical check.

Distance Between Parallel Lines Is Not Constant

Metric Contrast
A nonintersecting pair does not automatically have one constant separation.
  • Asymptotic parallels approach an ideal endpoint.
  • Ultraparallel lines have a common perpendicular realizing minimum distance.
  • Euclidean intuition about parallel strips no longer applies.
Ask: distance at which point, along which perpendicular, or at the closest approach?
The important contrast is that Euclidean parallel lines have a constant separation, so the phrase the distance between the lines is unambiguous. Hyperbolic nonintersecting lines require more care: asymptotic pairs can approach one another toward an ideal endpoint, while ultraparallel pairs have a common perpendicular realizing their minimum separation.

Growth Separates The Models

Curvature \(-1\) Scale
Metric balls reveal global curvature.
\(C_H(r)=2\pi\sinh r\)
\(A_H(r)=2\pi(\cosh r-1)\)

For small \(r\), these resemble Euclidean formulas. For large \(r\), the exponential terms dominate.

Hyperbolic drawings are bounded on paper, but the metric makes room expand rapidly.
Growth is the global version of the same phenomenon. Near the origin, hyperbolic measurements can look nearly Euclidean, but the circumference and area formulas expose exponential behavior at larger radius. Connect this to the chapter's warning that bounded drawings should not be confused with bounded hyperbolic size.

Proof Roadmap: Defect Is Additive

Proof Skeleton
If \(T=T_1\cup T_2\) along a geodesic segment, then
\(D(T)=D(T_1)+D(T_2)\)
  • Write each defect as \(\pi\) minus its angle sum.
  • The two new angles at the cut add to \(\pi\).
  • The extra \(\pi\) cancels exactly.
This additive behavior is why defect can serve as an area-like invariant in constant negative curvature.
Defect becomes powerful because it behaves additively under subdivision. The proof idea is accessible: when a triangle is cut along a geodesic, the new angles introduced along the cut add to a straight angle, so the missing angle from pi is conserved as a sum. This is the bridge from local angle sums to area.
