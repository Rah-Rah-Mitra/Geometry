From incidence to metric structure

Definition frame

A collineation preserves lines and incidence. An isometry preserves the metric, and therefore carries congruent configurations to congruent configurations.

\(d(F(P),F(Q)) = d(P,Q)\)
IncidencePoints on lines stay points on lines.
BetweennessOrder data survives when the model supports it.
DistanceSide lengths and metric circles are unchanged.
Group lawCompositions and inverses remain inside the motion family.
Use this slide to separate three ideas that often blur together. A map can respect lines without respecting length, and it can preserve shape up to scale without being an isometry. The distance equation is the test that moves us into metric geometry.

Disk models: the same boundary, different visible lines

Model rule

A disk picture is not the geometry by itself. The geometry is the disk together with the designated lines, metric, and admissible transformations.

  • Klein lines are visually straight chords, useful for incidence and projective arguments.
  • Poincare lines are arcs or diameters meeting the boundary at right angles, useful for angle inspection.
  • An isometry must preserve the model metric, not the ordinary Euclidean appearance of the drawing.
Instructor checkpoint

If a visual distance looks shorter near the boundary, ask which metric is being measured before drawing a conclusion.

The disk models are a deliberate change of glasses. The same bounded picture can encode different geometric promises depending on which curves count as lines and which metric is active. Press students to name the model rules before interpreting straightness, angle, or distance.

Reflections: one mirror, many consequences

Reflection test

A reflection fixes a line pointwise and sends every other point to the opposite side at the same perpendicular distance from the mirror.

Generator principle

Once reflections are available, many isometries are built by composing mirrors. Classification becomes a question about mirror geometry.

  • One reflection reverses orientation.
  • Two reflections preserve orientation.
  • The fixed set reveals the type of motion.
The reflection slide should feel concrete. Students can literally check the mirror line, equal perpendicular distances, and orientation reversal. Then shift from the single construction to the bigger lesson: reflections are not isolated tricks; they are a generating language for motion groups.

Double reflections convert mirror geometry into motion type

Composition rule

Reflect across \(m_1\), then across \(m_2\). The relative position of the mirrors determines the resulting isometry.

Pencils and cycles package invariant families

Vocabulary

A pencil is a family organized by a common condition. A cycle is a generalized circle or line used as an invariant object under transformations.

  • Common center: every member shares a central constraint.
  • Common limiting data: the family can be recognized by shared boundary behavior.
  • Transformation test: a useful motion sends the whole family to a corresponding family.
Why it matters

Classification becomes less point-by-point when whole families of cycles reveal what a transformation preserves.

Pencils and cycles are a way to reduce visual clutter. Instead of tracking one point at a time, we track a family with a shared rule. This is especially useful when a transformation is easier to recognize by its invariant circles or limiting data.

Common trap

The drawing may suggest a motion name, but the proof needs an invariant test that works for every point in the model.

Students usually recognize translations and rotations visually, but the chapter asks for a sharper standard. Use the grid to move from recognition to diagnosis: distance, orientation, fixed points, and invariant families. Those tests are portable across Euclidean and disk-model settings.

A coordinate lab for Euclidean isometries

Affine form

In the Euclidean plane, a motion can be tested in the form \(F(x)=Ax+b\).

\(F\) is an isometry when \(A^T A=I\).
Algebraic checkGeometric meaning
\(A^T A=I\)lengths and angles are preserved
\(\det A=1\)orientation is preserved
\(\det A=-1\)orientation is reversed
\((A-I)x=-b\)fixed points solve a linear system
This slide connects the synthetic chapter to computation without replacing proof by sampling. The matrix equation tests the linear part of the motion, while the fixed point equation explains why rotations and reflections have visible anchors and translations usually do not.

Model isometries preserve the chosen model structure

Disk-map checklist
  • The interior of the disk maps to itself.
  • Model lines map to model lines.
  • The model distance between any two points is unchanged.
  • Boundary or limiting data is carried consistently.
Typical formula family

Fractional linear maps provide a compact algebraic language for many disk transformations.

\(z \mapsto \dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}\)
Keep the focus on model structure rather than formula worship. A fractional linear expression is useful only after we know it sends the model to itself and preserves the relevant metric data. The visual checklist gives students a way to interrogate any proposed disk transformation.

The proof architecture is a dependency graph

Group viewpoint

The isometries of a model form a group: identity, closure under composition, inverses, and associativity inherited from functions.

  • Definitions specify admissible transformations.
  • Lemmas prove preservation under basic moves.
  • Classification theorems name every possible composition pattern.
  • Models and checks reveal which hypotheses are active.
This graph is a map of how the chapter thinks. It starts with primitive model data, turns that data into definitions, builds lemmas about reflections and invariants, and ends with classification theorems. Emphasize that computational checks support the route but do not replace the universal claims.

Misconceptions worth catching early

1. Straight in the image

A straight-looking curve in a drawing may not be a model line, and a curved-looking one may be the correct line.

2. One tested triangle

Preserving a sample triangle is not enough. An isometry statement quantifies over all point pairs in the model.

3. Composition order

Reflecting in \(m_1\) and then \(m_2\) need not equal reflecting in \(m_2\) and then \(m_1\).

Correction habit

State the active model, write the preservation equation, then identify fixed objects and invariant families.

Quick diagnostic

If a transformation is hard to name, ask what happens to a line, a circle, a pair of distances, and an orientation marker.

Proof habit

A classification theorem needs both existence and uniqueness: every allowed motion appears, and no two names overlap improperly.

This is the slide to slow down student confidence. Pictures are persuasive, but the chapter rewards careful predicates. Ask the room to repair each misconception by adding the missing quantifier, the missing model rule, or the missing composition order.

Takeaways

  • Geometry is encoded by the structures that transformations preserve.
  • Reflections are the workbench for building and classifying isometries.
  • Pencils and cycles expose invariant families, not just isolated points.
  • A model isometry must be judged by the model metric and model lines.
