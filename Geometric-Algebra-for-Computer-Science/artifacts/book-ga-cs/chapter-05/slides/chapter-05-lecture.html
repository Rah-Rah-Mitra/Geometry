Chapter 05

Intersection and Union of Subspaces

Meet and join are incidence operations for blades: powerful geometric products whose behavior changes when the subspaces themselves change relationship.

Start by naming the geometric tension of the chapter: intersections and unions are ordinary geometric questions, but they are not ordinary bilinear products. The title visual should orient the audience to the three objects that will recur throughout the lecture: two input blades A and B, their meet M, and the join J that contains the whole incidence problem.

Why The Outer Product Alone Is Not Enough

Phenomenology
The output grade can switch at incidence.
Two planes through the origin usually meet in a line, but coincident planes meet in a plane. A product that returns the desired subspace must notice containment, not just multiply coordinates.
  • Meet and join are defined for blades representing subspaces, not arbitrary multivectors.
  • Small perturbations can change the correct join and therefore change the result grade.
  • Magnitude and orientation of the meet are not absolute; they are meaningful only relative to a consistent factorization.
Incidence creates nonlinear case changes.
Use this slide to separate two ideas students often merge. The outer product is excellent for spanning independent directions, but intersection is controlled by containment. When the planes coincide, the desired answer changes grade. That grade switch is the first sign that meet and join cannot be simple bilinear extensions to all multivectors.

Outer Factorization: Peel Out The Common Blade

Definition Pattern
\(A = A' \wedge M,\quad B = M \wedge B'\)
\(J = A \cup B = A' \wedge M \wedge B',\quad M = A \cap B\)

The meet is the largest common outer factor. The join is the smallest blade that contains both input subspaces. The scalar weight can be traded between \(M\) and \(J\), so applications must keep a consistent convention.

common factor residual factors relative scale orientation bookkeeping
The factorization diagram gives a concrete mental model for the chapter. Invite learners to imagine physically sliding a common factor out of both blades. What remains on the left and right are residual directions. The join must contain each residual direction once, while the meet is only the shared attitude, up to a consistent scalar and sign.

Computing The Meet Once The Join Is Known

Relationship
\(J = A \wedge (M^{-1}\!\rfloor B) = (A\!\lfloor M^{-1}) \wedge B\)
\(M = A \cap B = (B\!\rfloor J^{-1})\!\rfloor A\)

In many geometric problems, the join is obvious: two planes in 3-space share \(I_3\), a line and a plane in 3-space also live in \(I_3\). Then contraction with the inverse join extracts the meet.

Practical Reading
Pick the smallest common stage \(J\), dualize one input inside that stage, then contract with the other input. The metric used for the contraction cancels out of the incidence result.
This is the computational center of the lecture. Emphasize that the formula is not a recipe until the join is chosen. In ordinary examples the join is the obvious pseudoscalar of the common subspace, but the same formula becomes wrong if the join changes at a degeneracy. That is why the diagram puts J in the middle.

Dual Meet: Complements Inside The Join

Relative Dual Formula
\((A \cap B)^* = B^* \wedge A^*\)

The star is not absolute. It is the dual inside the join \(J = A \cup B\). The formula says: intersecting subspaces is equivalent to spanning their complementary parts, then dualizing back.

Ordering Warning
The order is \(B^* \wedge A^*\). The sign depends on the codimensions \(j-a\) and \(j-b\), so swapping meet arguments may or may not change orientation.
Treat the duality formula as a conceptual bridge, not just a compact equation. The complement of A inside J and the complement of B inside J span what is missing from the meet. The reversed order matters, because it carries the sign convention that later appears in the table of common geometric situations.

Subspace Incidence: Line Meets Plane

Line And Plane Through The Origin
\(B = e_1 \wedge e_2,\quad a = a_1e_1 + a_2e_2 + a_3e_3\)
\(a \cap B = (B\!\rfloor I_3^{-1})\!\rfloor a = a_3\)

With the oriented join \(I_3\), the scalar meet records the signed piercing direction. The line contained in the plane is the degenerate case: the old join returns zero and a lower-dimensional join must be selected.

This incidence example is where sign becomes physically meaningful. The meet is a scalar because the line and plane share only the origin in the vector-space model. Its sign tells whether the directed line pierces the oriented plane from one side or the other. When the line lies in the plane, zero is not failure; it is the signal to change the join.

Mostly Linear Until The Join Changes

Local Behavior
For a fixed join, meet is built from contractions and therefore varies linearly with the blade coordinates. At a degeneracy, the correct join changes and the computation enters a different linear region.
line = e1 + t e3 plane = e1 ^ e2 meet_weight = line meet plane = t max_error_against_t = 0.0

The notebook sweep uses 161 values of \(t\) and verifies that the selected \(I_3\) join gives exactly the signed height until the contained-line case asks for a different join.

The phrase mostly linear is practical. It tells a programmer what to expect while the incidence type is stable: interpolation, perturbation tests, and residual checks behave normally. The zero at the center of the graph is not just a small number. It marks the moment where the old join is no longer the smallest common space.

The Meet Can Carry Angle Information

Normalized Join
For normalized blades inside a normalized join, the magnitude of \(A \cap B\) is the sine of the angle from \(A\) to \(B\), with sign fixed by orientation conventions.
Two lines in a plane
Swapping arguments reverses the sine. The meet is antisymmetric.
Line and plane in space
The order does not change the sign, but the piercing side still does.
Notebook check: maximum line and plane sine errors are \(1.22 \times 10^{-16}\).
This slide is the reason not to discard meet magnitude too quickly. In the right normalization, the meet records a sine of the relative angle, so it becomes a quantitative incidence measure. The sign should be discussed with care: it depends on the chosen join orientation and sometimes on argument order.

Linear Maps Preserve Meet And Join Structure

Structure Preservation
\(f[A \cup B] = f[A] \cup f[B]\)
\(f[A \cap B] = f[A] \cap f[B]\)

An invertible linear map cannot turn containment into non-containment. It may stretch metric measurements, but it preserves the outer-factorization relationships that define meet and join.

Dualization Detail
In computations after transformation, dualize relative to \(f[J]\), not the original \(J\). The transformed join is part of the data.
The transformation property looks surprising only if meet is viewed as a metric construction. The defining relationships are outer-factorization relationships, so an outermorphism carries them along. Remind the audience that the computational formula with contractions is still useful, but after applying f the duality must be taken relative to the transformed join.

Offset Subspaces Need A Model

Part I Limitation
The chapter works first with subspaces through the origin. Lines away from the origin, parallel lines, and translated planes require a model where offset incidence becomes linear again.

meet of lines: \(\ell_1 \times \ell_2\)

This slide prevents overgeneralizing the vector-space examples. In Part I, the subspaces pass through the origin, so many real geometric incidences are not yet available. Homogeneous coordinates add a scale coordinate, making offset lines and parallel behavior algebraic. The chapter deliberately previews this without replacing the later model-building work.

Floating-Point Trouble Is Often A Modeling Signal

Near Degeneracy
Meet and join algorithms must decide output grade from finite precision data. Near parallel or nearly coincident inputs can flip the detected grade when a condition threshold is crossed.
a = e1 b = e1 + epsilon e2 while join(a, b) is still a vector: epsilon = 1.01 epsilon report the first bivector join

Robust code should report the threshold and residuals, not hide the event behind a coordinate that looks ordinary.

Close the computational thread by connecting floating point behavior back to geometry. A grade flip is not merely roundoff noise; it may reveal that the incidence type is changing. The programming example searches for the first epsilon where two almost parallel vectors are treated as spanning a bivector. Good software should expose that transition.

Recap: A Useful But Careful Product Pair

Core Takeaways
  • Meet is the shared outer factor; join is the smallest containing blade.
  • The formula for meet needs the correct join and dualizes inside that join.
  • Magnitude and sign become meaningful only after a consistent normalization.
  • Degeneracy is a change in incidence, not just a numerical inconvenience.
Instructor Prompt
Take \(A=e_1\) and \(B=\cos\theta e_1 + \sin\theta e_2\). Predict the join, compute the meet relative to the join, and explain why swapping the arguments changes the sign.
The recap should leave students with a workflow rather than a memorized symbol. First identify the incidence type and the join. Then compute using the duality or contraction formula appropriate to that join. Finally check dimensions, sign, and limiting cases. The prompt makes the workflow concrete with two vectors where the antisymmetry is visible.
