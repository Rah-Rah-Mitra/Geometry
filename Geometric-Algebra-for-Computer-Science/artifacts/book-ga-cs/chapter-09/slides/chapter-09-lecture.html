Embedding Geometry In Algebra

Extra Coordinates Are Purchased Freedoms

The same Euclidean scene can be embedded into algebras that make different questions cheap.

Vector-Space Model

Directions, plane attitudes, rotations, projections, and angle tests stay close to the Euclidean metric.

Homogeneous Model

One coordinate makes finite and ideal elements share a projective incidence calculus.

Plucker Line Model

Projective 3-D lines become bivectors; incidence is strong, but Euclidean metric meaning is supplemental.

Conformal Model

Two null directions encode Euclidean distances and make motions act as versor sandwiches.

Frame the four panels as four answers to one problem: how should familiar Euclidean objects be made native? Vector space keeps the original metric and is excellent for directions. Homogeneous coordinates buy offset incidence. Plucker coordinates specialize projective line reasoning. Conformal embedding spends two dimensions so the metric and Euclidean operators are built into the algebra.
Incidence Predicates

When Geometry Becomes A Zero Test

The notebook diagnostics check that incidence and transformations commute in the chosen representation.

Predicate Forms

Homogeneous point on line

\(a x + b y + c w = 0\)

Plucker line condition

\(d\cdot m=0,\quad d_1\cdot m_2+d_2\cdot m_1=0\)

Conformal object incidence

\(P\cdot S=0\) or \(P\wedge L=0\), depending on representation.

The point of this slide is to make incidence feel operational. In the homogeneous model, a line equation becomes a predicate and the meet of two lines gives a point whose residual can be checked. Plucker line coordinates extend this style to projective 3-D line geometry. Warn that a zero incidence test is not the same as a good Euclidean measurement.
Metric Choices

The Metric Decides What Products Can Measure

Chapter 9 previews why homogeneous geometry is powerful for incidence but thin for native Euclidean metric operations.

Budget Questions

  1. Does the algorithm need lengths and angles, or mainly containment and intersection?
  2. Will transformations be ordinary matrices, outermorphisms, or versor sandwiches?
  3. Does de-embedding introduce a chart singularity such as \(w \approx 0\)?
  4. Can every metric claim be backed by a local residual check?

Homogeneous coordinates make offset flats linear, but Euclidean measurement often lives outside the algebra. Conformal coordinates make the algebra larger so distance and motion can become algebraic invariants.

Explain the word budget literally: a model spends dimensions, signature choices, and implementation effort in exchange for native operations. The vector-space model has the Euclidean metric but no offset flats. Homogeneous geometry cheaply fixes offset incidence but does not give a full Euclidean geometric product. Conformal geometry costs more but lets distance and Euclidean motions become product-level statements.

Choosing A Model

Ask For The Invariant Before The Coordinates

The model should make the algorithm's promises easy to state, compute, and test.

1. Name Native Objects

Is the problem about directions, offset flats, lines in 3-D projective space, rounds, tangencies, or Euclidean motions?

2. Name The Metric

Does the geometric product measure lengths and angles, or are those quantities external to the representation?

3. Name The Predicate

Which expression should be zero for incidence, containment, contact, or equality after transformation?

4. Name The Operator

Does a motion act by matrix multiplication, outermorphism, rotor, motor, or more general versor sandwich?

5. Name The Chart Risk

Where can normalization, a point at infinity, a null vector, or a degenerate line make numeric interpretation unstable?

6. Name The Check

Record a residual: incidence after a transform, distance after a rotation, or nullness after conformal embedding.

Turn this slide into a short design exercise. Ask students to imagine a ray tracer, a multi-camera calibration problem, and a rigid-body interpolation problem. Each task asks for different invariants, so it should not automatically use the same model. The strongest answer is the one where the native objects, metric, predicate, operator, chart risk, and validation check all fit together.
Takeaways

Modeling Is The Bridge From Algebra To Geometry

The rest of Part II is best read as a sequence of modeling choices, not a list of coordinate tricks.

What To Remember

  • A vector-space algebra is already powerful for directions, attitudes, and rotations through the origin.
  • Homogeneous coordinates make offset incidence and projective transformations linear.
  • Plucker coordinates expose 3-D line geometry as projective bivector algebra.
  • Conformal geometry uses a null metric so distance, rounds, and Euclidean versors become native.

Final rule: do not ask which model is most elegant; ask which model makes the intended geometric facts algebraic, inspectable, and stable.

Close by returning to the bridge idea. Chapter 9 is short, but it changes the reader's posture for the entire part. The algebraic machinery is not being replaced; it is being relocated into representation spaces where different geometric facts become native. That is why model selection is a computer science issue as much as a mathematical one.
