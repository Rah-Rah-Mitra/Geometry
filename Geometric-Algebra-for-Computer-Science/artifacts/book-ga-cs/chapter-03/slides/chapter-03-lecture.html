Chapter Question

Once the outer product spans lines, planes, and volumes, what extra operations become possible when the vector space has a dot product?

$$\text{span} \quad + \quad \text{metric} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{subspace computation}$$
  • Compare equal-grade blades by size and angle.
  • Contract unequal grades without dropping orientation.
  • Convert between direct and orthogonal-complement descriptions.
  • Make projection and coordinate extraction invariant checks.
Open by positioning Chapter 3 as the metric upgrade to the outer product. Students already know how to span a blade; now the central question is how two spans compare in size, attitude, perpendicular complement, and computational usefulness.

Same-Grade Comparison

Blade Scalar Products Read A Gram Matrix

grade k with grade k

Determinant Meaning

$$A = a_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge a_k,\quad B = b_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge b_k$$ $$A \star B = \det\!\left[a_i \cdot b_j\right]$$
  • For \(k=1\), this is the vector dot product.
  • For \(k=2\), all four pairwise vector comparisons matter.
  • If the factors become dependent, the determinant collapses.
  • Orientation and reverse conventions must be named in code.
Emphasize that the determinant is not a coordinate trick. It is the compact record of all pairwise vector dot products between two spanning lists, so dependence, orientation, and metric size all show up in one comparison.
Subspace Attitude

Angles Between Subspaces

normalize before interpreting

Angle Check

$$\cos \Theta = \frac{A \star B}{\|A\|\,\|B\|}$$

The denominator is a diagnostic. A zero blade norm means the spanning list was degenerate, so an angle is not defined as a stable geometric quantity.

Valid same grade, nonzero norms, metric known
Suspicious near-zero determinant or hidden sign convention
Present the angle formula as a normalized blade comparison. For lines it reduces to the ordinary dot-product angle; for planes it asks how much oriented area in one plane matches oriented area in the other, with degeneracy detected by a vanishing norm.
Unequal Grades

Contraction Lowers Grade

metric test, oriented remainder

Read The Operation

$$x \rfloor B \quad \text{has grade } 2 - 1 = 1$$

The result is not a loose coordinate pair. It is a blade-like object whose sign and direction still depend on the orientation of \(B\).

  • Outer product asks whether the factors add span.
  • Scalar product asks how much equal-grade content matches.
  • Contraction asks what oriented piece remains after a metric test.
Slow down on the intuition. A contraction is not simply projection and it is not another outer product. It tests one blade against another with the metric, then returns the lower-grade piece that remains with orientation still attached.
Metric Operation

Projection And Rejection

residual orthogonality is the test

Formula And Audit

$$P_B(x) = (x \rfloor B)B^{-1},\qquad R_B(x) = x - P_B(x)$$
In span \(P_B(x)\) is built from the blade \(B\), not from a coordinate-axis shortcut.
Orthogonal residual \(R_B(x) \cdot b_i = 0\) for every spanning direction \(b_i\).
Independent check Compare against a Gram solve when implementing the formula numerically.
Failure mode A near-singular blade makes the inverse and projection unstable.
Connect the formula to the diagram. The contraction produces a plane-oriented in-span object, multiplication by the blade inverse converts it back to a vector, and the rejection is the residual whose dot products with the spanning directions should vanish.
Orthogonal Complements

Direct Blades And Dual Constraints

ambient pseudoscalar required

Two Descriptions

$$\text{direct span: } B \qquad \text{dual constraint: } B^*$$

Duality is metric dependent because perpendicularity is metric dependent. It is also ambient-dimension dependent because the complement of a plane in 3-D is a line, while the complement changes in other dimensions.

  • Direct blades are natural for spans and incidence.
  • Dual blades are natural for normals and constraints.
  • The pseudoscalar \(I\) records the oriented full space.
Use direct and dual descriptions as two legitimate views of the same subspace. The direct blade lists directions that span the object, while the dual uses the ambient pseudoscalar and metric to encode the perpendicular constraint.
Implementation Guardrails

Inner Product Diagnostics

name the expected grade

Same Grade

Expect a scalar comparison. Use the blade norm and subspace angle tests.

$$\langle A,B\rangle_0$$
Good for size, angle, determinant collapse, and sign-convention checks.

Different Grades

Expect a grade-lowering blade. Decide left or right contraction before coding.

$$\operatorname{grade}(A\rfloor B)=s-r$$
Good for projection formulas, incidence tests, and orientation-sensitive leftovers.

Complement View

Expect the ambient pseudoscalar and metric to enter the conversion.

$$A^* = A I^{-1}$$
Good for normals, constraints, and direct-versus-dual representation checks.
Metric known? The same vectors can produce different products under a different metric.
Reverse needed? Positive size and scalar-product convention are related but not identical.
Inverse stable? Projection through a nearly dependent blade magnifies numerical error.
Result grade right? Unexpected grade is a fast signal for a contraction or selection error.
This is the troubleshooting slide. Before trusting a number, ask which grades are being compared, whether the metric is Euclidean or not, whether a reverse or blade inverse is required, and whether the result should be a scalar or a lower-grade blade.
Nonorthonormal Coordinates

Reciprocal Frames Extract Coordinates

dot products become coordinate probes

Coordinate Extraction

$$e_i \cdot e^j = \delta_i^j,\qquad x = \sum_i (x \cdot e^i)e_i$$

A skew frame is useful, but it needs a metric companion. The reciprocal frame provides coordinate probes that compensate for the nonorthogonal angles of the original basis.

  • Nearly dependent frame vectors produce long reciprocal vectors.
  • The dot-product table is the simplest correctness audit.
  • Color-space conversion is a concrete version of the same idea.
Frame the reciprocal basis as a metric companion, not as a separate linear-algebra afterthought. In a skew frame, the reciprocal vectors become the probes that isolate coordinates through dot products satisfying the Kronecker-delta table for the chosen frame.
Classical Bridge

Cross Product As Dual Area

3-D only, orientation chosen

Unhide The Choices

$$a \times b \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad (a \wedge b) I^{-1}$$

The blade \(a \wedge b\) is the dimensional object. The vector normal is a dual representation that only appears after choosing the 3-D pseudoscalar and orientation.

Portable The area blade generalizes beyond 3-D.
Special The cross-product vector is a 3-D dual artifact.
Treat the familiar cross product as a bridge back to classical 3-D vector algebra. The geometric object is the oriented area blade, and the normal vector appears only after choosing the three-dimensional pseudoscalar and dualizing.
Notebook Practice

Computation Should Audit Geometry

invariants beat coincidences

Four Useful Assertions

  1. Blade area squared matches the reverse-adjusted metric product.
  2. Contraction projection agrees with an independent Gram solve.
  3. The rejection is orthogonal to every target spanning direction.
  4. Reciprocal-frame coordinates reconstruct the original vector.
$$\max_i |R_B(x)\cdot b_i| \approx 0$$
Tie the deck back to notebook practice. The implementation should compare contraction projection against a Gram solve, check residual orthogonality, inspect determinant collapse near dependence, and verify reciprocal-frame reconstruction instead of relying on axis-aligned coincidences.
