Reflections

A Vector Can Be A Mirror Operator

02

Line Reflection

parallel kept, perpendicular flipped

\(x' = a x a^{-1}\)

Hyperplane Reflection

normal flipped, plane component kept

same sandwich idea, adjusted by grade convention

Inspection Target

Check which component changes sign. The mirror is stored by an invertible vector, not by a table of coordinate cases.

Emphasize that the reflecting vector has two readings. It is still a drawable direction, but when placed on both sides of an object it becomes an operator. The visual target is component behavior: the part aligned with the mirror survives while the complementary part changes sign.

Rotor Construction

Two Reflections Store One Rotation

03

Product Of Mirrors

\(R = b a\)

Reflect in \(a\), then in \(b\). The composite is even, so it preserves orientation.

Half-Angle Rule

mirror angle \(=\theta/2\)

visible rotation \(=\theta\)

Operational Equality

two mirrors: \(b(a x a^{-1})b^{-1}\)

one rotor: \(R x \widetilde R\)

Walk through the order slowly: the rightmost reflection acts first, and the product of the two mirror vectors stores the whole operation. The half-angle is the important geometric surprise. A small angle between mirrors produces twice that angle on any vector in the plane.

Sandwich Action

One Rotor Acts On Every Grade

04

Unit Rotor Contract

\(R \widetilde R = 1\)

The reverse is the inverse when the rotor is normalized.

Outermorphism

\(R(a \wedge b)\widetilde R = (Ra\widetilde R) \wedge (Rb\widetilde R)\)

Why It Matters In Code

One transformation path handles points, directions, normals as dual blades, and geometric products without separate case formulas.

Present the sandwich as an interface, not just a formula. Once the rotor is unit length, the reverse supplies the inverse, and the same expression can rotate a vector, an oriented area blade, or a product of objects. That is the coding payoff of the algebra.

Bivector Planes

The Rotation Plane Is The Generator

05

Rotor From Plane And Angle

\(R = \cos(\theta/2) - B\sin(\theta/2)\)

Here \(B\) is a unit bivector: an oriented plane element with \(B^2=-1\).

Axis Is Secondary

In 3-D an axis is the dual of the plane. The rotor stores the plane where motion happens, not only the line that stays fixed.

Inspection Target

The sign of \(B\) chooses clockwise versus counterclockwise within the same carrier plane.

Students often import axis-angle intuition too early. Recenter the picture on the bivector plane, because the plane is what multiplies like the imaginary unit and what appears in the exponential. In three dimensions the familiar axis is available by duality, but it is not the stored generator.

Double Cover

The Vector Closes Before The Rotor Does

06

Common Trap

The actions of \(R\) and \(-R\) on a single vector are identical, but the paths they encode are different.

Two Returns

visible vector: closes after \(2\pi\)

rotor element: closes after \(4\pi\)

Continuity Decision

Interpolation code must choose whether to preserve the remembered path or flip signs for the shortest visible motion.

Use the plot as a diagnostic rather than as a curiosity. A vector endpoint returns after one full turn, but the rotor has moved to negative identity. That sign matters whenever the path is continuous, especially in animation, tracking, interpolation, or controller state.

Composition

Rotor Products Remember Order

07

Function Order

first \(R_1\), then \(R_2\): \(R_2 R_1\)

Noncommuting Planes

In 3-D, the \(e_1e_2\) turn and the \(e_2e_3\) turn generally create different final frames when their order changes.

Diagnostics

Each even composition still preserves length and orientation, so determinant and norm checks can pass while the order is semantically wrong.

The key habit is to read rotor products like composed functions. The rotor nearest the object acts first after the sandwich expands. Stress that both orders can pass basic orthogonality checks, so a frame picture or a basis-image ledger is needed to catch order bugs.

Versors

Parity Separates Rotations From Reflections

08

Definition

A versor is a product of invertible vectors used as an operator in a sandwich product.

\(V = v_k \cdots v_2 v_1\)

Even

rotor

orientation preserving

Odd

reflection family

orientation reversing

Generalization

In higher dimensions, a rotor may include multiple bivector planes and top-grade parts while still acting by the same sandwich interface.

Define versor broadly enough that rotors become one case rather than the whole story. The parity of the vector-factor count controls orientation behavior. That lets students classify an operator before expanding it, and it prepares them for odd reflections and higher-dimensional even versors.

Subspaces As Operators

A Blade Can Be Both Object And Action

09

Object Reading

A blade draws a direct subspace with attitude, orientation, and weight.

Operator Reading

The same blade can generate a reflection operator through the sandwich construction.

Structure Preservation

transform first, then build equals build first, then transform

Make the object-operator dual reading explicit. A blade may represent a plane that can be inspected, but the same algebraic element can also define a reflection. The important invariant is structural: transformed wedges agree with wedging transformed factors, so constructions move coherently.

Classical Links

Complex Numbers And Quaternions Are Rotor Shadows

10

2-D Even Algebra

The unit pseudoscalar of the plane plays the role of \(i\).

3-D Bivector Basis

Quaternion coordinates are scalar plus oriented bivector parts.

Beyond 3-D

Rotor algebra does not stop where quaternion coordinates stop.

Use this as a translation slide. Complex numbers and quaternions are not abandoned; they are explained as low-dimensional rotor coordinates. The gain is conceptual and practical: the multiplication rules come from geometric products, and the same idea can move beyond the dimensions those classical systems cover.

Exponential Form

Logs Turn Rotor Motion Into Bivector Motion

11

Generator

\(R = \exp(-B\theta/2)\)

Relative Interpolation

\(\Delta = R_1\widetilde{R_0}\)

\(R(t)=\Delta^t R_0\)

Branch Choice

The logarithm chooses a representative bivector angle, so sign continuity and shortest-path behavior are policy decisions.

Connect logarithms to interpolation rather than treating them as formal decoration. The log extracts the bivector angle that generates the relative rotor. Scaling that bivector gives meaningful intermediate operators, and the same interpolated rotor can act on a vector path and a blade path together.

Diagnostics

What To Check When A Rotation Looks Wrong

12

Finite Test Set

  • Apply to basis vectors and inspect the frame.
  • Verify length, inner products, and blade weights.
  • Check determinant sign for even or odd parity.
  • Log the rotor to recover plane and angle.

Failure Pattern

A rotation may be normalized and still be the wrong operation because factors were multiplied in the wrong order or sign continuity was broken.

Frame diagnostics as small invariant questions. Norm preservation catches scaling errors, determinant sign catches parity mistakes, basis images catch order mistakes, and log coordinates catch the intended plane and angle. The goal is to debug the operator as a geometric object, not as a mysterious array.

Worked Lab

Decode A Rotor Before Trusting It

13

Prompt

\(R = 0.866 - 0.5 e_{12}\)

Read It

plane: \(e_{12}\)

angle: \(60^\circ\)

Use this as a short active pause. Students should identify the scalar as the cosine of half the angle, the bivector coefficient as the signed sine term, and the plane as e12. The diagnostic is not complete until they predict and test one basis vector image.
