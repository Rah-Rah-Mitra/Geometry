Linear Transformations of Subspaces

A matrix that moves vectors also determines how every oriented subspace moves. Chapter 4 packages that compatibility as the outermorphism.

Subspace Images

A Linear Map Sends Spans To Spans

Definition View

If \(U = \operatorname{span}\{a,b\}\), then its image is the span of the transformed generators:

\(M(U)=\operatorname{span}\{Ma,Mb\}\)
  • Lines stay lines through the origin.
  • Planes can shear, scale, rotate, or collapse.
  • The origin remains the common anchor.
Use this slide to connect ordinary linear algebra with the chapter language. A subspace is represented by generators, so the image subspace is obtained by transforming those generators. The important inspection target is the span itself: although the grid shears, the marked one-dimensional subspace remains a straight span through the origin and is now generated by the moved vectors.
Outermorphisms

Transform The Factors, Then Rebuild The Blade

Canonical Extension

The outermorphism \(\Lambda M\) is the unique grade-wise extension that preserves the outer product.

\(\Lambda M(a \wedge b) = (Ma)\wedge(Mb)\)

Why It Is Forced

  • Changing generators inside the same span does not change the blade rule.
  • Degenerate images become zero blades automatically.
  • Orientation and signed scale remain visible.
Present the outermorphism as a forced construction rather than a new arbitrary operation. The blade is an oriented span built from vector factors, so the honest way to transform it is to move the factors and wedge them again. Point out that this is exactly what preserves degeneracy, orientation, and coordinate independence when students later use matrix representations.
Transformed Blades

The Grade-Two Matrix Is Built From Minors

Coordinate Rule

After a basis blade order is fixed, \(\Lambda^2 M\) is an ordinary matrix acting on bivector coordinates.

The entries are \(2\)-by-\(2\) minors because each coordinate is a signed area projection.

\(B=b_{12}e_{12}+b_{13}e_{13}+b_{23}e_{23}\)

Move from the geometric rule to the implementation rule. The wedge construction is primary, but code usually stores coordinates, so we need the correct grade-two matrix. Explain that each bivector coordinate measures a signed projection onto a basis plane. Transforming an area therefore requires all two-by-two minors, which is why simply reusing the vector matrix on blade coordinates would be wrong.
Degenerate Images

When A Plane Collapses, The Blade Says Zero

Important Failure Mode

A rank-deficient map can send independent generators into a dependent set. The outermorphism detects that the image subspace lost dimension.

\(Ma \parallel Mb \quad \Longrightarrow \quad (Ma)\wedge(Mb)=0\)

Debug Signal

A near-zero transformed blade is not automatically a bug. It may be the correct certificate that the map flattened the original area.

Use the collapse example to make degeneracy constructive. Students may think a zero blade is a numerical accident, but under a rank-dropping transformation it is exactly the right output. The visual target is the disappearance of area after both transformed generators land on the same line, which ties the algebraic wedge test to geometric dimension loss.
Determinant And Volume

The Determinant Is Top-Grade Scaling

Geometric Definition

In \(n\) dimensions, the top-grade blade space is one-dimensional. A linear map can only scale the unit pseudoscalar.

\(\Lambda^n M(I)=\det(M)\,I\)
  • Magnitude gives volume change.
  • Sign gives orientation change.
  • Zero gives volume collapse.
Reframe determinant away from row operations and toward the top-grade blade. The unit pseudoscalar has no room to rotate inside its own grade, so the outermorphism can only multiply it by a scalar. That scalar is the determinant. Ask students to read magnitude, sign, and zero as volume scale, orientation, and collapse respectively.
Signed Area And Orientation

Volume Change Includes A Direction Of Traversal

Signed scale is the information that lets a transformed blade distinguish stretching from reflection.
Pause on the sign of the determinant before moving into metric products. The same area magnitude can come from an orientation-preserving or orientation-reversing map, and blades keep that distinction. Use the three panels to ask which ordered pair of transformed vectors agrees with the original traversal and which has been reflected.
Metric Products

The Adjoint Moves Measurements To The Other Slot

Adjoint Identity

The adjoint is the map satisfying the scalar-product transfer rule.

\(\langle Mx,y\rangle = \langle x,M^\ast y\rangle\)

Metric Pullback

In Euclidean coordinates, transformed measurements use \(M^T M\). With a general metric, the metric matrix participates in the adjoint.

After the metric-free outer product, introduce the first place where measurements need extra care. The adjoint is not a mysterious transpose trick; it is the bookkeeping map that moves a linear transformation from the object being measured to the measuring object. This prepares students for normals, contractions, and dual descriptions that cannot be pushed forward like ordinary tangent vectors.
Dual Representations

Normals Transform As Duals Of Tangent Blades

Common Mistake

A normal vector is often the dual of a tangent bivector. Under a nonuniform map, pushing it forward as \(Mn\) usually breaks perpendicularity.

\(n' = \det(M)\,M^{-T}n\)
Make this slide concrete by naming the programming error it prevents. Surface tangents are direct objects, so their bivector transforms by the outermorphism. The normal is the dual measurement that annihilates those tangents, so it must transform by the determinant times inverse transpose rule. Ask students to check perpendicularity against both transformed tangent directions.
Contractions Under Maps

Metric Operations Pay For The Adjoint

Identity To Remember

The outer product commutes cleanly with the outermorphism. Contraction uses the metric, so the contracted vector is pulled through the inverse adjoint.

\(M(a \,\lrcorner\, B) = (M^{-T}a)\,\lrcorner\,((\Lambda^2M)B)\)
Use the commuting diagram to show why contraction is the chapter's first genuinely metric-sensitive operation. Students already know the outer product transforms by moving factors. Contraction compares a vector with a blade using the metric, so the comparison vector must be adjusted by the inverse adjoint when the blade is transformed. The diagram gives two paths to the same vector result.
Inverse Maps

An Invertible Map Has Inverse Outermorphisms In Every Grade

Grade-Wise Invertibility

If \(M\) is invertible, then each exterior-power action is invertible and returns the original blade.

\(\Lambda^k(M^{-1}) = (\Lambda^k M)^{-1}\)

Geometric Reading

Undo the transformed generators first; the rebuilt blade returns with its orientation and signed scale restored.

Make the inverse map feel grade-wise, not merely vector-wise. If the vector map has an inverse, then applying the inverse to each transformed generator rebuilds the original blade. The formula involving exterior powers is the matrix version of that statement. Mention that this is also why singular maps are special: once a blade is collapsed, no inverse blade can recover the lost dimension.

The best sanity check is composition: transforming by M and then N should agree with the exterior power of the product. This detects many subtle basis-order and sign mistakes.
Recap And Debugging

What To Check When A Transformed Subspace Looks Wrong

1. Generators

Did the code transform every vector factor before rebuilding the blade?

2. Grade Matrix

Is the grade-two action built from minors rather than reused from the vector matrix?

3. Determinant

Does the top-grade output match signed volume scaling, including orientation reversal?

4. Dual Objects

Are normals and constraints moved by the inverse adjoint rule rather than pushed forward naively?

Chapter 4 Mantra

Subspace transformations are reliable when every grade action is compatible with the outer product and every metric operation accounts for the adjoint.

vectors -> blades -> volumes -> dual measurements
Close by giving students a practical debugging checklist. The chapter's ideas become useful when a transformed subspace, area, volume, or normal looks suspicious in code. Walk through the four questions and emphasize that each one is geometric: rebuild from transformed generators, use the correct grade matrix, check signed top-grade scaling, and treat dual measurements with the inverse adjoint.
