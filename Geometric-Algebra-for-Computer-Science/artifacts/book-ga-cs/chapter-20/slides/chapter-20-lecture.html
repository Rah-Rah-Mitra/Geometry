Open by making the contract explicit: this lecture is about implementation interfaces, not a new algebraic product. The chapter asks what happens after basis blades are already computable. We freeze an ordered basis, store multivectors as coordinates, and then decide when a matrix representation is helpful and when sparse distribution is the better engineering path.

Chapter route

Everything Is Linear Once The Basis Order Is Fixed

01

Linear operations

Addition and scalar multiplication are coordinate operations. Reversion, grade involution, conjugation, and projection are diagonal matrices.

Linear products

With A fixed, the map B ↦ A B is a matrix. Outer and contraction products use the same routing with different grade filters.

Sparse implementation

The practical representation stores only nonzero weighted basis blades and distributes each product over the nonzero term pairs.

Use this route slide to prevent students from treating the matrix construction as the whole chapter. The matrix view is a translation layer that exposes linearity and structure coefficients. The sparse list view is the implementation habit that keeps geometric algebra practical when most multivectors have only a few active basis blades.

Linear operations

Add And Scale Coordinates, Not Geometry Rules

03

Addition

\( A+B \leftrightarrow \llbracket A \rrbracket + \llbracket B \rrbracket \)

Entries add only because the same index names the same basis blade in both columns.

Scalar multiplication

\( \alpha A \leftrightarrow \alpha \llbracket A \rrbracket \)

Scaling keeps the support pattern and grade support. It changes magnitudes, not basis identities.

Implementation habit

Before testing a product, test these trivial operations. If coordinate alignment is wrong here, every later matrix will look mysteriously wrong.

Keep this slide deliberately simple. Addition and scalar multiplication are the sanity checks for the representation layer. They only work entrywise because both columns use the same L list. When students later see product matrices, remind them that the matrix is more complicated, but the coordinate contract underneath has not changed.

Stress the type distinction: A is a multivector, while the displayed matrix is the linear map obtained by fixing A as the left multiplier. This distinction prevents a common implementation mistake where the product itself is imagined as a single universal matrix. The matrix changes with A, but its sparsity pattern and routing rule come from basis-blade products.

Matrix assembly

Structure Coefficients Route Basis Products Into Matrix Cells

07

Basis product oracle

\( L_k L_j = \sum_i s_i^{k,j}L_i \)

In a diagonal Euclidean metric the sum usually has one term. In a general metric it may branch.

Matrix routing

\( (A_G)_{ij} = \sum_k s_i^{k,j} A_k \)

The row is the output basis blade, the column is the input basis blade, and k chooses the active coordinate of A.

geometric: 64 structure rows outer: 27 rows left contraction: 27 rows right contraction: 27 rows
Describe the routing as a sparse tensor lookup rather than as a mysterious formula. The pair of basis blades tells us which output basis blade appears and with what signed metric coefficient. Matrix assembly then places that scaled A coordinate in the cell where the corresponding B coordinate will multiply it during ordinary matrix-vector multiplication.

Product tables

Outer And Contraction Matrices Are Filtered Product Tables

08

Grade filters

  • Outer product keeps grade \(r+s\).
  • Left contraction keeps grade \(s-r\) when \(r\le s\).
  • Right contraction keeps grade \(r-s\) when \(r\ge s\).
KindStructure rowsInterpretation
geometric64all routed basis products
outer27highest-grade selection
left contraction27right grade reduced by left grade
right contraction27left grade reduced by right grade
Frame these as tables that differ by grade policy. The geometric product table routes every allowed basis product, while outer and contraction tables suppress rows that do not land in their target grade. This is why the matrices look related but not interchangeable, and why a test suite should compare each product kind separately.

Contractions

Left And Right Contractions Ask Opposite Grade Questions

09

Grade rules for homogeneous inputs

For left grade r and right grade s, left contraction keeps s-r. Right contraction keeps r-s. Impossible negative grades become zero.

Vector-blade split from the notebook

\( aB = (a\rfloor B) + (a\wedge B) \)

The sample geometric product separates into vector terms plus a trivector term.

Contractions are where grade bookkeeping pays off. For a vector against a bivector, the left contraction keeps grade one and the outer product keeps grade three, so the geometric product visibly splits. Then contrast this with right contraction: it is not a synonym but the grade question asked from the other side of the product.

General metrics

A Basis Product Can Branch When The Metric Is Not Diagonal

10

Diagonal shortcut

With an orthogonal basis, a basis-blade product often lands in one output blade with a sign.

General rule

\( L_kL_j=\sum_i s_i^{k,j}L_i \)

The algorithm does not assume one output. It adds each term to row i, column j.

Recorded example

The notebook's non-diagonal metric gives e1 e2 = 0.25 + e12, then verifies matrix and sparse products agree to about 1.72e-15.

This slide is a useful warning against overfitting to Euclidean diagonal examples. The clean sign-and-mask rule from basis blades remains valuable, but a non-diagonal metric can send one basis product into a small sum. The implementation should therefore store product results as sparse terms even when most classroom examples collapse to one blade.

Sparse list approach

Distribute Over Nonzero Weighted Basis Blades

11

Sparse product loop

result = empty sparse map for (ak, Lk) in nonzero(A): for (bj, Lj) in nonzero(B): for (si, Li) in product_terms(Lk, Lj): result[Li] += ak * bj * si simplify(result)

Why this matters

A dense 5D operator matrix has 1024 slots. The notebook's 5D sparse example checks 16 term pairs and still matches the matrix result.

Connect the sparse loop to distributivity, then immediately connect it to cost. The dense matrix method is excellent for deriving and bootstrapping symbolic recipes, but a typical geometric object or versor has limited support. Sparse loops spend work on active basis-blade pairs, then simplify duplicates, which is the path toward the optimizations later chapters specialize.

Test categories

  • Identity checks: projection sums and involutions.
  • Column checks: scalar input exposes the operator's first column.
  • Parity checks: matrix products and sparse products agree.
  • Metric checks: diagonal assumptions do not sneak into general code.

Design principle

Every product implementation should be tested against a second representation. Matrix and sparse routes make excellent independent witnesses.

Present the checks as reusable engineering assets. They are not just confirmation that one notebook ran once; they encode invariants future implementations can steal. In particular, comparing matrix and sparse products is powerful because the two paths fail for different reasons. The non-diagonal metric check is especially useful for catching hidden orthogonality assumptions.

Structural exercises

Four Prompts For Reading A Product Matrix

13

First column

Why do the geometric and outer product matrices reveal the left factor when the input column is the scalar basis blade?

Outer triangularity

In grade-first order, why does the outer product never move an input to a lower grade block?

Vector-blade split

Where can the decomposition \(aB = a\rfloor B + a\wedge B\) be seen in the product tables?

Right contraction

How should the routing change when the grade reduction is read from the left factor instead of the right factor?

Use these as active prompts rather than answers to recite. The goal is for students to read a product matrix geometrically: first by basis order, then by grade blocks, then by the surviving cells induced by a product rule. The right contraction prompt is a useful final check because it forces them to reverse the grade comparison deliberately.

Recap

Linear Products Become Reliable Software Through Three Contracts

14

1. Coordinate contract

The basis list L fixes meaning. All coordinate arrays, projectors, sign operations, and product matrices depend on that shared ordering.

2. Routing contract

Structure coefficients route products from basis-pair inputs into output coordinates. Product variants are grade selections of those routes.

3. Verification contract

Matrix and sparse implementations should agree. Projection, first-column, contraction, and metric tests make the agreement inspectable.

Bridge to the next implementation chapters

Dense matrices explain the algebra. Sparse lists respect typical geometric support. Specialized kernels then remove the remaining overhead without changing the mathematical contract.

Close by tying the matrix view and sparse view together rather than ranking one as universally better. The matrix view is the conceptual and symbolic scaffold; the sparse view is the practical default for typical multivectors. Later specialization should be presented as preserving these contracts while compiling away unnecessary loops and zero work.
