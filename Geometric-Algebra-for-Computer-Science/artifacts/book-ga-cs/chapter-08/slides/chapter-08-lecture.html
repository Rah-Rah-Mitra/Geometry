Open by naming the chapter's central move: differentiation is not a single operation until the allowed variation has been chosen. A scalar parameter, a vector direction, and a multivector perturbation all produce local change, but they probe different domains. The visual deliberately puts those three channels on one stage so students can keep the syntax tied to geometric meaning.
First decision

Choose The Domain Of Variation

Definition Pattern
A derivative in GA starts by declaring the line of perturbation, then measuring the first-order part of the output.
$$D_A F(X)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{F(X+hA)-F(X)}{h}$$
Scalar\(X=X(t)\), one parameter moves through the algebra.
Vector\(X=x\), the direction \(a\) selects one tangent probe.
Multivector\(X=M\), a blade direction \(A\) can carry any grade.
Use this slide to slow down the notation before formulas multiply. The same limit expression is being reused, but the object \(A\) lives in the domain being probed. This is the conceptual safeguard against treating vector derivatives, parametric derivatives, and multivector derivatives as unrelated recipes. The class should see a derivative as the first-order coefficient along a declared perturbation line.
Scalar path, multivector value

Rotor Motion Differentiates To A Commutator

Rotor path
For \(R(\epsilon)=\exp(-\epsilon B/2)\), the sandwich action has a first-order term controlled by the bivector generator.
$$R(\epsilon)X\widetilde{R(\epsilon)}=X+\epsilon\,(X\times B)+O(\epsilon^2)$$
  • The derivative is a tangent to a rotation orbit, not a new coordinate table.
  • The same commutator moves vectors, bivectors, and mixed multivectors.
  • Finite-angle error scales quadratically when the first-order model is right.
Present the commutator as the first major payoff of geometric algebra. A small rotor does not require separate matrix derivatives for every grade. The bivector generator tells which oriented plane is turning, and the commutator supplies the tangent velocity of the transformed object. Emphasize that the approximation is local: the quadratic remainder is evidence that the derivative captures exactly the first-order part.
Parametric differentiation

Multivector-Valued Functions Keep Their Geometry

Area-rate example
If \(A(t)=r(t)\wedge v(t)\), then the derivative is still a bivector: an oriented area element changing in time.
$$\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(r(t)\wedge v(t)\bigr)=r'(t)\wedge v(t)+r(t)\wedge v'(t)$$
Teaching point
Differentiate the construction. Coordinates are an audit trail; the blade type explains what the derivative means.
Make the product rule feel familiar and new at the same time. The algebraic rule is ordinary bilinearity, but the result is not merely a pair of component derivatives. The wedge makes the derivative an oriented area-rate, so students should inspect the parallelogram and ask how changing either edge changes the blade. This prepares them for rotor and surface calculations where the value is naturally multivector-valued.
Directional change

Inversion Couples Radial And Angular Motion

Stress test
Perturb \(x\) in direction \(a\). The inverse point need not move parallel to \(a\).
$$D_a(x^{-1})=-x^{-1} a x^{-1}$$
  • The radial part changes the scale of the inverse.
  • The tangential part swings the inverse direction.
  • The geometric product keeps both effects in one expression.
Treat inversion as a diagnostic example because it defeats the intuition that a directional derivative should point where the input perturbation points. A small move of \(x\) decomposes into radial and angular effects after inversion. The compact formula \(-x^{-1} a x^{-1}\) is valuable because it keeps both effects in product order. Ask students to locate \(x\), \(a\), \(x^{-1}\), and the derivative arrow before reading the equation.
Collect all coordinate probes

The Vector Derivative Is A Basis-Summed Operator

$$\partial_x F=\sum_i e_i\,D_{e_i}F(x)$$
Two anchors
\(\partial_x x=n\) in \(n\) Euclidean dimensions, while \(\partial_x(x^2)=2x\). Familiar answers emerge from a product-valued operator.
  • Each \(D_{e_i}\) is an ordinary directional finite probe.
  • Multiplication by \(e_i\) recombines the probes geometrically.
  • The result can contain scalar, vector, bivector, and higher-grade parts.
The purpose here is to demystify the vector derivative by building it from directional derivatives the students already understand. In an orthonormal basis, each coordinate direction gets probed, then the basis vector is multiplied on the left before summing. That last multiplication is why the operator can return several grades. The identity and squared-length examples are familiar landmarks, but the grade-rich output is the new geometric feature.
Classical operators inside one product

Gradient, Divergence, And Curl Are Grade Selections

Scalar-valued input
For \(f(x)\), the vector derivative \(\partial f\) is the gradient vector.
Vector-valued input
For \(F(x)\), the scalar part \(\langle \partial F\rangle_0\) records divergence, while the bivector part \(\langle \partial F\rangle_2\) records oriented circulation.
$$\partial F=\partial\cdot F+\partial\wedge F$$
Connect the GA operator to vocabulary students already know. The gradient is not a separate symbol system when the function is scalar-valued. For a vector field, the geometric product of \(\partial\) and \(F\) separates into a scalar part and a bivector part. The scalar part measures local expansion or compression, while the bivector part measures oriented circulation in coordinate planes. This is the clean GA replacement for switching between grad, div, and curl notations.
Full algebra perturbations

Multivector Differentiation Follows The Object

Blade-coordinate view
A multivector is a point in the vector space with basis \(1,e_1,e_2,e_3,e_{12},e_{13},e_{23},I\). Perturbing one blade coordinate is a directional derivative.
$$F(M)=MC,\qquad D_A F(M)=AC$$
  • Rotors move in even-grade directions.
  • Planes move in bivector directions.
  • Volume elements move in pseudoscalar directions.
This slide broadens the domain beyond vectors. Students often meet rotors, bivectors, and pseudoscalars as special objects, then unconsciously differentiate only vector coordinates. The blade atlas makes the domain explicit: the full algebra is a vector space, so every basis blade gives a legitimate perturbation direction. The linear check \(F(M)=MC\) is deliberately simple because it isolates the idea that the derivative along \(A\) is \(AC\).
First-order model

Local Linearization Is The Shared Payoff

$$F(X+hA)=F(X)+h\,D_A F(X)+O(h^2)$$
Spherical projection
For \(P(x)=x/\|x\|\), local change lives in the tangent plane. The radial component is invisible to first order.
$$D_aP(x)=\frac{(a\wedge x)x^{-1}}{\|x\|}$$
Course memory
Every section of the chapter is a way to build this local linear model for the correct geometric domain.
Close the technical arc by returning to first-order approximation. Whether the variable is a scalar parameter, a vector, or a multivector, the derivative is the coefficient that makes \(F(X+hA)\) locally predictable. Spherical projection is a strong final picture because it visibly rejects radial motion and keeps only the tangent component. That is the chapter's habit in miniature: identify the meaningful local direction, compute its first-order effect, and then check that the remainder is second order.
Instructor synthesis

What Students Should Carry Forward

One calculus, several domains
Scalar paths, vector directions, and blade-coordinate perturbations share the same local-linear logic.
Products explain geometry
Commutators describe infinitesimal turns; wedges describe oriented area and tangent rejection; geometric products combine scale and direction.
Grade selection is interpretation
The vector derivative contains the classical gradient, divergence, and curl information without changing languages.
