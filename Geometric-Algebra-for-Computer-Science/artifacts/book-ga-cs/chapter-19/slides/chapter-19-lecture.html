Bitmap convention

Blade Addresses

Representation Contract
A basis blade is stored as BasisBlade(bitmap, scale). The bitmap names the canonical blade; the scale carries weight and orientation.
Grade Test
\( \operatorname{grade}(E_a)=\operatorname{popcount}(a) \)

The grade is not recomputed from a symbolic label. It is the number of set bits in the address.

Human Label

0101 decodes to e1^e3, even though the printed binary string places bit zero on the right.

Original visual: bitmap and grade mapChapter 19 basis-blade layer
Emphasize that the bitmap is an address, not a visual drawing of the blade. Bit zero names e1 even though it prints on the right side of a binary string. Once that convention is accepted, grade counting, table indexing, and dependency tests become ordinary integer operations.
Integer operations with geometric meaning

Bit Tests Become Algebra Tests

Grade: bit_count()

Two set bits means a bivector address.

Overlap: a & b

A nonzero overlap means repeated basis-vector factors.

Output Address: a ^ b

Xor combines independent factors and removes common ones.

Original visual: bit masks as algebra tests&, ^, and popcount are the kernel
Use the three bit operations as the computational vocabulary for the chapter. Popcount reads grade, bitwise and detects shared basis-vector factors, and exclusive-or gives the canonical output address after common factors are removed. The arithmetic looks small because the convention has already stored the algebraic ordering.
Canonical ordering

Swap Parity Gives The Sign

Sign Rule
\( \operatorname{sign}(a,b)=(-1)^{N(a,b)} \)

Here N(a,b) counts pairs (i,j) with i in the left bitmap, j in the right bitmap, and i>j.

Why It Matters

The output bitmap can be correct while the coefficient is wrong. A single missed inversion changes signs throughout product tables, contractions, commutators, and sparse products.

Original visual: swap arcs and parity casesCanonical order: e1 before e2 before e3
Slow down here because sign bugs are contagious. Concatenate the left blade factors with the right blade factors, then count how many inversions must be swapped to restore canonical order. Odd inversion count flips the coefficient; even inversion count preserves it, independent of the eventual metric factor.
Exterior product of basis blades

Outer Product Fast Path

Basis-Blade Rule
$$E_a \wedge E_b = \begin{cases} 0, & a \mathbin{\&} b \ne 0,\\ \operatorname{sign}(a,b)E_{a\oplus b}, & a \mathbin{\&} b = 0. \end{cases}$$
Example

e2^e3 \wedge e1 = +e1^e2^e3 because the masks are disjoint and the two required swaps give a positive sign.

Zero Case

e1 \wedge e1^e3 = 0 because the shared e1 bit means the candidate span is dependent.

Original visual: outer-product decision gateDependence test: a & b
Present the outer product as the cleanest basis-blade operation. A shared bit means the candidate spanning set repeats a basis vector, so the outer product is zero. With no shared bits, the output bitmap is just xor and the coefficient is only the input scales times the parity sign.
Geometric product in a diagonal metric

Orthogonal Metric Factors

Diagonal Formula
\( E_aE_b=\operatorname{sign}(a,b)\left(\prod_{i\in a\mathbin{\&}b}g_{ii}\right)E_{a\oplus b} \)
Euclidean Fast Path

When all diagonal entries are +1, the metric factor disappears and the product remains a signed single blade.

Mixed Signature

For diag(1,-1,1), the repeated e2 bit contributes -1, so e2e2=-1.

Original visual: metric factor extractionGeometric product keeps duplicate bits meaningful
Connect the outer-product skeleton to the geometric product. In an orthogonal metric, repeated bits no longer force zero; they collapse into diagonal metric entries. This keeps the result as a single basis blade, which explains why diagonal Euclidean or mixed-signature tables are fast and compact.

Reference products from the geometric product

Metric Products By Grade Selection

Grade Projection
\( \langle AB\rangle_k \)

Basis-blade products can feed scalar products, contractions, and grade-filtered operations by selecting output grades.

Notebook Checks

Small cases such as \(e1 \rfloor e123 = e23\) and \(e12 \cdot e12 = -1\) make good implementation tests.

Original visual: grade-selection operation diagnosticsReference path before specialization
Explain scalar products, contractions, and commutators as reference operations built on top of the geometric product. The implementation can be optimized later, but grade selection gives a readable specification. If a faster contraction routine disagrees on these small basis-blade cases, the faster routine is suspect.
When one blade pair produces several terms

Nonorthogonal Metrics Expand

Null Basis Metric
$$G = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1\\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
Expansion
\( o\,\infty = -1 + o\wedge\infty \)
\( \infty\,o = -1 - o\wedge\infty \)
Storage Consequence

The product result is a sparse dictionary such as {0: -1, 3: 1}, not one weighted address.

Original visual: nonorthogonal expansion branchOff-diagonal metric entries create lower-grade terms
This is the counterexample to any one-output-bitmap assumption. In a null or skew basis, a vector multiplication can produce an outer part and a contraction part at the same time. A product of two basis blades may therefore be a sparse dictionary, not a single weighted blade.
From basis-blade products to sparse multivectors

Sparse Multivectors Distribute

Sparse Representation
\( A = \sum_a A_aE_a,\quad B = \sum_b B_bE_b \)
\( AB = \sum_{a,b} A_aB_b(E_aE_b) \)
Why Reference Code Matters

The direct double loop is not the fastest possible method, but it is transparent enough to validate optimized tables, generated kernels, and matrix product operators.

Original visual: sparse-product distribution gridBasis-blade product is the inner kernel
Move from one blade pair to the sparse multivector layer. Each nonzero term of A multiplies each nonzero term of B, and equal output bitmaps must be merged. This is intentionally direct: it is slow enough to optimize later but transparent enough to trust as a reference.
Cheap unary operations

Unary Signs Are Grade Tables

Closed Forms
\( \operatorname{reverse}: (-1)^{k(k-1)/2} \)
\( \operatorname{grade\;involution}: (-1)^k \)
\( \operatorname{Clifford\;conjugation}: (-1)^{k(k+1)/2} \)
Implementation Point

Once k = popcount(bitmap), these operations need no basis-index loop. They only multiply coefficients by grade-dependent signs.

Original visual: grade-dependent sign tableUnary operations are popcount plus coefficient sign
Point out how much implementation work disappears once grade is a popcount. Reversion, grade involution, and Clifford conjugation do not inspect the individual basis-vector indices; they use periodic grade-dependent signs. These tiny tables are excellent smoke tests for off-by-one and integer-width mistakes.
Implementation checklist

Operation Diagnostics

1. Decode

Every printed label must round-trip through bitmap indices.

2. Sign

Canonical reordering signs must match inversion parity.

3. Duplicate Bits

Outer products zero duplicates; geometric products metric them.

4. Metric Branch

General metrics must allow sparse sums of output blades.

5. Cleanup

Tiny coefficients need an explicit simplification policy.

6. Table Tests

Associativity and grade patterns should pass on small bases.

Original visual: operation diagnostics dashboardMake the low-level layer boringly reliable
Turn the final slide sequence into a debugging checklist for a geometric algebra library. Every product routine should be checked against bit order, parity, duplicate handling, metric path, grade projection, coefficient cleanup, and associativity on a small table before it is trusted inside larger geometric algorithms.
