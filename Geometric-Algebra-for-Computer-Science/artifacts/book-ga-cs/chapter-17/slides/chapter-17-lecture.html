Operational criterion

Covariance Is the Smoke Test

02 / 14

What must commute?

  • Construction: join, meet, product, projection, round from points.
  • Operator: rotation, translation, projective map, inversion, contact map.
  • Predicate: distance, incidence, angle, tangency, cross-ratio.
\(V[A \circ B] = V[A] \circ V[B]\)

If this identity fails for a promised operation, the model is asking the implementation to manage special cases.

Use the square as the governing test for every model in the lecture. If an object is constructed from inputs and then transformed, the result should agree with transforming the inputs first and reconstructing. That is what makes a representation operational instead of merely coordinate storage.

Representation space is engineered

Lifts Are Paid For by Invariants

04 / 14

Design question

Does the lift make the desired operators linear, orthogonal, versor-like, or at least invariant under a clean predicate?

Good lift Translations become matrix products; distances become metric pairings; lines become native objects.
Bad lift Coordinates grow, but every important operation still needs exceptions and conversions.
Walk upward through the ladder and ask what each added coordinate or constraint buys. A lift is justified when it turns the required transformations into simple algebraic actions or makes the desired predicates invariant. Without that payoff, extra coordinates are just extra testing burden.
Route 1

Vector-Space Model: Directions Win

05 / 14

Use when the origin is structural

  • Objects: directions, oriented subspaces, normals, attitudes.
  • Operators: orthogonal maps, reflections, rotors.
  • Predicates: length, angle, orientation, projection.
\(x \mapsto R x R^{-1}\)

Tradeoff: a pure translation moves the origin, so it is not a linear operator in the same space.

Treat the vector-space model as the lean baseline, not a primitive leftover. It is excellent when the task is angular, directional, or fixed-origin. Rotors and orthogonal maps preserve the metric on directions, but finite translations and incidence need bookkeeping outside the native model.
Route 2

Homogeneous Model: Incidence Wins

06 / 14

Use when joins and projections are central

  • Objects: finite points, points at infinity, lines, planes.
  • Operators: affine maps, camera projections, projective maps.
  • Predicates: incidence, collinearity, meet, horizon behavior.
\(P=[x:y:1],\quad \ell=P\wedge Q,\quad X=\ell_1\wedge\ell_2\)

Tradeoff: scale classes support incidence, but raw dot products are not Euclidean distances.

Point out that homogeneous coordinates are not only a computer-graphics convention. They make affine and projective transformations linear, and they make join and meet operations uniform. The tradeoff is equally important: the raw coordinate dot product is not Euclidean distance.
Route 3

Plucker Model: Lines Become First-Class

07 / 14

Use when rays and lines are the data

  • Objects: oriented 3D lines, camera rays, screw axes.
  • Operators: transforms inherited from homogeneous/projective space.
  • Predicates: validity, intersection, coplanarity, closest approach.
\(L=(d,m),\quad m=p\times d,\quad d\cdot m=0\)

Tradeoff: excellent for line geometry, but point distances and round objects require another layer.

Use Plucker coordinates as the line-first cousin of the homogeneous story. A ray or line can carry direction and moment together, so line incidence and closest-approach tests become first-class. It is powerful for cameras and robotics, but it is not a universal metric model of points.
Route 4

Conformal Model: Distance and Rounds Win

08 / 14

Use when metric objects must move together

  • Objects: points, lines, planes, point pairs, circles, spheres.
  • Operators: translations, rotations, dilations, inversions, screw-like motions.
  • Predicates: distance, tangency, sphere membership, incidence.
\(X(x)=x+n_o+\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^2 n_\infty,\quad X\cdot Y=-\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2\)

Tradeoff: the model is expressive because the metric is larger and more delicate.

Present conformal geometry as the route that pays two null directions to make distance and round objects algebraic. The same operator pattern can move points, lines, circles, spheres, and planes. The cost is a larger representation and stricter metric discipline.
Beyond the familiar catalog

Other Operational Models Start from New Invariants

09 / 14

The method generalizes

For a new domain, do not begin by asking which coordinates are convenient. Begin by asking which transformations define sameness.

Image Mix spatial motion with value-domain transformations.
Projective Seek operators that make projective invariants native.
Contact Represent tangency and collision constraints directly.
Custom Design the metric around the invariant, then test it.
This slide is deliberately speculative, matching the chapter's forward-looking tone. Image, projective, and contact geometries suggest that operational models are a method, not a finished catalog. The right question is which invariants and transformations deserve to be native for the application.
Decision workflow

Route by Native Work, Not by Habit

10 / 14

1. Workload

Name the repeated operation: estimate attitude, project camera rays, collide shapes, interpolate motions.

2. Objects

List what must survive: directions, points, lines, rounds, contact states, image values.

3. Predicates

Choose the invariant: angle, incidence, distance, tangency, cross-ratio, or value smoothness.

4. Operators

Pick the model where common transformations are natural and easy to test.

Run the decision chart as a live diagnostic. Begin with the workload, identify the objects that must survive transformations, then route by native predicates and operator cost. The class should see model choice as an engineering decision with tests, not a matter of taste.

Testing model promises

Measure the Geometry, Not the Storage Accident

12 / 14

Practical checks

  • Vector: dot products and norms are invariant under orthogonal operators.
  • Homogeneous: incidence survives scale and projective motion.
  • Plucker: line validity and intersection predicates survive transforms.
  • Conformal: nullness, infinity, and pairwise distances survive motions.
A coordinate array is not a geometry until its meaningful predicates are declared and tested.
Use the testing harness to prevent a common mistake: measuring the representation space as though it were automatically physical space. Each model has predicates that are meaningful and coordinate quantities that are merely storage artifacts. Good software names and tests that boundary explicitly.
Bridge to Part III

Operational Contract Comes Before Kernel Design

13 / 14

What carries forward?

  • The algebraic product tables must match the chosen metric.
  • Specialized coordinate types should encode model promises.
  • Performance matters after the invariant contract is explicit.
  • Tests should exercise operators, not just storage formats.

The next implementation chapters can be read as a response to this deck: once a model is operational, make it efficient without breaking its predicates.

Connect Chapter 17 to the next part of the book. Once the operational contract is chosen, implementation work becomes basis choices, product tables, specialized kernels, and validation. The algebraic elegance matters only if the resulting code preserves the promised operations efficiently.
Summary

Choose the Algebra by the Work It Must Preserve

14 / 14

1. Start

Name the operators that define sameness for the geometry.

2. Represent

Choose the space where those operators become natural actions.

3. Construct

Use products, joins, meets, or lifts that commute with operators.

4. Predicate

Declare what is meaningful: distance, incidence, angle, contact.

5. Test

Write invariants that fail when the wrong model is being used.

Route summary

  • Directions and attitudes: vector-space model.
  • Incidence and projection: homogeneous model.
  • Rays and line geometry: Plucker route.
  • Distances and rounds: conformal model.
  • New invariants: design an operational model.
Close by making the routing habit memorable. Start from operators, choose the representation that makes them native, name the predicates that are invariant, and write checks that fail when the wrong model is used. That is the practical lesson of operational models.
