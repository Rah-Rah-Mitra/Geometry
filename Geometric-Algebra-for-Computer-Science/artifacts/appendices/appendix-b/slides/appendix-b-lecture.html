Four Inner-Product Conventions In One Switchboard

B.1

Decision Rule

\(A_k \rfloor B_l = \langle A_kB_l\rangle_{l-k}\)
\(A_k \lfloor B_l = \langle A_kB_l\rangle_{k-l}\)
\(A_k \bullet B_l = \begin{cases}A_k\rfloor B_l,& k\le l\\ A_k\lfloor B_l,& k\ge l\end{cases}\)

The Hestenes inner product follows the dot branch but returns zero when either input is scalar.

Use the switchboard as the high-level map. Left contraction is deliberately asymmetric, right contraction is its opposite-side partner, the dot product chooses the lower-grade side, and the Hestenes variant adds scalar exceptions. The practical question is which convention leaves the fewest hidden branches in an algorithm.

Left Contraction Leaves The Residual Blade

B.1

Basis-Blade Example

\(e_1 \rfloor (e_1\wedge e_2)=e_2\)

The left operand is accounted for by the metric. The output is the oriented part of the right blade that remains.

\(e_3 \rfloor (e_1\wedge e_2)=0\)

Here zero means that the tested direction was not contained in the plane blade.

Spend time on the visual interpretation before the formulas. A left contraction asks what remains in the larger blade after the left operand has been matched metrically. The vector-plane example is small enough to compute aloud and strong enough to show why zero can carry geometric information.

Right Contraction Makes Dot Products Branch

B.1

Right-Side Residue

\( (e_1\wedge e_2)\lfloor e_2=e_1\)

Dot-Style Branch

if \(k\le l\): use \(A_k\rfloor B_l\)
if \(k\ge l\): use \(A_k\lfloor B_l\)
  • Good for compact formulas when grades are known.
  • Risky when data degenerates and grades become runtime values.
  • Hestenes inner product adds a scalar-input zero rule.

The notebook's agreement cases include vector-on-bivector, bivector-on-vector, scalar-on-vector, and bivector-on-bivector diagnostics.

Present the right contraction as the same selection idea viewed from the other side, then show why the dot product feels symmetric in many examples. The branch line is useful for compact notation, but it also means the operation changes behavior when input grades cross.

Grade Selection Predicts Every Surviving Lane

B.1

Read The Matrix

  • Left contraction: output grade \(l-k\), otherwise zero.
  • Right contraction: output grade \(k-l\), otherwise zero.
  • Dot product: use whichever side has lower grade.
  • Hestenes: same as dot product, except scalar inputs vanish.

The notebook generated a 64-pair grade-behavior table over basis blades in \(R^3\).

Read the grid as an implementation contract rather than as a table to memorize. Each colored lane tells a programmer which grade may survive. The important point is that left and right contractions preserve containment tests, while dot-style products move that decision into a grade comparison.

Agreement Happens In The Lowest-Grade-First Zone

B.1

Shared Zone

\(A\bullet B=A\rfloor B=A\bullet_HB\)

Use this when \(0<\operatorname{grade}(A)\le\operatorname{grade}(B)\). This is why many formulas translate cleanly between books.

A Vector Contracts Through A Blade By Deleting Generators

B.2

Alternating Deletion Formula

\(x\rfloor(a_1\wedge\cdots\wedge a_k)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i-1}(x\cdot a_i)\,a_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{a_i}\wedge\cdots\wedge a_k\)
Notebook check: \((2e_1-3e_2+5e_3)\rfloor(e_1\wedge e_2)=3e_1+2e_2\)

The hatted generator is omitted from the wedge.

Work the alternating sum as a deletion rule. The vector tests each generator by the metric, removes that generator from the blade, and keeps the signed leftover span. The notebook check gives a concrete result that students can recompute from two dot products and one sign change.

The Implicit Definition Is An Adjoint Test

B.2

Scalar-Product Pairing

\(X*(A\rfloor B)=(X\wedge A)*B\)

Contraction is the operation paired with wedging by \(A\) under the scalar product. In a nondegenerate metric, equality against all \(X\) identifies the result.

explicit rules \(\Rightarrow\) implicit test \(\Rightarrow\) equivalence when the metric is nondegenerate
Frame the implicit definition as an adjoint relationship with the outer product. Instead of treating contraction as a bag of special cases, the scalar product test says that wedging on one side and contracting on the other are paired operations. Nondegenerate metrics make this pairing unique.

Second Duality Needs A Containment Hypothesis

B.3

Identity And Proof Road

\((A\rfloor B)\rfloor C=A\wedge(B\rfloor C)\quad\text{when }A\subseteq C\)
0Handle scalar edge cases.
1Verify the vector seed identity.
2-3Extend through one vector and then a blade.
4Induct to the full blade \(A\).
Use the containment picture to keep the duality statement from becoming pure symbol pushing. The condition that A sits inside C is not cosmetic; it prevents extra terms from appearing. The proof ladder shows how a vector identity grows into a blade identity by induction.

Projection Is Contraction Followed By Blade Division

B.4

Projection Formula

\(P_B(x)=(x\rfloor B)B^{-1}\)
Example: \(x=2e_1+3e_2+4e_3,\; B=e_1\wedge e_2\)
projection\(2e_1+3e_2\)
rejection\(4e_3\)
check\(P+R=x,\; R\rfloor B=0\)
Connect the appendix proof back to the geometric operation students already use. Contracting into a non-null blade and multiplying by the inverse blade returns the projected component, while the norm relation says the contraction length measures the size of that component scaled by the blade.

Implementation Cautions Are Mathematical Cautions

B.1-B.4

Branch Points To Test

  • Grade order: \(k<l\), \(k=l\), and \(k>l\) must be separate cases.
  • Scalar inputs: dot and Hestenes conventions disagree.
  • Degenerate blades: inverse-based projection may fail.
  • Aliases: a displayed dot must name its convention.
Notebook artifact: 64 basis-blade pairs, zero convention failures.
This slide should feel like a code review checklist. Grade branches, scalar exceptions, degenerate blades, and convention aliases are not philosophical details; they decide whether a geometric algebra package returns zero, a lower-grade blade, or an exception. The notebook artifacts make those decisions testable.
