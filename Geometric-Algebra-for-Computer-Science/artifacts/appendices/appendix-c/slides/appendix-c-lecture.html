Open the lecture by making the retrieval theme explicit. Appendix C is not adding a new product; it is showing that the geometric product already contains the familiar subspace operations, provided we inspect the right grade component and remember the metric and duality conventions.

The Retrieval Contract

grade selectors
$$A_r \wedge B_s = \langle A_rB_s\rangle_{r+s}$$
$$A_r \rfloor B_s = \langle A_rB_s\rangle_{s-r}$$
$$A_r \lfloor B_s = \langle A_rB_s\rangle_{r-s}$$
$$A_r * B_s = \langle A_rB_s\rangle_{0}$$

Reading The Four Lines

  • Use homogeneous grades first; multivectors are handled component by component.
  • A requested negative grade or a grade above the ambient dimension contributes zero.
  • The top grade is exterior information; lower grades are metric information.
  • Projection, rejection, meet, and join are built by composing these retrievals.
outer left contraction right contraction scalar part
Present these equations as the contract for the whole deck. The notation says: multiply first, then select the grade that has the geometric meaning wanted. Students should notice that invalid target grades are not errors; they are zero contributions in the same algebraic bookkeeping.

Selection Is A Filter, Not A New Multiplication

The geometric product mixes grade-raising and grade-lowering effects. Appendix C's point is that the named subspace products are already present as stable grade slices of that mixed product.

Top grade Detects the independent span represented by the outer product.
Bottom grade Records how much one factor is measured against the other.
Same grade The scalar part is the blade scalar product.
Wrong grade The requested component simply vanishes.
Slow down here because this is the conceptual hinge. The geometric product may contain several grades at once, so the grade selector acts like a mathematical filter. This lets one computation support exterior spanning, metric comparison, and contraction without pretending those meanings are interchangeable.

Worked Example: Split A Vector-Plane Product

projection + rejection
$$a=e_1+2e_3,\qquad B=e_1\wedge e_2$$
$$aB=e_2+2e_{123}$$
Projection retrieval \(\langle aB\rangle_1=e_2=a\rfloor B\), and \((a\rfloor B)B^{-1}=e_1\).
Rejection retrieval \(\langle aB\rangle_3=2e_{123}=a\wedge B\), and \((a\wedge B)B^{-1}=2e_3\).

The plane inverse converts the blade-valued retrieval back into the vector component.

Walk through this example carefully. The same product aB produces a grade-one contraction and a grade-three exterior component. Multiplying those retrieved pieces by the blade inverse turns them into the familiar vector projection e1 and rejection 2e3, so the diagram matches the algebra.

Join And Meet From The Same Retrieval Habit

duality

Direct Join

$$A\vee_{\mathrm{join}}B \; \leadsto \; A\wedge B=\langle AB\rangle_{r+s}$$

When the represented directions are independent, the outer product is the blade for the span they generate together.

Dual Meet

$$A\cap B \; \leadsto \; \big(A^*\wedge B^*\big)^*$$

Intersect by turning subspaces into constraints, joining the constraints, then dualizing back.

Connect this slide to earlier meet and join ideas without changing conventions midstream. The join is the top-grade exterior part when independence allows it. The meet is retrieved indirectly: dualize to constraints, join those constraints, and dualize back to the represented intersection.

Projection And Rejection Pipeline

retrieval workflow
$$P_B(a)=(a\rfloor B)B^{-1}$$
$$R_B(a)=(a\wedge B)B^{-1}$$
Use this as the operational algorithm. First multiply by the blade, then choose the grade that represents metric contact or span growth, and finally multiply by the blade inverse to return to the original geometric type. This prevents projection from looking like an unrelated formula.

Mixed-Grade Inputs: Distribute First

notebook example

Example Multivectors

$$M=2+e_1+\tfrac12e_{12},\qquad N=e_2+e_{23}$$
$$M\wedge N=2e_2+e_{12}+2e_{23}+e_{123}$$

The notebook recovers exactly the same result by applying the homogeneous grade rule to every grade-pair contribution.

The mixed-grade example is useful because it prevents a common overstatement. The grade formulas are cleanest for homogeneous factors. For general multivectors, split into homogeneous pieces, apply the retrieval rule to each pair, and then add the valid contributions back together.

Invite students to compute these examples aloud, because the signs reveal whether they understand ordering and orientation. The coordinate-plane example is especially instructive: a simple product can encode a turn, while the actual intersection line is better recovered through the dual meet construction.

Proof Roadmap: Why The Grade Rules Work

Appendix C.3

1. Peel A Vector

Write a blade as one vector wedged with a smaller blade, then compare the highest possible grade in the product.

$$A_k=a_k\wedge A_{k-1}$$

2. Discard Impossible Grades

Terms with grade too high or too low cannot contribute to the selected component, so the selector keeps only the intended operation.

$$\langle A_kB_l\rangle_{l+k}=A_k\wedge B_l$$

3. Recurse Through Factors

The contraction identities follow by reducing one factor at a time, proving the earlier blade rules from the grade-selected definition.

$$\langle A_kB_l\rangle_{l-k}=A_k\rfloor B_l$$
Frame the proof as a filtering argument. Each time a vector is peeled from a blade, the possible grades are constrained. The selected component cannot receive contributions from the wrong grade, so induction turns the grade-selected expression into the familiar outer and contraction operations.

Diagnostics: What To Check Before Naming A Product

avoid false retrievals

Common Failure Modes

  • Applying a homogeneous formula to a mixed-grade multivector without splitting it first.
  • Calling every nonzero geometric product an outer product.
  • Forgetting that duality depends on the ambient pseudoscalar and metric convention.
  • Turning a blade-valued projection ingredient into a vector without the blade inverse.

Takeaway: Products Are Stored, Not Replaced

Appendix C recap

The Retrieval Pattern

  1. Multiply blades or homogeneous pieces.
  2. Select the grade with the intended meaning.
  3. Use duality or a blade inverse when the target operation requires it.
  4. Verify by a small symbolic example before trusting a general formula.

The Appendix C notebook checks the retrieval formulas against direct operations, grade support tables, and mixed-grade decompositions.

Close by returning to the main intellectual payoff. Appendix C reduces the number of separate-looking operations students must carry. Once they can multiply, select grades, dualize when needed, and use blade inverses, they can retrieve the major subspace operations from one algebraic source.
