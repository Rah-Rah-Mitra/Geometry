Why The Outer Product Is Not Enough

Nonmetric Data

The outer product records span, orientation, and independence. It deliberately does not ask whether two subspaces are long, short, nearly parallel, or nearly perpendicular.

Metric Data

A scalar product extends the vector dot product to blades of the same grade, so lines, planes, and volumes can be compared in a shared metric.

$$\text{outer product} + \text{metric} \Rightarrow \text{measured subspaces}$$
Emphasize that the outer product already does serious work: it builds lines, planes, and volumes without asking for lengths. The gap appears when a graphics or geometry routine must compare subspaces that are not already the same concrete patch. That is the motivation for adding a scalar-valued product.

Scalar Products Compare Same-Grade Blades

Blade Scalar Product

For two k-blades \(A = a_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge a_k\) and \(B = b_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge b_k\), the comparison is built from all pairwise vector dot products.

$$A * B = \det\left(a_i \cdot b_j\right)_{i,j=1}^{k}$$
  • 1For k = 1, this is the ordinary dot product.
  • 2For k = 2, the determinant compares oriented area elements.
  • 3Norms and angles become determinant statements rather than coordinate guesses.
Present the scalar product as a structured comparison, not as a mysterious new operation. The determinant of pairwise dot products is a compact way to compare all spanning directions at once. Stress that the determinant keeps the orientation-sensitive behavior of blades while introducing metric measurement.

Contraction: A Metric Grade-Lowering Product

Notebook Check

With \(B = e_1 \wedge e_2\) and \(x = 1.2e_1 + 0.55e_2 + 0.8e_3\), contraction plus \(B^{-1}\) recovers the in-plane vector \(1.2e_1 + 0.55e_2\).

$$\operatorname{proj}_{B}(x) = (x \,\lrcorner\, B) B^{-1}$$
Orientation Matters

Left and right contractions agree for vector dot products but can differ by sign once grades differ. The sign records how the operation meets the blade orientation.

Treat contraction as the moment where the scalar product and outer product start talking to each other. In the notebook example, a vector meets an oriented plane and produces an in-plane oriented residue. Multiplying that residue by the inverse blade converts it into the ordinary projected vector.

Duality Describes Orthogonal Complements

Direct Versus Dual

A direct blade stores the subspace itself. Its dual stores the orthogonal complement, using the ambient pseudoscalar as the bridge.

$$A^{*} = A I^{-1} \qquad \text{and} \qquad (A^{*})^{*} \sim A$$
3-D Plane direct bivector: the oriented plane element
Dual Normal vector perpendicular to every plane factor
Use direct and dual representations as two views of the same constraint. The direct blade says which directions span the subspace. The dual says which directions are perpendicular to it. Remind students that duality is metric and ambient-dimensional, because orthogonality and complement size require those choices.

Projection Becomes A Metric Certificate

Executed Notebook Example

For \(x=(1.15,0.85,1.05)\) and basis vectors \((1,0,0)\), \((0.35,1,0)\), the projection is \(p=(1.15,0.85,0)\).

$$x = p + r,\qquad b_i \cdot r = 0$$
  • 1Coordinates in the skew basis are \(0.8525\) and \(0.85\).
  • 2The residual is \((0,0,1.05)\), perpendicular to both spanning vectors.
  • 3The algebraic check is stronger than a visual read-off.
Projection is the first payoff most students recognize. The contraction formula agrees with a Gram solve, but it packages the answer geometrically. Point out the certificate: after projection, the residual has zero dot product with every spanning vector, so the picture has an auditable metric invariant.

The 3-D Cross Product Is A Dual Area

A Useful Special Case

The ordinary cross product works because 3-D Euclidean space lets an oriented area blade be represented by a perpendicular vector.

$$a \times b \quad \text{is the dual of} \quad a \wedge b$$
Programming Habit

Keep the blade as the primary object when possible. It records the plane element directly; the cross-product vector is one metric, dimension-specific encoding.

The cross product slide should feel like a translation guide rather than a nostalgia stop. In geometric algebra, the area blade is the fundamental object. Dualizing it in three dimensions gives the familiar perpendicular vector, while also exposing the dimension and orientation assumptions hidden in classical notation.

Reciprocal Frames Turn Skew Coordinates Into Dot Products

Reciprocal Rule

A reciprocal frame \(\{e^1,e^2,e^3\}\) satisfies \(e_i \cdot e^j = \delta_i^j\). Coordinates are then recovered by dotting with the reciprocal vectors.

$$x = \sum_i (x \cdot e^i)e_i$$
Color-Space Lab

The executed notebook treats RGB triples as vectors, converts them through a skewed primary frame, and checks that the displayed coordinates remain finite and clipped.

Reciprocal frames are a practical endpoint of the chapter because they convert a nonorthonormal coordinate problem into reusable metric data. Once the reciprocal frame is known, coordinate extraction is a set of dot products. The color-space lab makes this computational rather than purely symbolic.

What To Carry Forward

Span Use the outer product to build an oriented subspace from independent directions.
Measure Use scalar products to compare same-grade blades and recover norms or angles.
Contract Lower grade while preserving the metric relation between a blade and a subspace.
Dualize Switch between direct subspace data and orthogonal-complement data.
Certify Test projections, reciprocal frames, and cross-product translations by invariants.
Lecture Synthesis

Metric products let subspaces do measured work. The key programming habit is to test geometric invariants under tilted planes, skewed frames, and changed coordinates.

$$\text{blade} \rightarrow \text{metric comparison} \rightarrow \text{geometric operation} \rightarrow \text{invariant check}$$
Close the deck by moving from individual formulas to workflow. Students should leave with a pipeline: build the blade, choose the metric comparison, apply contraction or duality, then verify an invariant. That final check is what separates geometric algebra code from a coordinate coincidence.
