$\mathrm{Symp}$ and Its Identity Component

A group

Composition and inverses preserve $\omega$.

Identity component

$\mathrm{Symp}_0$ is reached by paths from $\mathrm{id}$.

Topology shows

$\pi_0(\mathrm{Symp})$ already sees the manifold's symmetry.

Definition

$\mathrm{Symp}(M,\omega)=\{\phi\in\mathrm{Diff}(M):\phi^*\omega=\omega\}$

Why infinite-dimensional

The tangent space is a space of vector fields, not a finite matrix algebra.

Printed pp. 385-416Symp and Its Identity Component
Symplectomorphisms form a group under composition because preserving omega is closed under inverses and products. The identity component collects those reachable by a continuous path from the identity. Distinguishing the whole group from this component already records topological information about the manifold and its symmetries.

The Lie Algebra: Closed One-Forms

Symplectic fields

$X$ with $\mathcal{L}_X\omega=0$, equivalently $\iota_X\omega$ closed.

Hamiltonian fields

$\iota_X\omega=dH$ is exact.

Cartan's identity

$\mathcal{L}_X\omega = d\,\iota_X\omega + \iota_X\,d\omega = d\,\iota_X\omega$

The dictionary

Symplectic field $\leftrightarrow$ closed $1$-form; Hamiltonian field $\leftrightarrow$ exact $1$-form.

Printed pp. 385-416The Lie Algebra of Symp
Differentiating a symplectic isotopy gives a vector field whose contraction with omega is a closed one-form. So the Lie algebra of the symplectomorphism group is the symplectic vector fields, identified with closed one-forms. Hamiltonian fields correspond to exact one-forms, the differentials of functions.

Hamiltonian Symplectomorphisms

Generating flow

A time-dependent $H_t$ defines $X_t$ via $\iota_{X_t}\omega=dH_t$.

The map

$\mathrm{Ham}$ collects the time-one maps $\psi_1$.

Time-one map

$\dfrac{d}{dt}\psi_t = X_t\circ\psi_t,\quad \psi_0=\mathrm{id},\quad \phi=\psi_1$

Normal subgroup

$\mathrm{Ham}\trianglelefteq\mathrm{Symp}_0$: conjugation rewrites the generator.

Printed pp. 385-416Hamiltonian Symplectomorphisms
A Hamiltonian symplectomorphism is the time-one map of a flow generated by a time-dependent function. These maps form a normal subgroup Ham inside the identity component. The generating Hamiltonian is the central computational object: its flow preserves the form and, on closed manifolds, conserves nothing extra by accident.

Where the Two Notions Split

Always

Hamiltonian $\Rightarrow$ symplectic.

The gap

Converse needs $[\iota_X\omega]=0$ in $H^1(M;\mathbb{R})$.

Torus example

A constant translation field is symplectic but not Hamiltonian.

Printed pp. 385-416Symplectic versus Hamiltonian Fields
Every Hamiltonian field is symplectic, but the converse fails when the first cohomology is nontrivial. The obstruction is precisely whether the closed one-form i_X omega is exact. On a torus a constant translation field is symplectic yet not Hamiltonian, which is the simplest visible gap.

The Flux Homomorphism

Input

A symplectic isotopy $\{\phi_t\}$ with generators $X_t$.

Output

An accumulated class in $H^1(M;\mathbb{R})/\Gamma$.

Definition

$\mathrm{Flux}(\{\phi_t\}) = \left[\,\int_0^1 \iota_{X_t}\omega\;dt\,\right] \in \dfrac{H^1(M;\mathbb{R})}{\Gamma}$

Homomorphism

Concatenating isotopies adds the integrated classes.

Printed pp. 385-416The Flux Homomorphism
Flux assigns to a symplectic isotopy the cohomology class obtained by integrating the one-forms of its generating fields over time. It descends to a homomorphism from the identity component into the first cohomology modulo a period lattice Gamma, measuring the swept class accumulated along the path.

Flux of a Loop: Swept Area

Pairing with a cycle

$\langle \mathrm{Flux}(\{\phi_t\}),\,c\rangle = \int_{[0,1]\times c}\omega$

The lattice $\Gamma$

$\Gamma$ is exactly the set of flux values of loops in $\mathrm{Symp}_0$.

Printed pp. 385-416Flux of a Loop
For a loop of symplectomorphisms the flux integral pairs against each one-cycle, recording the signed area swept as the cycle is dragged around the loop. The period subgroup Gamma is exactly the set of flux values of loops, which is why the quotient is the right target.

$\mathrm{Ham} = \ker(\mathrm{Flux})$

Characterization

An isotopy is Hamiltonian $\iff$ its flux is zero.

Flux conjecture

Asks whether $\Gamma$ is discrete, i.e. $\mathrm{Ham}$ is closed.

Short exact sequence

$1 \to \mathrm{Ham}(M,\omega) \to \mathrm{Symp}_0(M,\omega) \xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{Flux}\;} \dfrac{H^1(M;\mathbb{R})}{\Gamma} \to 0$

Consequence

$\mathrm{Ham}$ is normal; the quotient measures non-Hamiltonian directions.

Printed pp. 385-416Ham as the Kernel of Flux
The flux homomorphism fits into a short exact sequence whose kernel is the Hamiltonian group. So an isotopy is Hamiltonian if and only if its flux vanishes. This identifies Ham as a kernel, gives it a normal-subgroup structure, and frames the flux conjecture about discreteness of Gamma.

The Calabi Homomorphism

Setting

Exact symplectic $(M,\omega=d\lambda)$, compact support.

Domain

$\mathrm{Ham}_c(M,\omega)$, compactly supported Hamiltonian diffeos.

Independence

Value does not depend on the chosen generating path.

Definition

$\mathrm{Cal}(\phi) = \int_0^1\!\!\int_M H_t\,\omega^n\,dt$

What it adds

A real-valued invariant beyond flux on exact manifolds.

Printed pp. 385-416The Calabi Homomorphism
On an exact symplectic manifold the compactly supported Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms admit a further homomorphism, Calabi, integrating the generating Hamiltonian against the volume form over time. It is well defined independent of the chosen generating path and provides a nontrivial real-valued invariant of the group.

Calabi as an Averaged Hamiltonian

Homomorphism property

$\mathrm{Cal}(\phi\circ\psi) = \mathrm{Cal}(\phi) + \mathrm{Cal}(\psi)$

Kernel

Often a simple group, central to quasimorphism theory.

Printed pp. 385-416Calabi as a Volume Integral
Calabi averages the Hamiltonian over phase space and over the time interval, producing a single real number. Because admissible reparametrizations and conjugations leave this average unchanged, the construction is a genuine homomorphism whose kernel is a simple group in many cases of interest.

Homotopy type

$\mathrm{Symp}$ often retracts onto a compact Lie group.

Gromov

For the ball $B^4$ and $S^2\times S^2$, computed via $J$-curves.

Bridge ahead

Group topology links to pseudoholomorphic curve methods.

Example result

$\mathrm{Symp}(S^2,\omega)\;\simeq\;SO(3)$

Reading

$\pi_1(\mathrm{Ham})$ and $\pi_1(\mathrm{Symp})$ encode rich quantum and Seidel data.

Printed pp. 385-416Topology of the Group
The homotopy type of the symplectomorphism group carries deep geometry. Gromov computed it for the four-ball and the product of spheres, finding it deformation retracts onto a familiar compact Lie group. Such results connect group topology to pseudoholomorphic curve methods developed later in the book.
