Existence

Does a closed nondegenerate two-form live on this manifold?

Uniqueness

Printed pp. 503-548Existence and Uniqueness
This chapter spans printed pages 503 through 548 and asks two questions about symplectic forms. Existence asks which closed manifolds carry a symplectic structure at all, while uniqueness asks how many deformation classes survive once the cohomology class is fixed. Invariants organize both answers.

What a Symplectic Form Demands

Closed

$d\omega = 0$ gives a de Rham class $[\omega]\in H^2(M)$.

Nondegenerate

The top power $\omega^n$ is a volume form, never zero.

Two demands

$d\omega = 0, \qquad \omega^n \neq 0 \;\text{ in } H^{2n}(M)$

Reading

Every obstruction below descends from these two lines.

Printed pp. 503-548What a Symplectic Form Demands
A symplectic form is a closed two-form whose top power never vanishes. Closedness gives a de Rham cohomology class, and nondegeneracy forces that class to have nonzero top self-intersection. We read these two algebraic demands as the source of every necessary condition that follows.

Necessary Conditions for Existence

Dimension & orientation

$M$ is even dimensional; $\omega^n$ orients $M$.

Cohomology

A class $a\in H^2(M;\mathbb{R})$ with $a^n \neq 0$.

Four obstructions

$\dim M = 2n,\;\; a^n\neq 0,\;\; \exists\, J \text{ tamed by }\omega$

Almost complex

A compatible $J$ exists, with $\omega(\cdot,J\cdot)$ a metric.

Printed pp. 503-548Necessary Conditions
Four necessary conditions follow immediately. The manifold must be even dimensional, it must be orientable through the volume form, it must possess a degree two cohomology class with nonzero top power, and it must admit a compatible almost complex structure. Each is necessary but none alone suffices.

The Implication Chain

One direction

$\text{sympl.}\Rightarrow J\Rightarrow \text{even, orientable}$

Strict

None of the reverse implications hold in general.

Printed pp. 503-548The Implication Chain
The implications run one way only. Admitting a symplectic form forces an almost complex structure, which forces an even dimensional orientable manifold. The reverse arrows fail. The chapter is largely the study of the gaps between these classes, where topology stops controlling symplectic existence.

Almost Complex Is Not Enough

The gap

$J$ plus a cohomology class can exist with no symplectic $\omega$.

Obstruction

Gauge-theoretic invariants kill many almost complex candidates.

Strict inclusion

$\{\text{symplectic}\} \subsetneq \{\text{almost complex}\}$

Lesson

Topological obstructions are necessary, not sufficient.

Printed pp. 503-548Almost Complex Is Not Enough
The strict gap is the heart of the chapter. Many manifolds admit an almost complex structure and the required cohomology class yet carry no symplectic form. In dimension four Taubes constraints rule out candidates, showing that the obvious topological obstructions are necessary but genuinely insufficient for existence.

Uniqueness up to Deformation

Isotopy

A path of cohomologous symplectic forms; Moser's trick applies.

Deformation

A path of symplectic forms, cohomology free to move.

Moser

$[\omega_t]\text{ fixed}, \omega_t \text{ symplectic} \Rightarrow \text{isotopic}$

Question

Count the deformation classes on a fixed manifold.

Printed pp. 503-548Uniqueness up to Deformation
Uniqueness is studied through equivalence relations. Two forms may be diffeomorphic, isotopic through cohomologous symplectic forms, or merely deformation equivalent through a path of possibly non cohomologous symplectic forms. Moser's theorem links isotopy to fixed cohomology, so the interesting questions concern deformation classes.

Invariants That Separate Forms

J-holomorphic curves

Pseudoholomorphic curves depend only on the deformation class.

Gromov-Witten

Counts assign invariant numbers to each class.

Separation

$GW(\omega_0)\neq GW(\omega_1)\Rightarrow \omega_0 \not\sim \omega_1$

Use

Distinct counts certify genuinely different symplectic forms.

Printed pp. 503-548Invariants That Separate Forms
To prove two symplectic forms are inequivalent we need invariants finer than cohomology. J-holomorphic curve counts and Gromov-Witten invariants assign numbers to a deformation class. When two forms yield different counts they cannot be deformation equivalent, which is how distinct symplectic structures on one manifold are detected.

Dimension Four Is Special

Gauge theory

Seiberg-Witten invariants from a spin-$c$ structure and metric.

Smooth invariant

Independent of metric; a diffeomorphism invariant of $M^4$.

Input data

$SW: \mathrm{Spin}^c(M^4) \to \mathbb{Z}$

Interaction

A symplectic $\omega$ pins down the canonical spin-$c$ class.

Printed pp. 503-548Dimension Four Is Special
Dimension four behaves unlike all others. Gauge theory supplies Seiberg-Witten invariants computed from a spin-c structure and a metric, and these smooth invariants interact tightly with any symplectic form present. The chapter exploits this to constrain which four-manifolds are symplectic and to distinguish forms.

Taubes' Theorem: SW = Gr

Taubes

$SW(\mathfrak{s}) = Gr(\mathfrak{s}) \;\text{ on closed symplectic }M^4$

Bridge

Pseudoholomorphic curves realize Seiberg-Witten solutions.

Printed pp. 503-548Taubes: SW = Gr
Taubes proved that on a closed symplectic four-manifold the Seiberg-Witten invariants equal the Gromov invariants counting pseudoholomorphic curves. This identity bridges gauge theory and symplectic geometry. The smooth invariant on the left is computed analytically; the geometric count on the right reflects embedded J-holomorphic curves.

Consequences of Taubes

Canonical class

$SW(\pm K) = \pm 1$ for symplectic $M^4$; curves must appear.

Non-existence

Vanishing $SW$ forbids any symplectic form.

Test

$SW \equiv 0 \Rightarrow M^4 \text{ admits no } \omega$

Example

Connected sums with $b_2^+\!\geq 1$ each have $SW\equiv 0$.

Printed pp. 503-548Consequences of Taubes
The equality has sharp consequences. The canonical class of a symplectic four-manifold has a nonvanishing Seiberg-Witten invariant, so manifolds with vanishing invariants, such as connected sums with both pieces having positive second Betti number, admit no symplectic form despite passing every classical obstruction test.

Examples and Non-Examples

Symplectic

Kahler surfaces, symplectic fibrations, blow-ups of $\mathbb{CP}^2$.

Toy check

A candidate class needs positive square: $a_0^2 - a_1^2 > 0$.

Non-example

$3\,\mathbb{CP}^2 \;:\; J \text{ exists},\; \omega \text{ does not}$

Why

The Seiberg-Witten test fails although $J$ and the class exist.

Printed pp. 503-548Examples and Non-Examples
Concrete cases anchor the theory. Kahler surfaces and many symplectic fibrations exist, while the connected sum of three copies of complex projective plane admits an almost complex structure yet no symplectic form. These examples show the topological and symplectic worlds diverging exactly where invariants disagree.

Synthesis: An Atlas of Invariants

Topological layer

Even dimension, orientability, $a^n\neq 0$, almost complex $J$.

Symplectic layer

Gromov-Witten counts separate deformation classes.

Gauge layer

In dimension four, $SW = Gr$ governs existence and rigidity.

Organizing idea

$\text{existence \& uniqueness} \;\leftrightarrow\; \text{invariants under deformation}$

Printed pp. 503-548Synthesis
The chapter assembles an atlas indexed by invariants. Topological data gives necessary conditions, J-holomorphic and Gromov-Witten counts distinguish deformation classes, and in dimension four Taubes ties Seiberg-Witten theory to curve counts. Existence and uniqueness become questions about which invariants survive deformation and which obstruct it.
