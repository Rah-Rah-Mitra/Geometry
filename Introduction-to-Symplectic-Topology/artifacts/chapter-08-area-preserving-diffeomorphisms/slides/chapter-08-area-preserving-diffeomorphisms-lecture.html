Area Is the Symplectic Form

Dimension two

A symplectic form on a surface is an area form.

Symplectomorphism

An area- and orientation-preserving diffeomorphism.

Preservation law

$\varphi^{*}\omega = \omega, \qquad \det D\varphi(x) = 1$

Why it matters

Determinant one keeps signed area fixed, so fixed-point counts are symplectic.

Printed pp. 341-355Area Is the Symplectic Form
On a surface the symplectic form is just an area form, so a symplectomorphism is precisely an orientation- and area-preserving diffeomorphism. The defining condition is that the Jacobian determinant equals one everywhere, which keeps signed area unchanged and makes every later fixed-point count a genuinely symplectic statement rather than a topological accident.

The Annulus and Its Twist Map

Coordinates

Angle $\theta$ and radius $r$ on the closed annulus $A$.

Twist map

Advance the angle by an amount that depends on $r$.

Standard twist

$\Phi(\theta, r) = \big(\theta + \alpha(r),\; r\big)$

Boundaries

Both circles $r = r_{\min}, r_{\max}$ are invariant.

Printed pp. 341-355The Annulus and Its Twist Map
The closed annulus carries angle and radial coordinates with the standard area form. A twist map advances the angle by an amount depending monotonically on the radius, leaving the two boundary circles invariant. This is the cleanest model in which to watch how differential rotation across the annulus is forced to create fixed points.

The Twist Condition

Monotone twist

The angular advance strictly increases with radius.

Opposite ends

Inner and outer boundaries rotate by different amounts.

Twist condition

$\frac{\partial \theta_1}{\partial r} > 0, \quad \alpha(r_{\min}) \cdot \alpha(r_{\max}) < 0$

Printed pp. 341-355The Twist Condition
The monotone twist condition requires the angular advance to be strictly increasing in the radius, so the inner and outer boundaries rotate by different amounts. When these boundary rotations have opposite signs relative to a chosen lift, the angular displacement must vanish somewhere in between, which is the analytic seed of the fixed-point theorem.

Periodic Orbits

Period q

$\Phi^{q}$ returns a point to its start.

Rotation number

The orbit winds $p$ times around the annulus.

p/q periodic orbit

$\Phi^{q}(\theta,r) = (\theta + 2\pi p,\; r)$

Variational link

Orbits are critical points of an action sum.

Printed pp. 341-355Periodic Orbits
A point is periodic of period q when iterating the map q times returns it to its start, and the orbit winds p times around the annulus. Twist maps support rich families of such p over q orbits, and locating them reduces to solving the displacement equation, linking dynamics directly to the calculus of variations.

The Poincare-Birkhoff Theorem

Theorem

An area-preserving homeomorphism of the annulus that rotates the two boundary circles in opposite senses has at least two distinct fixed points.

Hypotheses

Area preservation and the boundary twist condition.

Conjecture to proof

Posed by Poincare, proved by Birkhoff in 1913.

Significance

A purely two-dimensional fixed-point theorem driven by area.

Printed pp. 341-355Poincare-Birkhoff Theorem
Poincare conjectured and Birkhoff proved that an area-preserving homeomorphism of the annulus satisfying the boundary twist condition has at least two distinct fixed points. Area preservation is essential: it forbids the map from pushing every point in one angular direction, forcing the displacement to change sign and produce crossings.

Why Two Fixed Points

One crossing is not enough

A single transversal zero would let area drift.

Area balances

Forward-swept area cancels back-swept area.

Area constraint

$\oint \big(\theta_1 - \theta\big)\, dr = 0$

Even count

The displacement must cross zero an even number of times.

Printed pp. 341-355Why Two Fixed Points
The displacement curve must cross zero, but a single transversal crossing would let a small area drift consistently, contradicting preservation. Conservation of signed area balances the region swept forward against the region swept back, which forces an even count of crossings and therefore at least two fixed points rather than one.

Billiards as Area-Preserving Maps

Reflection law

Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.

Phase space

Position on the boundary plus incidence angle.

Billiard map

Bounce to bounce preserves a natural area form.

Printed pp. 341-355Billiards as Area-Preserving Maps
A billiard trajectory in a convex table moves in straight chords and reflects so the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Encoding each bounce by its boundary position and incidence angle yields the billiard map on an annular phase space, and that map preserves a natural area form, making billiards a symplectic system.

The Generating Function

Chord length

The Euclidean distance between consecutive bounce points.

Exact symplectic

Length generates the billiard map.

Generating function

$L(s, s') = \lVert \gamma(s') - \gamma(s) \rVert$

Reflection from variation

$\partial_{s'} L(s,s') + \partial_{s'} L(s',s'') = 0$ gives equal angles.

Printed pp. 341-355The Generating Function
The billiard map is exact symplectic with the chord length serving as its generating function. Stationarity of total length under variation of the boundary points reproduces the reflection law exactly, so periodic billiard trajectories are critical points of a length functional, tying the dynamics to the calculus of variations.

Periodic Billiard Trajectories

Closed polygons

A q-periodic orbit is an inscribed q-gon.

Longest chord

The diameter gives a two-bounce orbit.

Variational principle

$\delta \sum_{k} L(s_k, s_{k+1}) = 0$

Birkhoff

Infinitely many periodic orbits in any smooth convex table.

Printed pp. 341-355Periodic Billiard Trajectories
Maximizing total chord length over closed inscribed polygons produces periodic billiard orbits, the simplest being the longest inscribed chord whose two-bounce path retraces itself along a diameter-like segment. Birkhoff used these variational and twist arguments to guarantee infinitely many periodic billiard trajectories in any smooth convex table.
