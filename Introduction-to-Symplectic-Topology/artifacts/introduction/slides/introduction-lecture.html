The Symplectic Form

Canonical form

$\omega = \sum_{i=1}^{n} dp_i \wedge dq^i$

Closed and nondegenerate

$d\omega = 0$ and $\omega$ pairs every nonzero vector nontrivially.

Antisymmetric

Measures signed area, not length: $\omega(u,v) = -\omega(v,u)$.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21The Symplectic Form
On phase space the canonical two-form sums the wedge of each momentum differential with its position differential. Being closed and nondegenerate, it measures signed area in every conjugate plane. Unlike a metric it pairs displacements antisymmetrically, so it records oriented area rather than length, which is the structure we will study throughout.

Hamilton's Equations

Form turns energy into flow

The defining relation $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ names a unique vector field.

Integral curves = motions

Trajectories are the flow lines of the Hamiltonian field $X_H$.

Equations of motion

$\dot q^i = \dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}$

Reading it

Nondegeneracy makes $X_H$ unique; the form is the translator from gradients to dynamics.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21Hamilton's Equations
Given an energy function, the symplectic form converts its differential into a unique vector field whose integral curves are the motions. The equations say position rates equal momentum derivatives of energy and momentum rates equal minus position derivatives. The form is exactly the device translating gradients into dynamics.

Hamiltonian Flows Preserve Area

Conservation law

$\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\,\omega = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (\phi_t)^*\omega = \omega$

Liouville's theorem

Phase-space volume $\omega^n/n!$ is invariant under the flow.

Stretch but conserve

A region may deform wildly yet keep its total signed area fixed.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21Flows Preserve Area
Because the Hamiltonian field is defined through the closed form, its flow drags that form to itself, so the form is conserved along motion. Liouville's theorem follows: phase-space volume is invariant. A region may stretch and fold wildly while its total signed area stays fixed, a conservation law with no Newtonian analogue.

Darboux: Local Flexibility

Darboux's theorem

Locally $\;\omega \cong \sum dp_i \wedge dq^i$

No local invariants

Every symplectic manifold looks identical near each point; nothing like curvature.

Moser's method

An isotopy built from a path of forms proves the normal form cleanly.

Consequence

If everything is locally the same, any distinction must be global.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21Darboux: Local Flexibility
Darboux's theorem says every symplectic manifold looks locally like standard phase space; there are no local invariants such as curvature. Moser's deformation argument supplies a slick proof. Locally everything is flexible and identical, so any genuine distinction between symplectic manifolds must be a global, not a pointwise, phenomenon.

Gromov's Nonsqueezing Theorem

Nonsqueezing (1985)

$B^{2n}(r)\hookrightarrow Z^{2n}(R)\;\Rightarrow\; r \le R$

Volume is not the obstacle

By volume alone a ball could squeeze into any thin cylinder.

A symplectic camel

The width $r$ cannot be reduced no matter how the ball is deformed.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21Gromov Nonsqueezing
Gromov's 1985 theorem is the first hard rigidity result. A symplectic embedding of a ball of radius r into an infinite cylinder forces the cylinder radius to be at least r. Volume would permit squeezing freely, so this is a purely symplectic obstruction, and it births the notion of a capacity.

Capacities and Embeddings

What is a capacity?

A number $c$ per region: monotone under embeddings, scaling like area.

Monotonicity

$U\hookrightarrow V \;\Rightarrow\; c(U) \le c(V)$

Cylinder capacity

Nonsqueezing fixes $c\big(Z(R)\big)=\pi R^2$, the cross-section area.

Open frontier

Exact embedding thresholds for ellipsoids and balls are still being mapped.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21Capacities and Embeddings
A symplectic capacity assigns each region a number that is monotone under embeddings and scales like area. Nonsqueezing makes the cylinder capacity equal to the cross-sectional area. Capacities turn embedding questions into inequalities, and the precise embedding thresholds for ellipsoids and balls remain an active and delicate research frontier.

The Arnold Conjecture

Fixed-point bound

$\#\mathrm{Fix}(\phi) \;\ge\; \sum_k \dim H_k(M)$

More than topology demands

A Hamiltonian map must have as many fixed points as a function has critical points.

Why it is deep

Generic maps need only a Lefschetz count; Hamiltonian rigidity forces far more.

Legacy

Its proof launched Floer homology, central to the modern theory.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21Arnold Conjecture
Arnold predicted that a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism must have at least as many fixed points as a function on the manifold has critical points. This forces far more fixed points than topology alone demands. Proving it drove the creation of Floer homology, a cornerstone of modern symplectic methods.

J-Holomorphic Curves: The Central Tool

Cauchy-Riemann

$\bar\partial_J u = \tfrac12\big(du + J\circ du\circ j\big) = 0$

Tame the form

Choose an almost complex $J$ with $\omega(v,Jv)>0$ on nonzero $v$.

One tool, many theorems

Nonsqueezing, capacities, and Arnold all reduce to counting these curves.

Rigidity as analysis

Gromov compactness turns symplectic rigidity into geometric PDE.

Printed pp. 1-8 | PDF pp. 14-21J-Holomorphic Curves
Gromov introduced an auxiliary almost complex structure tamed by the form and studied curves solving a Cauchy-Riemann equation. These J-holomorphic curves are the central technical tool: nonsqueezing, capacities, and the Arnold conjecture all rest on counting them. They convert symplectic rigidity into geometric analysis.
