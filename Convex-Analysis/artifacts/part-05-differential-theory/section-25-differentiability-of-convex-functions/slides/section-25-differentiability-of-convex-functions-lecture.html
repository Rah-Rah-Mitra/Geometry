Differentiability Of Convex Functions

For a convex function, differentiability is not an extra analytic ornament. It is the moment when all supporting certificates, all directional derivatives, and all finite-difference probes agree on one vector.

Lecture Goal

Explain why \(\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}\) is the right convex-geometric test for differentiability at an interior point.

Main Theorems

Theorem 25.1 gives the singleton criterion. Theorems 25.2 and 25.5 connect it to directional derivatives and almost-everywhere differentiability.

Running Contrast

Smooth bowls have one supporting plane. Absolute-value corners and max ridges keep a whole family of valid supports.

Supporting Geometry
one tangent, one certificate

Unique Supporting Hyperplane

Differentiability

\(f(z)=f(x)+\langle g,z-x\rangle+o(|z-x|)\), with \(g=\nabla f(x)\).

Convex Support

\(f(z)\ge f(x)+\langle x^*,z-x\rangle\quad\text{for every }z.\)

Geometric Reading

A subgradient is a supporting hyperplane to the epigraph. Differentiability says the first-order approximation and the supporting plane are the same object.

Failure Signal

If two different slopes support the graph at \(x\), no single affine first-order model can approximate the function from all directions.

Artifact: assets/singleton-subdiff.svgThe hyperplane picture is the local certificate picture
Use the epigraph picture before the formula. The ordinary derivative gives a linear first-order model, while convexity asks for affine lower supports. At a differentiable point those two roles merge: the gradient vector defines the only support that can touch at the point and remain below the function everywhere.
Theorem 25.1
gradient equals the only subgradient

Singleton Subdifferential

Equivalence

\(f\) convex and finite at \(x\): \(f\) is differentiable at \(x\) iff \(\partial f(x)\) consists of one vector.

Forward Direction

If \(f\) is differentiable, the directional derivative is \(y\mapsto \langle\nabla f(x),y\rangle\). The subgradient inequalities force every \(x^*\) to equal \(\nabla f(x)\).

Reverse Direction

If \(\partial f(x)=\{x^*\}\), subtract the affine support. The resulting convex function has only the zero subgradient at the origin, so its quotient \(g(y)/|y|\) tends to zero.

Differentiability is a geometric uniqueness statement: one exposed supporting plane touches the epigraph at \((x,f(x))\).

Theorem 25.1 plus Corollary 25.1.1A differentiability point lies in \(\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom} f)\)
State the theorem as the section's hinge. The forward direction is quick once the directional derivative is linear, but the reverse direction is the convex-analysis insight: a unique subgradient rules out first-order directional spread. Also flag the domain consequence, because a finite differentiability point must actually sit in the interior of the effective domain.
Theorem 25.2
directional derivatives become linear

Directional Derivatives Collapse To A Linear Map

Directional Derivative

\(f'(x;y)=\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{f(x+\lambda y)-f(x)}{\lambda}\).

Differentiable Case

\(f'(x;y)=\langle\nabla f(x),y\rangle\quad\text{for every }y.\)

Two-Sided Partial Test

For convex \(f\), finite two-sided partial derivatives in the coordinate directions are already enough to force linearity of \(f'(x;\cdot)\).

What Does Not Suffice

One-sided directional derivatives always exist for convex functions on the interior, but one-sided existence alone leaves room for corners.

Artifact: assets/directional-collapse.svgLinearity of \(f'(x;\cdot)\) is equivalent to differentiability
Make the directional derivative feel like a support function. In general it is sublinear because it is the support function of the subdifferential. When the subdifferential collapses to one point, the support function becomes a linear pairing. The coordinate partial derivative criterion is powerful precisely because convexity fills in the rest of the directions.

Use the quadratic bowl as the clean baseline. Example
absolute value corner

Absolute Value Corner

One-Dimensional Kink

\(f(t)=|t|,\qquad \partial f(0)=[-1,1].\)

Directional Spread

\(f'(0;1)=1,\quad f'(0;-1)=1,\quad -f'(0;-1)=-1.\)

Why Central Difference Misleads

The symmetric quotient at zero gives \(0\), but \(0\) is only one valid subgradient. It is not a two-sided linear derivative.

Subgradient View

Every slope between \(-1\) and \(1\) supports the V-shaped graph at the corner, so the singleton test fails.

Corner example: many supports at one pointA symmetric finite difference can hide nonsmoothness
This is the smallest warning example. The absolute value corner is convex, and every supporting slope in the interval is legitimate. A numerical central difference at the corner can return zero, but that does not certify differentiability. The real test is whether all directional slopes combine into one linear map, and they do not.
Example
maximum ridge

Max Ridge

Piecewise-Affine Convex Function

\(f(x_1,x_2)=\max\{x_1,x_2\}.\)

Off The Ridge

Only one affine piece is active, so the gradient is either \(e_1\) or \(e_2\).

On The Ridge

\(\partial f(x)=\operatorname{conv}\{e_1,e_2\}\). The active gradients form a segment, not a point.

Directional Formula

\(f'(x;y)=\max\{y_1,y_2\}\) at ridge points, a sublinear function that is not linear.

Artifact: assets/ridge-nondifferentiability.svgNondifferentiability can live on a thin ridge, not just a point
Move from the one-dimensional corner to a two-dimensional ridge. Away from the line where the affine pieces tie, one plane wins and the gradient is clear. Along the ridge, both planes are active, so the subdifferential is the convex hull of their gradients. The directional derivative is a maximum, which is sublinear but not linear.
Theorem 25.5
dense and almost everywhere

Almost-Everywhere Intuition

Gradient Set

\(D=\{x:f\text{ is differentiable at }x\}\) is dense in \(\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{dom} f)\), and its complement has measure zero.

One Dimension

Left and right slopes are monotone. Nondifferentiability occurs at jumps of a monotone function, hence at no more than countably many points.

Higher Dimension

Apply the one-direction result to coordinate directions, then intersect the full-measure differentiability sets.

Right Mental Model

Ridges can be important, but convexity makes them small inside the domain: dense smooth points still surround them.

Almost everywhere refers to interior domain measureDense does not mean every point is differentiable
Give the almost-everywhere theorem an intuition rather than treating it as magic. In one dimension, the right derivative is monotone, so nondifferentiability corresponds to jumps. In higher dimensions, the proof reduces directional differentiability to coordinate tests and then intersects full-measure sets. The result explains why ridges are visible but thin.
Continuity And Caveats
smooth on open interiors when differentiable everywhere

Gradient Continuity Caveats

Continuity On D

If \(D\) is the differentiability set, then \(x\mapsto\nabla f(x)\) is continuous relative to \(D\).

Open-Set Corollary

If \(f\) is finite convex and differentiable on an open convex set \(C\), then \(f\in C^1(C)\).

Caveat 1

Continuity on \(D\) does not define a gradient on ridge points outside \(D\).

Caveat 2

Boundary points need normal cones; Theorem 25.6 reconstructs \(\partial f\) from gradient limits plus the normal cone to the domain.

Artifact: assets/gradient-caveat.svgContinuous differentiability is an interior open-set conclusion
Separate the strong and weak continuity claims. The gradient is continuous on the differentiability set, and if a finite convex function is differentiable on an entire open convex region, then it is continuously differentiable there. But this does not erase nondifferentiable ridges, and it does not handle boundary behavior without adding the normal cone.

Probe Logic

Finite differences suggest a candidate \(g\). Convex differentiability asks whether \(\partial f(x)=\{g\}\) and whether \(f'(x;y)=\langle g,y\rangle\) for every direction.

Recap
one vector or a face

Recap

1. Support

A gradient is the slope of the unique supporting hyperplane to the epigraph at a smooth point.

2. Singleton

The exact convex criterion is \(\partial f(x)=\{\nabla f(x)\}\).

3. Directions

Differentiability is equivalent to \(f'(x;y)\) being linear in the direction \(y\).

4. Ridges

Corners and max ridges preserve convexity but leave an interval or face of subgradients.

Close by returning to the same inspection prompt from the opening slide. To decide differentiability for a convex function, first identify the local supporting slopes. If they form one point, that point is the gradient and all directional derivatives agree with it. If they form a segment, interval, cone, or face, the function is nonsmooth at that point.
