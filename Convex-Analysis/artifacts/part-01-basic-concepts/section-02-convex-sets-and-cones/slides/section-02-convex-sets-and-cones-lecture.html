Convex Sets And Cones

A lecture on operations and certificates: line segments, convex combinations, hulls, cones, conic hulls, dual halfspace checks, and concrete tests for failure.

Segment Test

Definition-Level Test

\(x,y \in C,\ 0 \leq t \leq 1 \Rightarrow (1-t)x + ty \in C\)

A set is convex when every displayed segment survives every value of \(t\), not just the midpoint.

Inspection Habit

  • Choose two points from the same candidate set.
  • Sweep the whole interval of weights.
  • Look for the first parameter that exits.
Make the segment test feel like the smallest possible convexity experiment. Pick two displayed points first, then sweep the parameter t. The important habit is that a convexity claim is not just a shaded region; it commits to every intermediate point between every admissible pair.

Convex Combinations

From Two Points To Many

\(x = \lambda_1x_1 + \cdots + \lambda_kx_k\)
\(\lambda_i \geq 0,\quad \lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_k = 1\)

Convex combinations are finite averages with explicit weights. They certify membership in any convex set containing the listed points.

Certificate Checklist

Condition Geometric meaning
\(\lambda_i \geq 0\) No point is used by extrapolating through the others.
\(\sum_i \lambda_i = 1\) The combination is affine and keeps the scale of the original space.
finite support The certificate is checkable from a short list of points.
Operation: weighted average Certificate: nonnegative weights with total mass one
Introduce convex combinations as the algebra behind the segment sweep. The weights carry all the geometry: nonnegative prevents extrapolation, and sum one keeps the point affine rather than conic. Ask students to read the formula as a certificate that can be checked line by line.

Convex Hull As Closure Under Mixing

Smallest Convex Container

The convex hull of \(A\) is the set of all finite convex combinations of points of \(A\).

\(\operatorname{conv}(A)=\{\sum_i \lambda_i a_i: a_i\in A,\ \lambda_i\geq0,\ \sum_i\lambda_i=1\}\)

Closure Under Repeated Mixing

Mixing two already mixed points produces another convex combination, so the hull does not change after the operation is allowed.

Use the hull picture to turn repeated mixing into an object. Start with a finite point cloud, allow every convex combination, and the filled polygon appears as the closure of all possible averages. The displayed weights are not decorative; they are a membership certificate for the interior point.

Caratheodory-Style Compression View

Compression Principle

In \(\mathbb{R}^d\), a point in a convex hull can be certified using at most \(d+1\) active points.

\(\mathbb{R}^2:\quad 5\hbox{ weights } \leadsto 3\hbox{ weights}\)

Cones And Positive Scaling

Cone Closure

\(x\in K,\ \alpha\geq0 \Rightarrow \alpha x\in K\)

If \(K\) is also convex, it is closed under nonnegative combinations of its points. The origin is always present once the zero scale is allowed.

Scale Move outward or inward on a ray using a nonnegative scalar.
Add For convex cones, sums of admissible ray points stay admissible.
Forget Mass No normalization condition fixes the total coefficient size.
Inspect Rays The boundary rays act like primitive generators.
Cones add a second closure rule: once a point is admitted, every positive scaling of it is admitted as well. Emphasize that this removes the normalization sum one. The origin becomes an anchor, rays become primitive evidence, and addition of admissible rays supplies the fan.

Conic Combinations

Convex Versus Conic Weights

Combination Formula Constraint
Convex \(\sum_i \lambda_i x_i\) \(\lambda_i\geq0,\ \sum_i\lambda_i=1\)
Conic \(\sum_i \alpha_i g_i\) \(\alpha_i\geq0\)

Membership Certificate

\(g_1=(2,0.7),\quad g_2=(0.5,2.2)\)
\(y = 1.4g_1 + 0.8g_2 = (3.2,2.74)\)

The sign check is the point: both coefficients are nonnegative, so \(y\) belongs to the conic hull of the two generators.

Artifact: assets/conic-membership-certificate.json Conic hull: \(\operatorname{cone}\{g_1,g_2\}\)
Conic combinations are the cone analogue of convex combinations. The check is simpler and harsher: coefficients must be nonnegative, but they do not have to sum to one. Read the target point as generated evidence, then mention that a negative coefficient would point outside the cone certificate.

Pointed, Flat, And Full-Dimensional Examples

Flat

Generated by one ray. The cone is convex but has empty interior in the ambient plane.

Pointed

Contains no nonzero vector together with its negative. The apex is genuinely sharp.

Full-Dimensional

The generator span has rank two in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), so the cone has nonempty interior.

Use the taxonomy slide to separate three issues that are often blurred. A flat cone can be low-dimensional, a pointed cone can have no line through the origin, and a full-dimensional cone can still be sharply constrained. These examples prepare students for rank and lineality checks.

Polyhedral Cones From Generators

Generator Description

\(K = \operatorname{cone}\{g_1,\ldots,g_m\}= \{G\alpha:\alpha\geq0\}\)

A finite list of rays gives a computational handle on the entire cone.

Extreme Ray Intuition

Boundary rays that cannot be split into two different cone directions behave as irreducible edges of the fan.

Treat a polyhedral cone as the result of finitely many generator rays. The fan is a computational object: build the matrix of generators, solve for nonnegative coefficients, then inspect rank to decide the dimension of the span. Extreme rays are the irreducible visual edges of the fan.

Dual Inspection By Halfspaces

Dual Cone Test

\(K^*=\{c: c\cdot x\geq0\ \hbox{for all }x\in K\}\)

For a generated cone, it is enough to check the generators:

\(c\cdot g_i\geq0\quad i=1,\ldots,m\)
Probe Dot products Verdict
\(c=(0.35,0.6)\) 1.12, 1.495 accepted
\(c=(0.9,-0.4)\) 1.52, -0.43 rejected

One negative generator dot product is a halfspace witness that the probe is outside the dual cone.

Dual inspection reverses the direction of evidence. Instead of producing the point from generators, choose a linear functional and check its sign on every generator. If all generator tests are nonnegative, the functional is in the dual cone; one failed dot product is a separating diagnostic.

Convexity Under Intersection

Operation Rule

\(C_\alpha\ \hbox{convex for every }\alpha \Rightarrow \bigcap_\alpha C_\alpha\ \hbox{convex}\)

If \(x\) and \(y\) belong to every set in the family, their whole segment belongs to every set too.

Why Halfspaces Matter

Polyhedra and many cones are intersections of linear inequalities. Convexity arrives one halfspace at a time.

Intersection is the quiet workhorse operation. Each convex set promises to keep the whole segment between two shared points, so the segment stays in every member at once and therefore in the intersection. This explains why halfspace descriptions are so powerful for polyhedra and cones.

Nonconvex Failure Gallery

Counterexample Form

\(x,y\in S,\quad (1-t)x+ty\notin S\)

One valid witness disproves convexity. It does not need to describe the whole set.

Inspection Prompt

  • Where are the two endpoints?
  • Which \(t\) fails first?
  • Is the failure caused by a gap, a union, or a curved equality?
The failure gallery is meant to sharpen the eye. Each mini-example gives endpoints that pass local membership but a midpoint or chord that leaves the set. Ask the room to name the failing witness before reading the caption; a single witness is enough to disprove convexity.

Recap / Inspection Prompt

Given a proposed set \(S\), ask:

Can every segment between two points of \(S\) be certified inside \(S\)? If not, exhibit the first clear counterexample.

Given a proposed point \(x\), ask:

Is there a convex-weight, conic-weight, or halfspace-sign certificate that proves membership or proves exclusion?
