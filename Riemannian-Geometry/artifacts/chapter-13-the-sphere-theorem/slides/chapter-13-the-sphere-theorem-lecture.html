The Sphere Theorem

When curvature pinching forces topology

Hypothesis

Complete, simply connected, strictly $\tfrac14$-pinched curvature.

Conclusion

The manifold is homeomorphic to the sphere $S^n$.

Tools

Rauch, Klingenberg, Bonnet–Myers, Toponogov.

Printed pp. 265-285The Sphere Theorem
This chapter, spanning printed pages 265 to 285, builds toward one of Riemannian geometry's landmark results. We will see how a purely metric pinching hypothesis on sectional curvature forces a complete simply connected manifold to be topologically a sphere, and assemble the comparison machinery that makes the conclusion possible.

Strict Quarter Pinching

The band

Every sectional curvature obeys $\tfrac14 < K(\sigma) \le 1$.

Strictness matters

The open lower bound excludes the projective spaces.

Pinching condition

$\dfrac14 < K(\sigma) \le 1 \quad \text{for all planes } \sigma$

Printed pp. 265-285Strict Quarter Pinching
The hypothesis is strict quarter pinching: sectional curvature lies in the half open interval one quarter to one. We normalize the upper bound to one so distances compare against the unit sphere. The strictness at one quarter is essential, since the closed condition admits other space forms like projective spaces.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 13

Statement of the Theorem

Topological Sphere Theorem

If $M^n$ is complete, simply connected, and $\tfrac14 < K \le 1$, then $M^n$ is homeomorphic to $S^n$.

Caveat

Homeomorphic, not yet diffeomorphic — exotic spheres lurk.

Why simply connected?

Lens spaces have constant $K=1$ yet are not spheres; $\pi_1$ must vanish.

Why complete?

Completeness lets geodesics run to the cut locus and the exponential map cover $M$.

Printed pp. 265-285Statement of the Theorem
Here we state the topological sphere theorem precisely. A complete simply connected manifold whose sectional curvature is strictly quarter pinched is homeomorphic to the n-sphere. We stress homeomorphic, not diffeomorphic, since the smooth refinement required decades more work and exotic spheres complicate the differentiable picture.

Conjugate Points and Rauch

Rauch comparison

Curvature bounds control how fast neighboring geodesics spread.

No early conjugate point

$K \le 1 \;\Rightarrow\; d(p,\text{conj}) \ge \pi$

Printed pp. 265-285Conjugate Points and Rauch
Rauch comparison controls how fast geodesics spread according to curvature bounds. With curvature at most one, no conjugate point occurs before distance pi, so geodesics of length less than pi minimize against that obstruction. This conjugate point estimate is the first half of bounding the injectivity radius from below.

The Injectivity Radius

Definition

Largest $r$ on which $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism on the ball $B_r(0)$.

Two competitors

The conjugate radius vs. half a shortest closed geodesic.

Governing quantity

$\mathrm{inj}(M) = \min\!\big(\text{conj radius},\ \tfrac12\,\ell_{\min}\big)$

Goal

Bound $\mathrm{inj}(M)$ below by $\pi$ — the geometric core.

Printed pp. 265-285The Injectivity Radius
The injectivity radius is the largest radius on which the exponential map stays a diffeomorphism. It is governed by two competitors: the first conjugate point along a geodesic, and half the length of the shortest closed geodesic. Bounding it below is the geometric heart of the proof we now pursue.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 13

Klingenberg's Lemma

Klingenberg estimate

$\mathrm{inj}(M) \ge \min\!\big(\pi,\ \tfrac12\,\ell_{\text{cg}}\big)$

Conjugate side

Pinching gives conjugate radius $\ge \pi$ via Rauch.

Printed pp. 265-285Klingenberg's Lemma
Klingenberg's lemma states that the injectivity radius equals the minimum of the conjugate radius and half the shortest closed geodesic length. Under quarter pinching the conjugate radius is at least pi, and a separate argument bounds the closed geodesic, yielding an injectivity radius of at least pi for the manifold.

Bonnet–Myers Diameter Bound

Lower bound at work

$K > \tfrac14$ forces Ricci curvature large enough for Bonnet–Myers.

Diameter estimate

$K > \tfrac14 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{diam}(M) < 2\pi$

Two estimates combine

$\mathrm{inj}(M)\ge\pi$ and $\mathrm{diam}(M)<2\pi$.

Consequence

Every point lies within $\pi$ of $p$ or of the far point $q$.

Printed pp. 265-285Bonnet–Myers Diameter Bound
Combining the lower curvature bound one quarter with Bonnet Myers gives a diameter at most two pi. Together with the injectivity radius at least pi, every point lies within reach of a single exponential chart from suitable centers. These two metric estimates frame the covering we will build from two balls.

Toponogov & Berger

Hinge comparison

Triangles in $M$ are fatter than model triangles of curvature $\tfrac14$.

Angle inequality

$\angle_M \ge \angle_{\tilde M}, \quad K_{\tilde M}=\tfrac14$

Printed pp. 265-285Toponogov & Berger
Toponogov's theorem compares geodesic triangles in our pinched manifold against model triangles on the sphere of curvature one quarter. Angles in our manifold are no smaller than the comparison angles. Berger applied this hinge comparison to control how far apart two maximally distant points sit and to align the two covering balls.

Cover by Two Balls

Antipodal centers

Choose $q$ at maximal distance from $p$.

Covering claim

$M = B_\pi(p) \cup B_\pi(q)$

Each ball is a disk

$\exp$ is a diffeomorphism on each $B_\pi$.

Printed pp. 265-285Cover by Two Balls
Pick a point p and a point q at maximal distance. The open balls of radius pi about p and about q are each diffeomorphic to a Euclidean ball through the exponential map. The pinching estimates guarantee these two balls already cover the whole manifold, leaving no uncovered region between them.

Two Disks Make a Sphere

Topological fact

A union of two embedded $n$-disks along their boundary is $S^n$.

Conclusion

$D^n \cup_{\partial} D^n \;\cong\; S^n$

Printed pp. 265-285Two Disks Make a Sphere
The manifold is now two disks overlapping in a collar near their common boundary sphere. Any manifold that is a union of two embedded disks is homeomorphic to the standard sphere, by reversing the equatorial gluing that builds the sphere from two hemispheres. This is the topological punchline of the construction.

Sharpness and Rigidity

Why strict?

$\mathbb{CP}^n$ and $\mathbb{HP}^n$ carry curvature in $[\tfrac14,1]$ yet are not spheres.

Borderline models

$\tfrac14 \le K_{\mathbb{CP}^n} \le 1$

Equality case

Weak pinching at $\tfrac14$ leads to rank-one symmetric spaces.

Lesson

The open inequality at $\tfrac14$ cannot be relaxed.

Printed pp. 265-285Sharpness and Rigidity
Quarter pinching is sharp: the complex and quaternionic projective spaces carry metrics with curvature exactly between one quarter and one, but they are not spheres. They show why the inequality at one quarter must be strict. The rigidity case at equality leads to the rich theory of rank one symmetric spaces.

Recap and Outlook

The chain

Pinching → Rauch → Klingenberg → diameter → Toponogov → two balls.

Result

A homeomorphism $M^n \cong S^n$ from two geodesic disks.

Beyond

The differentiable sphere theorem via Ricci flow (Brendle–Schoen).

One-line summary

$\tfrac14 < K \le 1,\ \pi_1=0 \;\Rightarrow\; M \cong_{\text{top}} S^n$

Printed pp. 265-285Recap and Outlook
We recapped the logical chain: pinching, Rauch and conjugate points, Klingenberg's injectivity estimate, Bonnet Myers diameter, Toponogov comparison, and the two ball covering that yields a homeomorphism to the sphere. The differentiable sphere theorem and Ricci flow proofs extend this story far beyond our pages.
