Curvature as Failure to Commute

Flat case

In $\mathbb{R}^n$ second derivatives commute; transport is path-independent.

Curved case

Covariant derivatives no longer commute. The gap is curvature.

Definition of $R$

$R(X,Y)Z = \nabla_X\nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y\nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$

Bracket term

$\nabla_{[X,Y]}$ corrects for non-coordinate fields.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109Failure to Commute
On Euclidean space mixed second derivatives commute, so transporting along two orders agrees. On a curved manifold the covariant analogue fails, and the gap is exactly the curvature operator. We define R by this commutator and stress that the bracket term repairs the failure for non-coordinate fields.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 4

$R$ Is a Tensor

Tensoriality

$R(X,Y)Z$ is $C^\infty$-linear in $X$, $Y$, and $Z$.

Pointwise

Its value at $p$ depends only on the vectors at $p$.

Linearity check

$R(fX,Y)Z = f\,R(X,Y)Z$

Components

$R^l_{\,ijk} = \partial_i\Gamma^l_{jk} - \partial_j\Gamma^l_{ik} + \Gamma\Gamma - \Gamma\Gamma$

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109The Curvature Tensor
We confirm that the commutator definition is tensorial: R of X, Y acting on Z is linear over smooth functions in all three slots, so its value at a point depends only on the vectors there. This pointwise character is what lets curvature be read off a single tangent space.

The Holonomy Picture

Infinitesimal loop

$\Delta v \approx R(X,Y)v \cdot \mathrm{Area}$

Reading

The vector comes back rotated; curvature is the rotation density.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109Holonomy Around a Loop
Parallel transporting a vector around a small coordinate loop returns it rotated, not unchanged. The infinitesimal rotation per unit area is the curvature operator. This holonomy picture is the geometric heart of the chapter and the one students should carry away from the algebra of the commutator.

Algebraic Symmetries

Antisymmetry

$\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle = -\langle R(Y,X)Z,W\rangle$

Antisymmetry

$\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle = -\langle R(X,Y)W,Z\rangle$

Pair symmetry

$\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle = \langle R(Z,W)X,Y\rangle$

Consequence

Independent components drop sharply for each dimension.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109Symmetries of R
The curvature tensor obeys three algebraic symmetries: antisymmetry in the first pair, antisymmetry in the last pair, and pair-interchange symmetry. Together with the first Bianchi identity these cut the number of independent components dramatically and underlie every later trace and contraction we form.

The First Bianchi Identity

Cyclic sum

The cyclic sum of $R$ over its first three slots vanishes.

Origin

Follows from the torsion-free Levi-Civita connection.

Identity

$R(X,Y)Z + R(Y,Z)X + R(Z,X)Y = 0$

Use

Essential to prove that $K$ determines $R$.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109The Bianchi Identity
The first Bianchi identity is the cyclic sum of R over its first three arguments vanishing. It is an algebraic constraint following from the symmetry of the Levi-Civita connection, and it is indispensable when proving that sectional curvature determines the full tensor.

Sectional Curvature $K(\sigma)$

A plane invariant

Assign a number to each tangent two-plane $\sigma$.

Gauss link

For surfaces, $K(\sigma)$ is the Gauss curvature.

Definition

$K(\sigma) = \dfrac{\langle R(x,y)x,\,y\rangle}{|x|^2|y|^2 - \langle x,y\rangle^2}$

Normalization

The denominator is the squared area of the spanning parallelogram.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109Sectional Curvature
Sectional curvature assigns a number to each tangent two-plane by evaluating R on an orthonormal basis of that plane. The denominator normalizes for non-orthonormal spanning vectors. This single function of two-planes is the most geometric curvature invariant and generalizes the Gauss curvature of surfaces.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 4

Geodesics and the Sign of $K$

$K>0$

Geodesics converge (sphere).

$K=0$

Geodesics stay parallel (plane).

$K<0$

Geodesics spread apart (saddle).

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109Sphere, Plane, Saddle
Positive sectional curvature makes nearby geodesics converge as on a sphere; zero curvature keeps them parallel as in the plane; negative curvature makes them spread as on a saddle. This trichotomy is the visual signature of the sign of K and the payoff of the whole construction.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 4

Sectional Curvature Determines $R$

Theorem

Knowing $K(\sigma)$ for all $\sigma$ fixes the full tensor $R$.

Method

Polarization plus the algebraic symmetries.

Constant curvature model

$\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle = K\big(\langle X,Z\rangle\langle Y,W\rangle - \langle Y,Z\rangle\langle X,W\rangle\big)$

Reading

Constant $K$ gives a single explicit formula for $R$.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109K Determines R
A theorem of the chapter states that if sectional curvature is known on every two-plane then the full curvature tensor is determined. The proof uses the algebraic symmetries and a polarization argument. Constant sectional curvature yields the explicit model expression for R.

Ricci Curvature

A trace

Average sectional curvatures of planes containing $x$.

Volume

Measures the spreading of a geodesic cone about $x$.

Definition

$\mathrm{Ric}(x) = \dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i} \langle R(x,e_i)x,\,e_i\rangle$

Form

A symmetric bilinear form on each tangent space.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109Ricci Curvature
Ricci curvature is the trace of the curvature operator in one slot: average the sectional curvatures of planes containing a fixed direction. It is a symmetric bilinear form measuring how volume of a thin geodesic cone deviates from the Euclidean reference along that direction.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 4

Scalar Curvature

Full trace

Average Ricci over all directions at a point.

Coarsest

A single number summarizing all bending at $p$.

Definition

$S(p) = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j} \mathrm{Ric}(e_j,e_j)$

Volume defect

Controls how small geodesic balls differ from Euclidean balls.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109Scalar Curvature
Scalar curvature is the full trace of Ricci, a single number at each point averaging all sectional curvatures through it. It is the coarsest curvature invariant and controls the leading volume defect of small geodesic balls compared with Euclidean balls of equal radius.

The Hierarchy of Curvature

Tensor

$R$ carries all curvature data.

Sectional

$K$ on every two-plane.

Ricci

Trace over a direction.

Scalar

Trace at a point.

do Carmo, printed pp. 88-109The Hierarchy of Curvature
We close by stacking the invariants: the full tensor R, its sectional refinement on two-planes, the Ricci trace on directions, and the scalar trace at points. Each step discards information for a coarser summary, and recognizing what survives each trace is the chapter's organizing idea.
