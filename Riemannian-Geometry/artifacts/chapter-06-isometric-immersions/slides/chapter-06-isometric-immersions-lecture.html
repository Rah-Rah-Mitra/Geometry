Isometric Immersions

Intrinsic geometry meets ambient bending

Setting

A manifold $M$ immersed isometrically in $\bar M$.

Question

How does curvature of $M$ relate to its bending in $\bar M$?

Key tool

The second fundamental form $B$.

Chapter six, printed pages 124 to 143, studies a Riemannian manifold sitting isometrically inside a larger one. The central question is how the intrinsic geometry of the submanifold relates to the way it bends inside the ambient space, captured by the second fundamental form.

Immersions and the Induced Metric

Immersion

$f:M^n\to\bar M^{n+k}$ with injective $df_p$.

Pullback

The ambient metric induces $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ on $M$.

Splitting

$T_p\bar M = T_pM \oplus (T_pM)^\perp$.

Orthogonal decomposition

$T_p\bar M = T_pM \;\oplus\; N_pM$

Local picture

Work in a neighborhood where $f$ is an embedding; extend fields to $\bar M$.

Printed pp. 124-143Immersions and the Metric
An immersion has injective differential, so the ambient metric pulls back to a metric on the source. Locally the submanifold splits each ambient tangent space into tangent and normal parts, and this orthogonal decomposition is the engine driving every formula in the chapter.

Splitting the Ambient Connection

Tangential part

The projection recovers the induced connection $\nabla_X Y$.

Normal part

The remainder $B(X,Y)$ measures bending.

Gauss formula

$\bar\nabla_X Y = \nabla_X Y + B(X,Y)$

Printed pp. 124-143Splitting the Connection
Extend tangent fields to the ambient space and apply its Levi-Civita connection. Projecting the result onto the tangent space recovers the induced connection, while the leftover normal component is new information measuring how the submanifold curves away from flatness inside the ambient manifold.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

The Second Fundamental Form

Definition

$B(X,Y)=\bar\nabla_X Y-\nabla_X Y$, a normal vector.

Symmetric

$B(X,Y)=B(Y,X)$ from a torsion-free $\bar\nabla$.

Tensorial

Bilinear over functions in $X$ and $Y$.

Bilinear map

$B:\,T_pM\times T_pM \to N_pM$

Geometric meaning

$B$ records how a tangent direction is forced off $M$ in the ambient space; it vanishes iff $M$ is totally geodesic.

Printed pp. 124-143The Second Fundamental Form
The normal part of the ambient derivative defines the second fundamental form B, a symmetric bilinear map sending two tangent vectors to a normal vector. Symmetry follows from the torsion-free ambient connection, and B vanishes exactly when the submanifold is totally geodesic.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

The Shape (Weingarten) Operator

Definition

For unit normal $\eta$: $\langle S_\eta X, Y\rangle=\langle B(X,Y),\eta\rangle$.

Self-adjoint

$S_\eta$ is symmetric, so it diagonalizes.

Weingarten

$S_\eta X = -(\bar\nabla_X \eta)^\top$

Printed pp. 124-143The Shape Operator
Fixing a unit normal converts the second fundamental form into a self-adjoint shape operator on the tangent space. The Weingarten equation shows this operator is minus the tangential rate of change of the normal field, so it records how the normal tilts as you move.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

The Gauss Equation

Statement

Ambient curvature equals intrinsic curvature plus quadratic $B$ terms.

Theorema Egregium

For surfaces in $\mathbb R^3$: $K=\det S$ is intrinsic.

Gauss equation

$\langle \bar R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle = \langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle$

$-\,\langle B(Y,W),B(X,Z)\rangle + \langle B(X,W),B(Y,Z)\rangle$

Reading

Bending generates intrinsic curvature: the same surface bent differently carries different $K$ only if $\bar R$ allows.

Printed pp. 124-143Gauss Equation
The Gauss equation expresses the ambient sectional curvature in terms of the intrinsic curvature plus quadratic terms in the second fundamental form. This is the precise algebraic statement of how bending creates intrinsic curvature, generalizing the Theorema Egregium of classical surface theory.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

The Codazzi Equation

What it controls

The tangential derivative of $B$.

Integrability

A compatibility condition on the bending data.

Codazzi equation

$(\bar R(X,Y)Z)^\perp = (\nabla_X B)(Y,Z) - (\nabla_Y B)(X,Z)$

Constant curvature

When $\bar M$ has constant curvature the right side vanishes: $B$ is a Codazzi tensor.

Printed pp. 124-143Codazzi Equation
The Codazzi equation governs the tangential derivative of the second fundamental form. It states that the normal component of the ambient curvature equals the difference of covariant derivatives of B, providing the integrability condition that the tangential bending data must satisfy.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

Normal Connection and the Ricci Equation

Normal connection

$\nabla^\perp_X \eta = (\bar\nabla_X\eta)^\perp$ on $N_pM$.

Normal curvature

$R^\perp$ measures twisting of the normal bundle.

Ricci equation

$\langle \bar R(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle = \langle R^\perp(X,Y)\eta,\zeta\rangle - \langle [S_\eta,S_\zeta]X,Y\rangle$

Third of the trio

Gauss, Codazzi, Ricci jointly capture all local data of the immersion.

Printed pp. 124-143Normal Connection and Ricci
The normal bundle carries its own metric-compatible connection, and its curvature is measured by the Ricci equation. This equation links the normal curvature, the ambient curvature, and commutators of the shape operators, completing the trio of fundamental equations that govern an isometric immersion locally.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

Principal, Gaussian, Mean Curvature

Principal curvatures

Eigenvalues $k_1,\dots,k_n$ of $S_\eta$.

Gaussian

$K=\det S_\eta = k_1\cdots k_n$.

Mean

$H=\tfrac1n\operatorname{tr}S_\eta$.

Printed pp. 124-143Principal and Gaussian Curvature
Eigenvalues of the shape operator are the principal curvatures, and the eigendirections are the principal directions. Their product is the Gaussian curvature and their average is the mean curvature. For a hypersurface these few scalars completely summarize the entire second fundamental form at a single point.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

Mean Curvature Vector and Minimal Submanifolds

Mean curvature vector

$\vec H=\tfrac1n\operatorname{tr}B$, a normal vector.

Minimal

$\vec H\equiv 0$: a critical point of area.

Minimal condition

$\vec H = \tfrac1n\sum_i B(e_i,e_i) = 0$

Example

A soap film spanning a wire loop minimizes area, so its mean curvature vanishes pointwise.

Printed pp. 124-143Mean Curvature and Minimal
The mean curvature vector is the normalized trace of the second fundamental form across an orthonormal tangent frame. Submanifolds with vanishing mean curvature are called minimal: they are critical points of the area functional. Soap films model this condition, balancing surface tension equally in all tangent directions.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

Totally Geodesic Submanifolds

Definition

$B\equiv 0$ identically on $M$.

Consequence

Geodesics of $M$ are geodesics of $\bar M$.

Examples

Great circles on $S^n$; linear subspaces of $\mathbb R^n$.

Printed pp. 124-143Totally Geodesic Submanifolds
When the second fundamental form vanishes identically the immersion is totally geodesic, and geodesics of the submanifold are geodesics of the ambient space. Great circles on spheres and linear subspaces in Euclidean space are the model examples of this rigidity.
Riemannian GeometryChapter 6

Synthesis: The Three Fundamental Equations

Gauss

Tangential curvature: intrinsic $R$ + $B$ terms.

Codazzi

Mixed component: derivative of $B$.

Ricci

Normal curvature: $R^\perp$ + shape commutators.

Decomposition of ambient curvature

$\bar R = \underbrace{R + B}_{\text{Gauss}} \;\oplus\; \underbrace{\nabla B}_{\text{Codazzi}} \;\oplus\; \underbrace{R^\perp}_{\text{Ricci}}$

Takeaway

The ambient curvature of an immersion splits cleanly into intrinsic curvature, bending of the tangent bundle, and twisting of the normal bundle.

Printed pp. 124-143Synthesis
Gauss, Codazzi, and Ricci together encode all the local geometry an isometric immersion can have. They split ambient curvature into intrinsic curvature, the bending of the tangent bundle, and the twisting of the normal bundle, unifying the chapter into one structural picture.
