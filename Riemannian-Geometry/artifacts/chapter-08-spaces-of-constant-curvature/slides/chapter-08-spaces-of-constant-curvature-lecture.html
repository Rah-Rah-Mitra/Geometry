Constant Sectional Curvature

Definition

$M$ has constant curvature if $K(\sigma)$ is independent of the plane $\sigma\subset T_pM$ and of the point $p$.

Why it is special

Among all curvature hypotheses this is the most rigid short of vanishing curvature.

Sectional curvature

$K(\sigma)=\dfrac{\langle R(X,Y)Y,X\rangle}{|X|^2|Y|^2-\langle X,Y\rangle^2}$

Consequence

A single scalar $K$ now governs every two-plane in the tangent bundle.

Constant curvature means the sectional curvature does not depend on the chosen tangent plane or base point. We unpack what that single number controls and why it is the strongest possible curvature hypothesis short of flatness, setting up the algebraic form of the tensor.

The Curvature Tensor Formula

Lemma

Constant $K$ forces the Riemann tensor into a single algebraic shape determined by the metric.

Reading it

No extra data: $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ and the number $K$ fix every component.

Constant-curvature tensor

$R(X,Y)Z = K\big(\langle Y,Z\rangle X - \langle X,Z\rangle Y\big)$

do Carmo, Riemannian Geometry — pp. 155-190The Curvature Tensor Formula
When sectional curvature is the constant K, the full Riemann tensor collapses to one universal algebraic expression built from the metric alone. We read off the formula and note that the metric, not extra data, now determines all curvature components.

Three Model Geometries

$S^n$, $K=1$

Round sphere. Geodesics are great circles; no parallels.

$\mathbb{R}^n$, $K=0$

Euclidean. Geodesics are straight lines; unique parallel.

$H^n$, $K=-1$

Hyperbolic. Geodesics diverge; infinitely many parallels.

The sign of K splits the world into three simply connected model spaces: the round sphere for positive curvature, Euclidean space for zero, and hyperbolic space for negative. We preview their geodesics and the way triangles behave in each.

The Sphere $S^n$: $K=1$

Geodesics

Great circles; any two distinct ones intersect, so parallels do not exist.

Global shape

Compact, finite volume, diameter $\pi$ at unit radius.

Curvature tensor

$R(X,Y)Z=\langle Y,Z\rangle X-\langle X,Z\rangle Y$

do Carmo, Riemannian Geometry — pp. 155-190The Sphere
On the unit sphere geodesics are great circles and any two of them meet, so there are no parallels. Curvature is positive everywhere, distances are bounded, and the geometry is the prototype of a closed, finite-volume space form.

Euclidean Space $\mathbb{R}^n$: $K=0$

Geodesics

Ordinary straight lines; the parallel postulate holds exactly.

Flatness

The Riemann tensor vanishes identically: $R\equiv 0$.

Borderline case

$R(X,Y)Z=0\quad(K=0)$

do Carmo, Riemannian Geometry — pp. 155-190Euclidean Space
Flat space is the borderline case where geodesics are ordinary straight lines and the parallel postulate holds exactly. The curvature tensor vanishes identically, and this is the unique simply connected model sitting between the spherical and hyperbolic worlds.

Hyperbolic Space $H^n$: $K=-1$

Poincare disk

Geodesics are diameters and arcs meeting the boundary at right angles.

Many parallels

Through a point off a geodesic pass infinitely many disjoint geodesics.

Curvature tensor

$R(X,Y)Z=-\big(\langle Y,Z\rangle X-\langle X,Z\rangle Y\big)$

Hyperbolic space carries constant negative curvature, modelled here on the Poincare disk where geodesics are diameters and circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles. Through any point off a geodesic pass infinitely many non-intersecting lines.

Cartan's Determination Theorem

Statement

If $M$ and $\tilde M$ have the same constant curvature $K$, then near any pair of points they are isometric.

Mechanism

A linear isometry of tangent spaces transports through $\exp_p$ to a local isometry.

Local isometry

$\varphi=\exp_{\tilde p}\circ\, i\circ\exp_p^{-1},\quad i:T_pM\to T_{\tilde p}\tilde M$

do Carmo, Riemannian Geometry — pp. 155-190Cartan's Determination Theorem
Cartan's theorem says curvature determines local geometry. If two spaces share the same constant curvature, the exponential maps furnish a local isometry between them, so the three model spaces exhaust all local possibilities up to isometry.

Space Forms as Quotients

Universal cover

A complete constant-curvature $M$ has universal cover $S^n$, $\mathbb{R}^n$, or $H^n$.

Quotient

$M=\tilde M/\Gamma$ where $\Gamma$ acts freely and properly discontinuously by isometries.

Classification

$M\cong \tilde M/\Gamma,\quad \Gamma\subset \mathrm{Isom}(\tilde M),\ \pi_1(M)\cong\Gamma$

do Carmo, Riemannian Geometry — pp. 155-190Space Forms as Quotients
Complete constant-curvature manifolds are quotients of a model space by a discrete group of isometries acting freely and properly discontinuously. The universal cover is one of the three models, and the deck transformations record the global topology.

Discrete Isometry Groups

$K=0$: tori

Lattices of translations of $\mathbb{R}^n$ yield flat tori and Bieberbach manifolds.

$K=1$: lens spaces

Finite fixed-point-free subgroups of $O(n+1)$ act on $S^n$.

$K=-1$: surfaces

Fuchsian groups act on $H^2$ to build hyperbolic surfaces of genus $\geq 2$.

Recap and Synthesis

One tensor

$R(X,Y)Z=K(\langle Y,Z\rangle X-\langle X,Z\rangle Y)$ encodes constant curvature.

Three models

$S^n$, $\mathbb{R}^n$, $H^n$ as the sign of $K$ runs through $+,0,-$.

Cartan rigidity

Equal curvature forces local isometry through the exponential map.

Global classification

Complete forms are discrete quotients $\tilde M/\Gamma$ of the models.
