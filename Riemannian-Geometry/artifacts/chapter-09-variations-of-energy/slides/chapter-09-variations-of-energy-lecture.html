The Energy Functional

Definition

Energy integrates squared speed along the curve.

Versus length

Cauchy-Schwarz ties length to energy.

Energy

$E(c) = \tfrac12\displaystyle\int_a^b \big|\,c'(t)\,\big|^2\, dt$

Inequality

$L(c)^2 \le 2(b-a)\,E(c)$

Equality

Equality holds iff the speed is constant.

Printed pp. 191-209The Energy Functional
Energy weights speed quadratically, unlike arc length, which makes it differentiable and minimized exactly by constant-speed parametrizations. Cauchy-Schwarz gives length squared at most twice energy times interval length, with equality precisely for constant speed, so minimizing energy chooses both shortest path and uniform pacing.

Variations of a Curve

Variation

A smooth family $f(s,t)$ deforming the base curve.

Proper

Endpoints stay fixed for every parameter $s$.

Variational field

$V(t) = \left.\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial s}\right|_{s=0}$

Printed pp. 191-209Variations of a Curve
A proper variation is a smooth family of curves sharing the same endpoints, indexed by a parameter that deforms the base curve. Its variational field measures the transverse velocity at parameter zero, and differentiating energy along the family converts geometry into a single ordinary derivative we can evaluate.

First Variation Formula

Interior term

Pairs $V$ against the covariant acceleration.

Boundary term

Records how the endpoints move.

First variation

$\tfrac12 E'(0) = -\!\displaystyle\int_a^b \!\Big\langle V, \tfrac{D}{dt}c'\Big\rangle dt + \big\langle V, c'\big\rangle\Big|_a^b$

Integration by parts

Curvature of the curve separates from endpoint motion.

Printed pp. 191-209First Variation Formula
Differentiating energy and integrating by parts splits the result into an interior integral against the acceleration field plus boundary terms. The interior term measures geodesic curvature, while the boundary term records endpoint motion, cleanly separating where a curve fails to be straight from how its ends move.

Geodesics Are Critical Points

Proper variation

Boundary term vanishes since endpoints are fixed.

Vanishing condition

$E'(0)=0$ for all $V$ forces zero acceleration.

Theorem

$E'(0)=0\ \forall V \iff \dfrac{D}{dt}c' \equiv 0$

Conclusion

Critical points of energy are exactly the geodesics.

Printed pp. 191-209Geodesics Are Critical Points
For proper variations the boundary term vanishes, so the first variation is zero for every admissible field exactly when the covariant acceleration vanishes identically. Thus geodesics are precisely the critical points of energy among curves with fixed endpoints, the variational characterization underpinning the entire chapter.

Second Variation Formula

At a geodesic

Second derivative of energy equals the index form.

Curvature enters

Sectional curvature competes with the field's stretch.

Second variation

$\tfrac12 E''(0) = \displaystyle\int_a^b\!\Big(\big|\tfrac{DV}{dt}\big|^2 - \langle R(V,c')c', V\rangle\Big)dt$

Reading

Positive curvature subtracts from the cost.

Printed pp. 191-209Second Variation Formula
The second derivative of energy at a geodesic equals the index form evaluated on the variational field. It pairs the squared covariant derivative of the field against a curvature term, exposing exactly how sectional curvature governs whether nearby curves cost more or less energy than the geodesic.

The Index Form

Bilinear form

Symmetric on fields vanishing at endpoints.

Definiteness

Positive definite means the geodesic minimizes locally.

Index form

$I(V,W) = \displaystyle\int_a^b\!\Big(\big\langle\tfrac{DV}{dt},\tfrac{DW}{dt}\big\rangle - \langle R(V,c')c',W\rangle\Big)dt$

Negative direction

Any $I(V,V)<0$ gives a shorter competitor.

Printed pp. 191-209The Index Form
The index form is a symmetric bilinear form on fields vanishing at the endpoints, built from the covariant derivative and curvature. Its definiteness decides stability: positive definite means the geodesic locally minimizes energy, while any negative direction exhibits a shorter nearby competitor and certifies instability.

Jacobi Fields and Conjugate Points

Kernel

Null directions of $I$ solve the Jacobi equation.

Conjugate

Endpoints joined by a Jacobi field vanishing at both.

Jacobi equation

$\dfrac{D^2 J}{dt^2} + R(J,c')c' = 0$

Printed pp. 191-209Jacobi Fields and Conjugate Points
Nontrivial fields in the kernel of the index form solve the Jacobi equation, linking second variation to the geodesic deviation studied earlier. Endpoints joined by such a field vanishing at both ends are conjugate, marking where neighbouring geodesics refocus and where minimization can first break down.

Past a Conjugate Point

Instability

Beyond the first conjugate point $I$ has a negative direction.

Construction

Cut and rescale the Jacobi field into a test variation.

Printed pp. 191-209Past a Conjugate Point
Beyond the first conjugate point the index form acquires a negative direction, so the geodesic no longer minimizes energy. An explicit variation built from the Jacobi field, cut and rescaled, produces a strictly shorter neighbouring curve, visually a transverse push that lowers energy past the focal point.

Bonnet-Myers Theorem

Hypothesis

Complete, with Ricci bounded below by $(n-1)/r^2 > 0$.

Mechanism

Long geodesics gain a negative index direction.

Theorem

$\operatorname{Ric}\ge \tfrac{n-1}{r^2} \implies \operatorname{diam}(M)\le \pi r$

Consequence

$M$ is compact with finite fundamental group.

Printed pp. 191-209Bonnet-Myers Theorem
If Ricci curvature is bounded below by a positive constant, the second variation applied to long geodesics forces a negative index direction, contradicting minimality beyond a fixed length. Hence a complete manifold with such curvature has diameter at most pi over the square root of that bound, making it compact.
