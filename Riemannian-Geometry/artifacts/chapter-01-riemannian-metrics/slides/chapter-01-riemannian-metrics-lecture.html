A Smoothly Varying Inner Product

Pointwise

Symmetric, bilinear, positive definite on each $T_pM$.

Smooth

Coefficients vary differentiably with the base point.

Why both matter

Positivity gives a norm; smoothness ties points together.

Definition

$\langle u,v\rangle_p \;=\; g_p(u,v),\quad g_p(u,u)>0\ \text{for}\ u\neq 0$

Manifold + metric

The pair $(M,g)$ is a Riemannian manifold.

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Smoothly Varying Inner Product
A Riemannian metric assigns to each point a positive definite symmetric bilinear form on the tangent space, and the assignment must vary differentiably with the point. Differentiability is the word that turns pointwise linear algebra into geometry on the whole manifold, so we stress it here.

The Metric in Local Coordinates

Coordinate frame

Basis $\partial_1,\dots,\partial_n$ from a chart $x$.

Components

$g_{ij}$ are smooth functions, symmetric in $i,j$.

Positive definite

The matrix $[g_{ij}]$ is positive definite at every point.

Components

$g_{ij}(p)=\big\langle \partial_i,\partial_j\big\rangle_p,\qquad \langle u,v\rangle = g_{ij}\,u^i v^j$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Metric in Local Coordinates
In a chart the metric becomes a symmetric positive definite matrix of smooth functions, the components g of i and j. Reading the inner product of coordinate vector fields recovers these functions, and every computation in the chapter eventually reduces to manipulating this matrix.

Field of Metric Ellipses

The unit ball of each inner product, drawn point by point

Unit ball

$\{u:\langle u,u\rangle_p=1\}$ is an ellipse in $T_pM$.

Reading it

Stretch and tilt encode how $g$ distorts directions.

Flat vs curved

Constant field is Euclidean; varying field is not.

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Field of Metric Ellipses
Picturing the unit ball of each inner product gives an ellipse at every point, and the way these ellipses stretch and tilt across the manifold is exactly the geometric content of the metric. A constant field is flat; a varying field encodes curvature to come.

Length of a Curve

Speed

$\lVert c'(t)\rVert = \sqrt{\langle c'(t),c'(t)\rangle}$.

Length

Integrate speed over the parameter interval.

Invariance

Independent of orientation-preserving reparametrization.

Length functional

$\ell(c)=\displaystyle\int_a^b \sqrt{g_{ij}\,\dot c^i \dot c^j}\;dt$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Length of a Curve
The speed of a curve at each instant is the metric norm of its velocity vector, and integrating that speed over the parameter interval defines the length. This integral is independent of reparametrization, so length is a genuine geometric quantity attached to the image curve.

Arc Length and Distance

Arc length

$s(t)=\int_a^t \lVert c'(\tau)\rVert\,d\tau$ is a unit-speed parameter.

Distance

Infimum of lengths over all curves joining $p$ to $q$.

Metric space

$d$ recovers the manifold topology.

Riemannian distance

$d(p,q)=\inf_{c}\ \ell(c),\quad c(a)=p,\ c(b)=q$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Arc Length and Distance
Arc length measured from a base point gives a preferred parameter in which the curve has unit speed. Taking the infimum of lengths over all curves joining two points defines the Riemannian distance, which turns a connected manifold into a metric space compatible with its topology.

Example: The Euclidean Metric

Standard form

The constant dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Components

$g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$, the identity matrix everywhere.

Ellipses

Every unit ball is a round circle of equal radius.

Flat model

$\langle u,v\rangle=\textstyle\sum_i u^i v^i,\qquad \ell(c)=\int_a^b |c'(t)|\,dt$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Euclidean Metric
On real n space the standard dot product, constant in every chart, is the model Riemannian metric. Its components form the identity matrix, lengths reduce to the familiar integral of the Euclidean speed, and every ellipse in the field is a round circle of the same radius.

Example: The Hyperbolic Half-Plane

Upper half-plane

$\mathbb{H}=\{(x,y): y>0\}$ with the Poincare metric.

Conformal scale

Euclidean form scaled by $1/y^2$.

Boundary

Distances diverge as $y\to 0$.

Poincare metric

$g_{ij}=\dfrac{\delta_{ij}}{y^{2}},\qquad ds^2=\dfrac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2}$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Hyperbolic Half-Plane
The Poincare upper half-plane carries the metric obtained by scaling the Euclidean form by one over y squared. Near the boundary distances blow up, so the field of ellipses shrinks downward; this single example already shows how varying metrics produce genuinely non-Euclidean geometry.

Induced Metric on a Submanifold

Immersion

$f:M\to N$ with injective derivative.

Pullback

Restrict the ambient inner product to tangent directions.

Surfaces

This is the classical first fundamental form.

Pulled-back metric

$\langle u,v\rangle_M=\big\langle df_p(u),\,df_p(v)\big\rangle_N$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Induced Metric
An immersion pulls the ambient inner product back to the source, restricting it to tangent directions of the submanifold. A surface in three space inherits its first fundamental form this way, and the construction shows that submanifolds of any Riemannian manifold are themselves Riemannian.

Product Metrics

Tangent splitting

$T_{(p,q)}(M\times N)\cong T_pM\oplus T_qN$.

Add the factors

The metric is the orthogonal sum of the two.

Flat torus

$S^1\times S^1$ from a product of circles.

Product metric

$\langle (u_1,u_2),(v_1,v_2)\rangle = \langle u_1,v_1\rangle_M + \langle u_2,v_2\rangle_N$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Product Metrics
On a product of two Riemannian manifolds the natural metric adds the two factor inner products on the splitting of the tangent space. The flat torus arises this way from a product of circles, and lengths of curves separate into contributions from each factor.

Isometries and Local Isometries

Isometry

Diffeomorphism whose derivative preserves $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$.

Consequence

Lengths, angles and distance are unchanged.

Local version

Drop the global bijection: indistinguishable in the small.

Isometry condition

$\big\langle df_p(u),df_p(v)\big\rangle_{f(p)} = \langle u,v\rangle_p$

do Carmo · printed pp. 35-47Isometries
A diffeomorphism is an isometry when its derivative preserves the inner product at every point, so it carries lengths, angles and distances unchanged. Dropping the global bijection requirement gives local isometries, the maps under which two manifolds are geometrically indistinguishable in the small.
