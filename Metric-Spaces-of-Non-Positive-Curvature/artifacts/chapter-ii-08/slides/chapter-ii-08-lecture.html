The Boundary at Infinity of a CAT(0) Space

Ideal points from geodesic rays, the cone topology, and horofunctions

Goal

Attach to a complete CAT(0) space $X$ a set of ideal points $\partial_\infty X$ recording the directions geodesics escape to.

One equivalence

Two geodesic rays name the same ideal point when they stay a bounded distance apart for all time.

Printed pp. 260-276The Boundary at Infinity
Open by motivating the question. A complete CAT(0) space has no edge, yet we want to attach ideal points that record the directions in which geodesics escape. Stress that the entire construction rests on geodesic rays and a single equivalence: rays that stay a bounded distance apart name the same point at infinity.

Geodesic Rays

Definition

A geodesic ray is an isometric embedding $c:[0,\infty)\to X$, so $d(c(s),c(t))=|s-t|$.

Abundance

In a complete CAT(0) space rays exist freely; convexity of the metric keeps them from wandering.

Unit-speed ray

$d\big(c(s),c(t)\big)=|s-t|,\quad c(0)=p$

Convexity

$t\mapsto d\big(c(t),c'(t)\big)$ is convex for any two rays — the engine behind everything below.

Printed pp. 260-276Geodesic Rays
Define a geodesic ray as an isometric embedding of the half-line. Emphasise that in a complete CAT(0) space rays exist in abundance: from any basepoint and any other point you can extend, and convexity of the metric keeps rays from oscillating. This is the raw material for every ideal point.

Asymptotic Rays

The relation

Rays $c,c'$ are asymptotic if $d\big(c(t),c'(t)\big)$ stays bounded as $t\to\infty$.

Equivalence

Reflexive, symmetric, transitive — so it partitions all rays into classes.

Asymptotic

$\sup_{t\ge 0}\, d\big(c(t),c'(t)\big)<\infty$

Printed pp. 260-276Asymptotic Rays
Introduce the asymptotic relation. Two rays are asymptotic when the distance between corresponding points stays bounded for all time. Walk through why this is an equivalence relation, and note the picture: two rays leaving different basepoints but shadowing each other forever define one ideal direction.

The Visual Boundary $\partial_\infty X$

Definition

$\partial_\infty X$ is the set of asymptotic classes of geodesic rays in $X$.

Rigidity

In complete CAT(0) $X$: from each basepoint $p$, exactly one ray represents each boundary point.

Boundary as rays from a point

$\partial_\infty X \;\cong\; \{\text{rays } c:\, c(0)=p\}$

Consequence

The identification is independent of $p$, giving a canonical, basepoint-free set of ideal points.

Printed pp. 260-276The Visual Boundary
Define the visual boundary as the set of asymptotic classes of rays. Give the key rigidity fact for complete CAT(0) spaces: from any basepoint exactly one ray represents each boundary point. So the boundary is canonically the set of rays issuing from a fixed origin, which we will topologise next.

The Cone Topology on $\bar X = X\cup\partial_\infty X$

Neighbourhoods

Fix radius $r$ and tolerance $\varepsilon$. Collect points and rays whose geodesics from $p$ stay $\varepsilon$-close out to radius $r$.

Basepoint-free

The resulting topology does not depend on $p$.

Printed pp. 260-276The Cone Topology
Explain the cone topology through nested neighbourhoods of a ray: fix a radius and a tolerance, and collect points and rays whose geodesics stay close out to that radius. Stress that the topology is independent of basepoint and that it glues the boundary smoothly onto the space, building the bar-X compactification.

When is $\bar X$ a Compactification?

Theorem

If $X$ is a proper complete CAT(0) space, then $\bar X$ with the cone topology is compact and $\partial_\infty X$ is a closed subset.

Proof idea

Arzelà–Ascoli: unit-speed rays from $p$ form an equicontinuous family in a proper space.

Properness matters

Closed balls must be compact. Drop this and $\bar X$ can fail to be compact.

Embedding

$X$ is open and dense in $\bar X$; the cone topology restricts to the metric topology on $X$.

Printed pp. 260-276Compactification
State the compactness theorem: when X is proper and complete, bar-X is compact and the boundary is closed. The proof idea is Arzela-Ascoli on unit-speed rays from the basepoint. Warn that properness is essential; in infinite-dimensional CAT(0) spaces the visual boundary need not be compact.

Busemann Functions

Definition

For a ray $c$, the Busemann function is a renormalised limit of distance functions.

Properties

$b_c$ is convex and $1$-Lipschitz; its level sets are horospheres.

Busemann function

$b_c(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\big(d(x,c(t))-t\big)$

Up to a constant

Asymptotic rays give Busemann functions differing by a constant, so each $\xi\in\partial_\infty X$ has a well-defined horofunction up to additive constant.

Printed pp. 260-276Busemann Functions
Define the Busemann function of a ray as the renormalised limit of distance functions. Each ray gives a 1-Lipschitz convex function whose level sets are horospheres. Highlight that adding a constant or choosing an asymptotic ray shifts the function by a constant, so it is well defined up to additive constant per boundary point.

Horoballs and Horospheres

Definitions

$\text{horoball: } \{b_c\le r\}\quad \text{horosphere: } \{b_c=r\}$

Convexity

$b_c$ convex $\Rightarrow$ every horoball is a convex set.

Printed pp. 260-276Horoballs and Horospheres
Describe sublevel sets of Busemann functions as horoballs, with horospheres as their boundaries. In Euclidean space horospheres are hyperplanes; in hyperbolic space they are the familiar tangent spheres. Convexity of the Busemann function makes every horoball convex, a fact used constantly when studying parabolic isometries.

The Action of $\mathrm{Isom}(X)$ on $\partial_\infty X$

Extension

Every isometry sends rays to rays and preserves the asymptotic relation, so it extends to a homeomorphism of $\partial_\infty X$.

Induced map

$\gamma\cdot[c]=[\gamma\circ c],\quad \gamma\in\mathrm{Isom}(X)$

Elliptic / hyperbolic

Fixed points or invariant axes inside $X$; hyperbolics fix two boundary points.

Parabolic

Fixes a single $\xi\in\partial_\infty X$ and permutes its horoballs.

Printed pp. 260-276Isometries Act on the Boundary
Explain that every isometry of X extends to a homeomorphism of the boundary, because it sends rays to rays and preserves the asymptotic relation. This action classifies isometries: elliptic and hyperbolic ones have fixed structure inside X, while parabolics fix a boundary point and preserve its horoballs.

Example: $\partial_\infty \mathbb{E}^n = S^{n-1}$

Directions

Two rays in $\mathbb{E}^n$ are asymptotic iff they are parallel and point the same way.

Boundary

$\partial_\infty \mathbb{E}^n \cong S^{n-1}$ (round sphere of directions)

Busemann is affine

$b_c(x)=-\langle x-p,\,v\rangle$ for unit direction $v$; horospheres are hyperplanes $\perp v$.

Printed pp. 260-276Example: Euclidean Space
Work the cleanest example. The boundary of n-dimensional Euclidean space is the round sphere of directions, since two rays are asymptotic exactly when they are parallel and point the same way. Busemann functions are affine, horospheres are hyperplanes. This is the model every other example is compared against.

Examples: Trees and $\mathbb{H}^n$

Tree → Cantor set

The boundary of a regular simplicial tree is its space of ends: totally disconnected, a Cantor set when branching is uniform.

$\mathbb{H}^n$ → sphere

Hyperbolic $n$-space again has boundary $S^{n-1}$, but the cone topology now carries a conformal structure.

Lesson

Same dimension, very different boundaries: curvature and branching are visible at infinity.

Printed pp. 260-276Examples: Trees and Hyperbolic Space
Contrast two further boundaries. A simplicial tree has a totally disconnected Cantor-set boundary of ends, reflecting branching. Hyperbolic n-space again has a sphere boundary, but the cone topology now carries the conformal structure visible at infinity. Together these show how curvature shapes the boundary.

Synthesis: Three Views of $\partial_\infty X$

Rays

Points of $\partial_\infty X$ are asymptotic classes of geodesic rays.

Cone topology

Compactifies a proper complete $X$ to $\bar X$.

Horofunctions

Busemann functions give horoballs and the parabolic story.

Take-away

$\partial_\infty X = \{\text{rays}\}/\!\sim,\quad \bar X = X\cup\partial_\infty X$

Looking ahead

The boundary is a CAT(0)-isometry invariant; in the hyperbolic setting it is also quasi-isometry invariant, driving rigidity and group-action arguments.

Printed pp. 260-276Synthesis
Close by tying the three viewpoints together: rays give points, the cone topology compactifies, Busemann functions give horofunctions. Remind students that the boundary is a quasi-isometry-flavoured invariant in the hyperbolic setting but only a CAT(0)-isometry invariant in general, and preview its use in rigidity and group actions.
