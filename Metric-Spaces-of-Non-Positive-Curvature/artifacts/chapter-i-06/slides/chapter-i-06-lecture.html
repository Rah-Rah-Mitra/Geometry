The Hyperboloid Model

Lorentzian form

Signature $(n,1)$ bilinear form on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

Upper sheet

Points with $\langle x,x\rangle=-1$, $x_{n+1}>0$.

Distance

$\cosh d(x,y) = -\langle x,y\rangle$

Why it matters

$O(n,1)$ acts by linear maps preserving the form.

Printed pp. 81-96The Hyperboloid Model
Introduce the Lorentzian form of signature (n,1) and the upper sheet where it equals minus one. Distance comes from the inner product through an inverse hyperbolic cosine. Point out that this model is where the isometry group O(n,1) acts most transparently, by linear maps preserving the quadratic form.

The Poincare Ball

Conformal metric

Euclidean metric scaled near the rim.

Boundary at infinity

The unit sphere sits infinitely far away.

Metric

$ds^2 = \dfrac{4\,|dx|^2}{(1-|x|^2)^2}$

Angles

Hyperbolic angle equals Euclidean angle.

Printed pp. 81-96The Poincare Ball
Write the conformal metric on the open unit ball and note the factor that blows up near the boundary, which pushes the rim to infinite distance. Stress conformality: hyperbolic angles equal Euclidean angles here, which is exactly why geodesics meet the bounding sphere orthogonally.

Upper Half-Space and Klein

Half-space

Vertical rays and semicircles centred on the boundary plane.

Metric

$ds^2 = \dfrac{|dx|^2}{x_n^2}$

Klein disk

Geodesics are straight chords, but angles distort.

Dictionary

Printed pp. 81-96Upper Half-Space and Klein
Contrast the half-space, where geodesics are vertical rays and semicircles centred on the boundary plane, with the Klein disk, where geodesics become honest straight chords at the price of losing conformality. The Cayley-type transforms linking these charts are the dictionary entries we keep returning to.

The Isometry Group $O(n,1)$

Identification

$\mathrm{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^n)\cong PO(n,1)$.

Generators

Reflections in hyperbolic hyperplanes.

Mobius view

$g\cdot x = \dfrac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ on the boundary

Conformal

Boundary-preserving conformal maps of the ball.

Printed pp. 81-96The Isometry Group O(n,1)
Identify the full isometry group with the projective Lorentz group, generated by reflections in hyperplanes. Mention the Mobius description in the ball and half-space: isometries are conformal maps preserving the boundary. Reflections generating the group is the recurring structural fact worth memorising.

The Boundary at Infinity

Definition

Asymptoty classes of geodesic rays.

Identification

$\partial_\infty\mathbb{H}^n \cong S^{n-1}$, the rim of the ball.

Asymptotic

$\xi\sim\eta \iff \sup_t d(\xi(t),\eta(t))<\infty$

Action

Isometries extend to conformal homeomorphisms of $S^{n-1}$.

Printed pp. 81-96The Boundary at Infinity
Define boundary points as equivalence classes of asymptotic geodesic rays, and identify the resulting sphere at infinity with the bounding sphere of the ball model. Stress that isometries extend continuously to this sphere, acting as conformal homeomorphisms, which seeds the later study of limit sets.

Horospheres and Horoballs

Horosphere

Limit of spheres whose centre escapes to $\xi$.

Horoball

The region a horosphere bounds.

Companions

Hyperspheres and equidistant hypersurfaces.

Printed pp. 81-96Horospheres and Horoballs
Describe a horosphere as a limit of spheres whose centre escapes to a boundary point, appearing as a sphere internally tangent to the bounding sphere. The region it bounds is a horoball. Contrast horospheres with equidistant hypersurfaces and genuine hyperspheres, which are the three companion families.

Busemann Functions

Definition

Renormalised limit of distance to a ray.

Level sets

Horospheres; sublevel sets are horoballs.

Busemann

$b_\xi(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\big(d(x,\gamma(t))-t\big)$

Gradient

Flow points toward the ideal point $\xi$.

Printed pp. 81-96Busemann Functions
Introduce the Busemann function attached to a ray as the renormalised limit of distance functions. Its level sets are horospheres and its sublevel sets are horoballs. Emphasise that the gradient flow points toward the boundary point, giving a clean analytic handle on the geometry near infinity.

Exponential Divergence of Geodesics

Curvature $-1$

Rays from a point spread exponentially.

Contrast

Euclidean rays spread only linearly.

Spread

$d(\gamma_1(t),\gamma_2(t)) \sim C\,e^{t}$ as $t\to\infty$

Consequence

Thin triangles and Gromov hyperbolicity.

Printed pp. 81-96Exponential Divergence
Make the curvature minus one consequence vivid: two geodesics from a common point separate at an exponential rate set by the hyperbolic sine of the travelled distance. Contrast with the linear spread of Euclidean rays. This single inequality is the engine behind thin triangles and hyperbolicity.
