Comparison Angle

Model triangle

Build the Euclidean triangle with the same three side lengths.

Read the angle

The comparison angle is the model angle at the vertex.

Law of cosines

$$\bar\angle_p(x,y)=\arccos\frac{d(p,x)^2+d(p,y)^2-d(x,y)^2}{2\,d(p,x)\,d(p,y)}$$

Concrete

Three distances determine the comparison angle exactly.

Printed pp. 184-192Comparison Angle
Before defining the true angle we measure the comparison angle of a triangle by building its model triangle in the Euclidean plane and reading the Euclidean angle at the chosen vertex. The law of cosines gives an explicit formula, so comparison angles are concrete numbers we can compute from three distances.

The Alexandrov Angle

Shrink the triangle

Take points $c(t),c'(s)$ near $p$ along the two geodesics.

Limit superior

The angle is the upper limit of comparison angles as $s,t\to0$.

Definition

$$\angle_p(c,c')=\limsup_{s,t\to 0}\ \bar\angle_p\big(c(t),c'(s)\big)$$

Printed pp. 184-192The Alexandrov Angle
The Alexandrov angle between two geodesics issuing from a point is the limit superior of comparison angles of the small triangles cut off as both parameters shrink to zero. This upper limit always exists, lies between zero and pi, and captures the infinitesimal opening between the two directions.

Properties of the Angle

Triangle inequality

For three geodesics from $p$ the angles obey the triangle law.

Upper semicontinuity

Angle cannot jump up under small perturbation of endpoints.

Pseudometric on directions

$$\angle_p(c_1,c_3)\le \angle_p(c_1,c_2)+\angle_p(c_2,c_3)$$

Why it matters

Exactly the regularity needed to pass to limits of geodesics.

Printed pp. 184-192Properties of the Angle
The Alexandrov angle satisfies the triangle inequality among three geodesics from a point, so it behaves like an honest pseudometric on directions. It is also upper semicontinuous in the endpoints, which is exactly the regularity needed to pass to limits and to control how angles vary along families of geodesics.

Angles in CAT(k) Spaces

Monotonicity

Comparison angles do not decrease as points approach $p$.

Limit exists

The limsup is a genuine limit; angle equals the model angle.

Angle sum bound

$$\angle_A+\angle_B+\angle_C\ \le\ \bar\angle_A+\bar\angle_B+\bar\angle_C$$

Reading

Triangles are thinner than their comparison models.

Printed pp. 184-192Angles in CAT(k) Spaces
In a CAT(k) space comparison angles are monotone: as the points slide toward the vertex the comparison angle does not decrease, so the limit superior is a genuine limit. The Alexandrov angle then equals the comparison angle of the model triangle, and angle sums of triangles are no larger than their model values.

Space of Directions

Quotient by zero angle

Geodesics with $\angle_p=0$ define the same direction.

Complete it

The completion is the link $\mathrm{Lk}(p)$, the space of directions.

Printed pp. 184-192Space of Directions
Two geodesics from a point are equivalent when the angle between them is zero. The Alexandrov angle descends to a metric on equivalence classes; completing this pseudometric space yields the space of directions, written as the link of the point. It records all infinitesimal directions in which one can leave the point.

The Tangent Cone

Cone over the link

$T_p X = C_0\,\mathrm{Lk}(p)$ with apex the point $p$ itself.

Pairs

A point is a direction together with a radius $r\ge 0$.

Metric analogue of $T_pX$

$$T_pX=\big(\,[0,\infty)\times\mathrm{Lk}(p)\,\big)\big/\!\sim,\quad (0,\xi)\sim(0,\eta)$$

Role

The natural arena for infinitesimal geometry at $p$.

Printed pp. 184-192The Tangent Cone
The tangent cone at a point is the Euclidean cone over the space of directions. Points are formal pairs of a direction and a non-negative radius, with the apex collapsing all zero-radius pairs. This is the metric analogue of the tangent space and is the natural place to do infinitesimal geometry.

The Euclidean Cone

Add a radius

Take $Y$ of diameter $\le\pi$ and a radial coordinate $r\ge0$.

Glue the apex

All radius-zero points collapse to one apex $0$.

Printed pp. 184-192The Euclidean Cone
Given any metric space Y of diameter at most pi we build the Euclidean cone by adding a radial coordinate and gluing all points at radius zero to a single apex. The cone distance comes from the spherical law of cosines using the Y-distance as the angular separation between two rays.

Cones and CAT(0)

Cone metric

Distance from the spherical cosine rule on radii and angle.

Berestovskii

$C_0 Y$ is CAT(0) exactly when $Y$ is CAT(1).

Cone distance

$$d\big((r,\xi),(s,\eta)\big)^2=r^2+s^2-2rs\cos\big(\min(d_Y(\xi,\eta),\pi)\big)$$

Theorem

$$C_0 Y\ \text{is CAT}(0)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ Y\ \text{is CAT}(1)$$

Printed pp. 184-192Cones and CAT(0)
Berestovskii's theorem is the structural payoff: the Euclidean cone over Y is a CAT(0) space if and only if Y itself is a CAT(1) space. Curvature of the cone is therefore completely governed by curvature of its link, turning a global condition into a condition on directions.

The Spherical Join

Sew by right triangles

A join point is $(\theta,\xi,\eta)$ with $\theta\in[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]$.

Orthogonal sum

The link-level analogue of a direct sum of directions.

Join distance

$$\cos d\big((\theta,\xi),(\theta',\eta)\big)=\cos\theta\cos\theta'\cos d_1(\xi)+\sin\theta\sin\theta'\cos d_2(\eta)$$

Notation

Written $Y_1 * Y_2$; spheres are iterated joins of points.

Printed pp. 184-192The Spherical Join
The spherical join of two spaces sews them together by spherical right triangles, parametrising a join point by an angle between zero and pi over two together with a point in each factor. The join distance is again given by a spherical cosine formula. It is the link-level analogue of an orthogonal direct sum.

Joins and CAT(1)

Closure

The join of two CAT(1) spaces is again CAT(1).

Compositional

Build curvature-controlled spaces from simple pieces.

Theorem

$$Y_1,Y_2\ \text{CAT}(1)\ \Longrightarrow\ Y_1 * Y_2\ \text{CAT}(1)$$

Example

$\mathbb{S}^{n}=\mathbb{S}^{0}*\cdots*\mathbb{S}^{0}$ certifies the round sphere.

Printed pp. 184-192Joins and CAT(1)
The spherical join of two CAT(1) spaces is again CAT(1). Combined with the cone theorem this lets us build curvature-controlled spaces compositionally: spheres are iterated joins of points, and links of cell complexes are joins, so the join theorem certifies non-positive curvature of large constructions.

Limits of Spaces

Ultralimits

Limits of CAT(k) spaces stay CAT(k) under the comparison.

Tangent cone as limit

$T_pX$ is the rescaling limit of $X$ about $p$.

Blow-up limit

$$T_pX=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\big(\lambda X,\ p\big)$$

Synthesis

Angles, cones, joins, and limits form one tangent toolkit.

Printed pp. 184-192Limits of Spaces
Ultralimits and Gromov-Hausdorff limits let sequences of CAT(k) spaces converge to a limit space that is again CAT(k). The tangent cone is itself such a limit, the rescaling limit of the space about a point. Limits, angles, cones, and joins thus form one connected toolkit for tangent geometry.
