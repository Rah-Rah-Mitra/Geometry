Boundary as a set

$\partial_\infty X$ is the set of asymptote classes of geodesic rays — a topological sphere only.

The enrichment plan

  • Define an angle between ideal points.
  • Verify it is a metric.
  • Pass to the induced length metric.
Ideal points

$$\xi=[\,c\,],\qquad \eta=[\,c'\,]$$

Classes of asymptotic rays $c,c':[0,\infty)\to X$.

Why bother

The metric structure makes $\partial_\infty X$ remember flats, splittings, and curvature.

The Angular Metric

Definition

$$\angle(\xi,\eta)=\sup_{p\in X}\ \angle_p(\xi,\eta)$$

Read it as

The largest Alexandrov angle the two ideal directions ever subtend at an interior vertex $p$.

Monotonicity

In CAT(0), $\angle_p(\xi,\eta)$ does not decrease as $p$ recedes; the supremum is the limiting value.

It is a metric

Symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality, and bounded by $\pi$ between any two ideal points.

Printed pp. 277-298The Angular Metric
Fix a basepoint and shoot the two rays toward distinct ideal points; their Alexandrov angle at that basepoint is one estimate of separation. The angular metric takes the supremum over all basepoints. In a CAT(0) space the angle does not decrease as you move the vertex outward, so this supremum is realized in the limit and is genuinely a metric.

The Angle Stabilizes at Infinity

Move the observer out

Slide $p$ along a ray and track the wedge subtended by fixed $\xi,\eta$.

Stable limit

$$\angle(\xi,\eta)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\angle_{c(t)}(\xi,\eta)$$

Printed pp. 277-298Angle Stabilizes at Infinity
Walk the observation point along a ray and watch the subtended angle between two fixed ideal points. By convexity of the metric the angle is monotone and converges. That stable limit is the Tits angle. The picture to hold is a shrinking or settling wedge whose opening reaches a definite asymptotic value, independent of how you traveled out.

The Tits Metric

Why a length metric

The angular metric may be disconnected or have gaps no single path realizes; we take its length metric.

Definition

$$\mathrm{Td}(\xi,\eta)=\inf_{\gamma}\ \mathrm{length}_{\angle}(\gamma)$$

Infimum over paths $\gamma$ in $\partial_\infty X$.

Tits boundary

$\partial_T X = (\partial_\infty X,\ \mathrm{Td})$ is the boundary with its Tits metric.

Local agreement

For close ideal points the Tits and angular distances coincide; they differ globally.

Printed pp. 277-298The Tits Metric
The angular metric can be disconnected or have short cuts that no path realizes, so we take its associated length metric. The Tits distance is the infimum of angular lengths of paths joining two ideal points, capped appropriately. This length metric is the Tits metric, and the boundary equipped with it is the Tits boundary of the space.

The Tits Boundary is CAT(1)

Theorem

If $X$ is a complete CAT(0) space, then $\partial_T X$ is a complete CAT(1) space.

Why scale one

Comparison triangles live in the unit sphere $S^2$; angles cap at $\pi$, the natural sphere scale.

Consequence

Geodesics in $\partial_T X$ of length $<\pi$ are unique; this rigidity propagates from inside $X$.

Curvature flow up

$$\kappa(X)\le 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \kappa(\partial_T X)\le 1$$

Printed pp. 277-298The Boundary is CAT(1)
Here is the central theorem. If X is a complete CAT(0) space, its Tits boundary is a complete CAT(1) space. Comparison triangles in the boundary are read in the unit sphere. This is why angles capped at pi matter: the boundary inherits curvature bounded above by one, the natural scale for a sphere of ideal directions.

Flats Become Round Spheres

Flat to subsphere

$$E^n\hookrightarrow X\ \Longrightarrow\ \partial_T E^n = S^{n-1}$$

A standard round sphere of diameter $\pi$.

Example

A Euclidean plane $E^2$ contributes a great circle of circumference $2\pi$ in $\partial_T X$.

Printed pp. 277-298Flats Become Round Spheres
Take a flat, an isometrically embedded Euclidean n-space. Its ideal directions form a round (n minus one)-sphere, and inside the Tits boundary that sphere appears with its standard metric of diameter pi. So a Euclidean plane contributes a great circle of circumference two pi. The Tits boundary literally photographs every flat as a round subsphere.

Visibility Spaces

Definition

$X$ is a visibility space if for all distinct $\xi,\eta\in\partial_\infty X$ there is a geodesic line $\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to X$ with ends $\xi,\eta$.

Boundary reading

$$\mathrm{Td}(\xi,\eta)=\infty\quad(>\pi)\ \text{ for }\xi\neq\eta$$

Signature

Visibility is the boundary-level fingerprint of genuinely negative curvature.

No flat strips

Rays cannot run parallel; divergence forces a connecting bi-infinite geodesic.

Printed pp. 277-298Visibility Spaces
A CAT(0) space is a visibility space when any two distinct ideal points are joined by a geodesic line running between them. Equivalently the Tits distance between any two distinct boundary points is infinite, more than pi. Visibility is the boundary-level signature of genuinely negative curvature: every pair of ends sees one another along a bi-infinite geodesic.

Hyperbolic Space is the Model

$\mathbb{H}^n$

Any two distinct points of the sphere at infinity are the two ends of a unique geodesic line.

Tits boundary

$$\partial_T \mathbb{H}^n:\quad \mathrm{Td}\equiv\infty\ \text{(discrete)}$$

Printed pp. 277-298Hyperbolic Space is Visibility
Real hyperbolic n-space is the model visibility space. Any two distinct points on its sphere at infinity are the two ends of a unique geodesic line. In the Tits metric the boundary is totally disconnected with all distances infinite, since no flat strip exists to interpolate angles. Strict negativity forbids the rays from running parallel.

Flats versus Visibility

Flat regime

  • Parallel rays exist.
  • $\mathrm{Td}=\pi$ across the flat.
  • Boundary is a round sphere, finite diameter.

Visibility regime

  • Rays diverge exponentially.
  • $\mathrm{Td}=\infty$ for distinct ends.
  • Boundary is discrete.
Printed pp. 277-298Flats versus Visibility
Contrast the two regimes side by side. In a flat, parallel rays give Tits distance pi and the boundary is a round sphere with finite diameter. In a visibility space, divergence is exponential, Tits distance is infinite, and the boundary carries the discrete metric. Mixed spaces have flats inside an otherwise visibility-like geometry, breaking pure visibility.

Detecting Rank and Splittings

Joins signal products

A spherical join $\partial_T X = A * B$ reflects a metric product splitting $X = X_1\times X_2$.

Rank via spheres

$$S^{k-1}\subset\partial_T X\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{a }k\text{-flat in }X$$

Rank rigidity

Controlling embedded Tits spheres constrains the Euclidean and irreducible factors of $X$.

The circle of ideas

Boundary geometry $\to$ flats $\to$ splittings $\to$ rigidity theorems.

Printed pp. 277-298Detecting Rank and Splittings
The Tits boundary is a rank detector. A spherical join in the Tits boundary signals a product splitting of the space; an embedded round sphere of dimension at least one signals a higher-rank flat. This circle of ideas feeds rank rigidity: controlling Tits-boundary spheres constrains how the space can decompose into Euclidean and irreducible factors.

Recap and Outlook

Two metrics

Angular metric $=\sup$ of interior angles; Tits metric $=$ its length metric.

Central theorem

$X$ CAT(0) $\Rightarrow \partial_T X$ CAT(1).

Two regimes

Flats $\to$ round spheres, diameter $\pi$. Visibility $\to$ infinite Tits distances.

Where it leads

Splittings, rank detection, and the rank-rigidity program.

Printed pp. 277-298Recap and Outlook
To recap pages 277 through 298: the angular metric is the supremum of interior angles, the Tits metric is its length metric, and the Tits boundary of a CAT(0) space is CAT(1). Flats appear as round spheres of diameter pi, visibility spaces have infinite Tits distances, and these invariants drive splitting and rank-rigidity arguments.
