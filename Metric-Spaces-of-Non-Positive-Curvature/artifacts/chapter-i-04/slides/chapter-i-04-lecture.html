From a Norm to a Metric

Norm axioms

Positivity, homogeneity, and the triangle inequality on a vector space $V$.

Induced distance

Distance is the norm of the difference of two vectors.

Inherited structure

Translation invariance and scaling come for free.

Metric from a norm

$$d(x,y) = \lVert x - y \rVert$$

Why it is a metric

$\lVert x-z\rVert \le \lVert x-y\rVert + \lVert y-z\rVert$

Key reduction

All local geometry is recorded once by the unit ball $B = \{v : \lVert v\rVert \le 1\}$.

Printed pp. 47-55From a Norm to a Metric
Recall the norm axioms and define the induced distance as the norm of the difference. Stress that translation invariance and homogeneity are inherited directly, so the entire local picture is captured once by the unit ball. The triangle inequality for the metric is just the triangle inequality for the norm.

Straight Segments Are Always Geodesics

Affine path

$$\gamma(t) = (1-t)\,x + t\,y,\quad t\in[0,1]$$

Length equals distance

Homogeneity makes each subsegment add up exactly to $\lVert x-y\rVert$.

Conclusion

In every normed space the straight segment realises the distance.

What is open

Existence is settled; uniqueness is the subtle question.

Printed pp. 47-55Straight Segments Are Always Geodesics
Prove the easy half: the affine segment parametrised by arc length has length equal to the distance between its endpoints, because homogeneity makes each subsegment additive. So in any normed space a straight line realises the distance and is a geodesic. Uniqueness is the part that fails.

The Unit Ball Encodes the Geometry

Sphere = price list

The unit sphere records the cost of moving in each direction.

Convex $\Leftrightarrow$ triangle ineq.

Convexity of $B$ is equivalent to subadditivity of the norm.

Flat faces = equality

A line segment on the boundary is an equality case of the triangle inequality.

Printed pp. 47-55The Unit Ball Encodes the Geometry
Make the unit ball the central character. Its boundary, the unit sphere, records exactly which directions cost what. Convexity of the ball is equivalent to the triangle inequality; flat faces on the boundary are equality cases. Three classic balls organise the rest of the lecture: diamond, circle, square.

Strict Convexity

Definition

$\lVert u\rVert=\lVert v\rVert=1,\ u\neq v \ \Rightarrow\ \big\lVert \tfrac{u+v}{2}\big\rVert < 1$

Boundary view

The open chord between distinct boundary points lies strictly inside $B$.

Equality view

$\lVert u+v\rVert=\lVert u\rVert+\lVert v\rVert$ forces $u,v$ to be non-negative multiples.

Printed pp. 47-55Strict Convexity
Define strict convexity precisely: the open segment between two distinct boundary points stays strictly inside the ball, equivalently equality in the triangle inequality forces the vectors to be non-negative multiples of one another. This is the property that pins down geodesics, so spend time making the boundary picture vivid.

The Uniqueness Theorem

Theorem

Geodesics between any two points of a normed space are unique if and only if the norm is strictly convex.

Strict $\Rightarrow$ unique

Equality in the triangle inequality holds only along the straight segment.

Flat $\Rightarrow$ many

A flat face yields a continuum of distinct distance-realising paths.

Printed pp. 47-55Uniqueness Theorem
State the central theorem: geodesics between any two points are unique if and only if the norm is strictly convex. Sketch both directions. Strict convexity forces equality in the triangle inequality only on the straight segment; a flat face produces a continuum of alternative distance-realising paths.

The $\ell^p$ Family of Norms

$p$-norm on the plane

$$\lVert x\rVert_p = \big(|x_1|^p + |x_2|^p\big)^{1/p}$$

$1 < p < \infty$

Strictly convex ball $\Rightarrow$ geodesics are unique.

$p = 1$ and $p = \infty$

Polygonal balls with flat faces $\Rightarrow$ uniqueness fails.

Printed pp. 47-55The l-p Family of Norms
Introduce the p-norms on the plane and compute the three landmark balls. For p strictly between one and infinity the ball is strictly convex, so geodesics are unique. The extreme cases p equal one and p infinite have polygonal balls with flat faces, and there uniqueness collapses.

Non-Unique Geodesics: the $\ell^\infty$ Plane

Sup distance

$$d_\infty(x,y) = \max_i |x_i - y_i|$$

Staircase paths

Any monotone path inside the bounding box has length equal to $d_\infty$.

Consequence

A whole family of distinct geodesics shares the same endpoints and length.

The $\ell^1$ Mirror Image

Taxicab distance

$$d_1(x,y) = |x_1-y_1| + |x_2-y_2|$$

Same pathology, rotated

The diamond ball has flat faces too, so monotone paths all realise $d_1$.

Why they match

In the plane $\ell^1$ and $\ell^\infty$ are linearly isometric — a $45^\circ$ turn.

The Euclidean Contrast

Euclidean distance

$$d_2(x,y) = \Big(\sum_i (x_i-y_i)^2\Big)^{1/2}$$

Strictly convex

The round ball forces equality in the triangle inequality only on collinear vectors.

One geodesic

The straight segment is the unique distance-realising path.

Printed pp. 47-55Euclidean Contrast
Return to the circle. The Euclidean ball is strictly convex, equality in the triangle inequality forces collinearity, and the straight segment is the only geodesic. This is the calm case that the curvature theory will demand, and it sets up why an inner product is special among norms.

Why CAT(0) Forces an Inner Product

Rigidity

A normed space is $\mathrm{CAT}(0)$ iff its norm comes from an inner product.

Comparison axiom

Triangles must be no fatter than Euclidean comparison triangles.

Flat faces break it

$\ell^1,\ \ell^\infty$ fail the comparison badly — not even uniquely geodesic.

Parallelogram law

$$2\lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert v\rVert^2 = \lVert u+v\rVert^2 + \lVert u-v\rVert^2$$

Printed pp. 47-55Why CAT(0) Forces an Inner Product
Connect to the larger arc of the book. The CAT(0) comparison axiom is rigid: a normed space is CAT(0) precisely when its norm comes from an inner product. Flat-faced balls fail the comparison badly, so non-positive curvature singles out Hilbert geometry among all norms.

Recap and Outlook

Norm gives a metric

And the metric space is always geodesic.

Strict convexity $\Leftrightarrow$ uniqueness

The unit ball's shape is the single deciding invariant.

$\ell^1,\ \ell^\infty$ collapse

Flat faces give staircases — infinitely many geodesics.

Only Hilbert is CAT(0)

Non-positive curvature selects the inner-product case.

Printed pp. 47-55Recap and Outlook
Summarise the dividing line: norm gives metric, segments are always geodesics, strict convexity is equivalent to uniqueness, and only inner-product norms are CAT(0). Preview that the next chapters build curvature comparison on exactly the strict, Euclidean side of this dichotomy.
