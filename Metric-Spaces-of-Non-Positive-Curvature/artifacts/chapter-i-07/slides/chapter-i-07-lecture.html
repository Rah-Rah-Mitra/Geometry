The Model Space $M_\kappa$

One curvature

Fix $\kappa$; every cell is cut from $M_\kappa$.

Three regimes

$\kappa>0:\ \mathbb{S}^n_\kappa,\quad \kappa=0:\ \mathbb{E}^n,\quad \kappa<0:\ \mathbb{H}^n_\kappa$

Rescaling

$M_\kappa$ has constant curvature $\kappa$; sphere and hyperbolic space are rescaled by $1/\sqrt{|\kappa|}$.

Where curvature lives

Each cell is locally as flat as $M_\kappa$ allows; curvature of the complex comes from the combinatorics of gluing.

Printed pp. 97-122The Model Space $M_\kappa$
Everything is cut from one model. For positive kappa we use a rescaled sphere, for zero kappa Euclidean space, for negative kappa rescaled hyperbolic space. Fixing kappa once means all cells share a curvature, so curvature lives only in how the cells are combinatorially assembled, not inside any single cell.

Convex Polyhedral Cells

Definition

$C=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m} H_i^{-}\subset M_\kappa$

A compact convex polytope: a finite intersection of half-spaces.

Faces

Each face of $C$ is again a convex cell of lower dimension.

Printed pp. 97-122Convex Polyhedral Cells
A cell is a compact convex polytope inside the model, the intersection of finitely many half-spaces. Its faces are again convex cells of lower dimension. These cells are the rigid Lego bricks of the theory: every metric statement about the complex is ultimately reduced to geometry inside one such convex piece.

Gluing Along Isometric Faces

Data

A disjoint family $(C_\lambda)$ of cells plus face isometries $g$.

Quotient

$K=\Big(\bigsqcup_\lambda C_\lambda\Big)\big/\!\sim$

Discipline

Each gluing map is an isometry between faces, so the model geometry matches across every seam.

Printed pp. 97-122Gluing Along Isometric Faces
We take a disjoint family of cells and a collection of isometries identifying faces in pairs. The quotient under the generated equivalence relation is the complex. The crucial discipline is that gluing maps are isometries of faces, so the inherited Euclidean, spherical, or hyperbolic geometry agrees across every seam.

Finitely Many Shapes

Shapes

$\mathrm{Shapes}(K)=\{C_\lambda\}/\,\mathrm{isometry}$

Hypothesis

$\mathrm{Shapes}(K)$ is finite even when infinitely many cells are glued.

Why it matters

A single uniformity bound on cells controls the geometry near every point.

Printed pp. 97-122Finitely Many Shapes
Shapes are isometry classes of cells. The finiteness hypothesis says only finitely many isometry types appear, even if infinitely many cells are glued. This single uniformity assumption controls the geometry near every point and is exactly what later powers the completeness and geodesic conclusions of Bridson's theorem.

Strings and the Intrinsic Pseudometric

String

$\Sigma=(x_0,\dots,x_n),\quad \bar x_i\sim x_i$ on shared faces

Length and distance

$\ell(\Sigma)=\sum_{i} d_{C_i}(x_{i-1},x_i),\quad d(p,q)=\inf_\Sigma \ell(\Sigma)$

Reading it

A string hops through a chain of cells; within each cell it uses the model distance $d_{C_i}$.

Intrinsic

The metric is the infimum over all admissible strings respecting the gluing.

Printed pp. 97-122Strings and the Intrinsic Pseudometric
Distance is defined by chains of points called strings that hop through a sequence of cells, summing the within-cell distances. The pseudometric is the infimum over all admissible strings joining two points. This is the taut-string idea: the metric measures the shortest path that respects how the cells are stitched together.

From Pseudometric to Metric

The risk

A bare infimum could give $d(p,q)=0$ for distinct $p\neq q$.

Non-degeneracy

$\text{finite shapes}\ \Rightarrow\ d(p,q)>0$ for $p\neq q$

Uniform lower bound

Finiteness of shapes forces a positive injectivity-style gap that separates points.

Outcome

$d$ is a genuine metric; we can now ask whether it is realised by paths.

Printed pp. 97-122From Pseudometric to Metric
A priori the infimum could vanish between distinct points, so we only have a pseudometric. Under finitely many shapes this degeneracy cannot happen: distinct points stay a positive distance apart. The quotient is then a genuine metric space and we may ask whether the metric is realised by paths.

Bridson's Theorem

Theorem (Bridson)

If an $M_\kappa$-polyhedral complex $K$ has only finitely many isometry types of cells, then $(K,d)$ is a complete geodesic metric space.

Complete

Every Cauchy sequence converges in $K$.

Geodesic

Any $p,q$ are joined by a path of length exactly $d(p,q)$.

Engine

Finitely many shapes is the hypothesis that makes both conclusions hold.

Printed pp. 97-122Bridson's Theorem
Bridson's theorem is the chapter's engine: an M_k-polyhedral complex with only finitely many isometry types of cells is a complete geodesic metric space. Stress both payoffs. Completeness means Cauchy sequences converge, and geodesic means any two points are joined by a shortest path realising their distance.

The Link of a Vertex

Definition

$\mathrm{Lk}(v,K)=\{\text{unit directions at } v\}$

Each cell at $v$ contributes a spherical cell of its incident directions.

It is an $M_1$ complex

The link is itself a spherical polyhedral complex of dimension one lower.

Printed pp. 97-122The Link of a Vertex
Zoom into a vertex and record the unit directions leaving it. Each incident cell contributes a spherical cell of directions, and these glue into the link, a smaller M_1 spherical complex. The link is the infinitesimal model of the complex at that vertex and packages all of its local geometry.

Links Control Local Geometry

Cone structure

$B_\varepsilon(v)\ \cong\ C_\kappa\big(\mathrm{Lk}(v,K)\big)$

A neighbourhood of $v$ is a metric cone over its link.

Loop threshold

Short loops in the link should have length at least $2\pi$.

Local test

Curvature near $v$ is decided by lengths of loops in $\mathrm{Lk}(v,K)$ — a finite combinatorial check.

Printed pp. 97-122Links Control Local Geometry
The link is where curvature is tested. A neighbourhood of the vertex is a metric cone over its link, so local geodesic and curvature behaviour is governed by lengths of loops in the link. The threshold to remember is that short loops must have length at least two pi for the expected local behaviour.
