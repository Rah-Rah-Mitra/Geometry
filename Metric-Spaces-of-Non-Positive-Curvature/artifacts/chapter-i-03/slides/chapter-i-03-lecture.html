The Induced Length Metric

Infimum over paths

Take the cheapest joining curve.

Dominates $d$

The induced metric is never smaller.

May be infinite

If no rectifiable path joins the points.

Induced length metric

$$d_\ell(x,y)=\inf\{\,\ell(c): c \text{ joins } x \text{ to } y\,\}$$

$d(x,y)\le d_\ell(x,y)$

Here is the central construction: the length metric assigns to two points the infimum of lengths of paths joining them. Emphasize that this infimum may be larger than the ambient distance, may be infinite if no rectifiable path exists, and always dominates the original metric.

What Makes a Length Space

Self-consistent

The metric equals its own length metric.

Realized by paths

Distance is approached by joining curves.

Not automatic

The chord metric on a sphere fails this.

Defining condition

$$d(x,y)=d_\ell(x,y)\quad\text{for all }x,y$$

Fix-up

Replacing $d$ by $d_\ell$ always yields a length metric.

Printed pp. 32-46Length Spaces
A length space is one where the metric already equals its own induced length metric, so distance is realized arbitrarily well by joining paths. Contrast this with the chord metric on the sphere, which is not a length metric until we replace it by arc length.

In a length space distance is approached by paths but geodesics need not exist. Introduce approximate mid-points: points almost equidistant whose distances to the endpoints nearly sum to the whole. Completeness will later upgrade these approximate mid-points into genuine mid-points and then into geodesics.

Completeness and Local Compactness

Complete

Every Cauchy sequence converges in $X$.

Locally compact

Each point has a compact neighborhood.

Neither alone

Standing setup

$X$ is a length space satisfying both.

State the two standing hypotheses precisely. Complete means Cauchy sequences converge; locally compact means every point has a compact neighborhood. Neither alone suffices, but together, in a length space, they are exactly the conditions Hopf-Rinow needs to deliver properness and geodesics.

Proper Metric Spaces

Definition

Closed balls are compact.

Heine-Borel

Bounded sequences have convergent subsequences.

Strength

Proper implies complete, not conversely.

Proper

$$\overline{B}(x,r)\ \text{compact for all } x,\ r\ge 0$$

Why it matters

It extracts limit geodesics from approximating paths.

Printed pp. 32-46Proper Metric Spaces
Define proper: every closed bounded set, equivalently every closed ball, is compact. Properness is strictly stronger than completeness. The Heine-Borel property gives us convergent subsequences for free, which is the engine that extracts limit geodesics from sequences of approximating paths.

The Hopf-Rinow Theorem

Theorem

Let $X$ be a length space that is complete and locally compact. Then $X$ is proper and geodesic.

Proper

Closed balls $\overline{B}(x,r)$ are compact.

Geodesic

Any two points are joined by a minimizing geodesic.

Geodesic between $x,y$

$$\exists\,\gamma:[0,d(x,y)]\to X,\ \ \ell(\gamma)=d(x,y)$$

Printed pp. 32-46The Hopf-Rinow Theorem
State the theorem cleanly. For a length space that is complete and locally compact: it is proper, hence closed balls are compact, and it is a geodesic space, so any two points are joined by a length-minimizing geodesic. This is the chapter's keystone result.

Proof Architecture

Step 1

Compact balls have radii forming a clopen set; so all radii work.

Step 2

Properness follows; bounded closed sets are compact.

Step 3

Approximate mid-points converge to a true mid-point; iterate to a geodesic.

Printed pp. 32-46Proof Architecture
Sketch the logic without full detail. Local compactness propagates outward so that the radius of compact balls is open and closed, hence all of the space; that gives properness. Properness plus completeness lets approximate mid-points converge to mid-points, and iterating builds the geodesic.

Why Both Hypotheses Bite

Drop completeness

The open disc is locally compact but incomplete; a full diameter has no realizing geodesic.

Failure

$\overline{B}\ \text{not compact at the missing edge}$

Drop local compactness

An infinite-dimensional sphere is complete but not locally compact; closed balls are not compact.

Failure

$\overline{B}(x,r)\ \text{has no convergent subsequence}$

Printed pp. 32-46Why the Hypotheses Bite
Show the necessity with counterexamples. The open disc is locally compact but incomplete, so a diameter has no geodesic. An infinite-dimensional sphere is complete but not locally compact, and closed balls fail to be compact. Both hypotheses are genuinely required.
