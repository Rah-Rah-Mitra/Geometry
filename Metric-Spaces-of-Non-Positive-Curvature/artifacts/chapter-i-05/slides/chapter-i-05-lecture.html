The Product Metric

Pythagorean combination

Distances in factors add in squares, like coordinate displacements.

Geodesics factor

A product geodesic is a pair of factor geodesics run at proportional speed.

Inherited

Completeness, being geodesic, and uniqueness pass factor by factor.

l-two product metric

$$d\big((x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2)\big)=\sqrt{\,d_X(x_1,x_2)^2+d_Y(y_1,y_2)^2\,}$$

Printed pp. 56-80The Product Metric
The l-two product metric combines two spaces by adding squared distances under a square root, exactly as Pythagoras combines coordinate displacements. Stress that geodesics in the product are pairs of geodesics traced at proportional speed, and that completeness, geodesicity, and uniqueness of geodesics all transfer factor by factor to the product.

Curvature of Products

Theorem

A product of two $\mathrm{CAT}(0)$ spaces is again $\mathrm{CAT}(0)$.

Why it works

A triangle projects to triangles in each factor; squared distances add.

Caution

For positive bounds the clean additivity fails, so joins take over.

Comparison adds factorwise

$$\kappa(X{\times}Y)\le 0 \iff \kappa(X)\le 0 \;\text{and}\; \kappa(Y)\le 0$$

Printed pp. 56-80Curvature of Products
Products preserve curvature bounds in the non-positive regime: a product of two CAT(0) spaces is CAT(0). Emphasise that the comparison works because a geodesic triangle in the product projects to triangles in each factor, and the squared distances add. Warn that for positive bounds the clean additivity fails, which is why joins replace products there.

The Euclidean Cone

Rays from an apex

Attach a half-line $[0,\infty)$ to each point of the base $X$.

Base reads as angle

The angle between two rays is $d_X$ capped at $\pi$.

Law of cosines

Cone distance is computed by Euclidean trigonometry on that angle.

Cone distance $C_0 X$

$$d\big((r,x),(s,y)\big)^2=r^2+s^2-2rs\cos\theta,\quad \theta=\min\{d_X(x,y),\pi\}$$

Printed pp. 56-80The Euclidean Cone
The Euclidean cone glues a copy of a half-line to every point of the base, then measures distance using the law of cosines with the base distance read as an angle. Walk through the formula carefully: the angle between two rays is the base distance capped at pi, and the cone is built so apex neighbourhoods record that angle.

Cone Over a Circle of Circumference $L$

The dial

The apex angle of the cone encodes the circle's circumference $L$.

Flat iff $L=2\pi$

The cone unrolls to the Euclidean plane exactly at this value.

Threshold

$L<2\pi$: positively curved apex, $\mathrm{CAT}(0)$ fails. $L>2\pi$: negative concentration.

Flatness condition

$$C_0 S^1_L \cong \mathbb{E}^2 \iff L = 2\pi$$

Printed pp. 56-80Cone Over a Circle
This is the signature picture. Coning a circle of circumference L yields a genuine cone whose sharpness encodes L. When L equals two pi the cone unrolls flat to the Euclidean plane; when L is below two pi the apex is positively curved and CAT(0) fails; above two pi the apex carries negative curvature concentration. Make students feel the threshold.

The $\kappa$-Cone

Rays in a model

Build joining segments inside the model space $M_\kappa^2$ of curvature $\kappa$.

Model cosine rule

Distance uses the law of cosines for curvature $\kappa$ geometry.

Unifies

$\kappa=0$ is the Euclidean cone; $\kappa>0$ closes into a suspension.

$\kappa$-cone metric (case $\kappa=1$)

$$\cos d\big((r,x),(s,y)\big)=\cos r\cos s+\sin r\sin s\cos\theta$$

Printed pp. 56-80The k-Cone
The k-cone generalises the Euclidean cone by building rays inside the model space of curvature k rather than the flat plane. The distance formula uses the law of cosines for that model geometry. For k positive the cone closes up into a sphere-like suspension; this single definition unifies cones, suspensions, and the spherical pieces used later.

The Spherical Join

Quarter-circle bridges

Join $X$ and $Y$ by geodesics of length $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ from each point to each point.

Spheres add

$S^m \ast S^n \cong S^{m+n+1}$: joining two spheres raises dimension.

It is the link

The join is the unit-tangent picture the $\kappa$-cone produces at its apex.

Spherical join metric

$$\cos d=\cos t_1\cos t_2\cos d_X+\sin t_1\sin t_2\cos d_Y$$

Printed pp. 56-80The Spherical Join
The spherical join interpolates between two spaces by a quarter-circle of joining geodesics, with the join metric built from the spherical law of cosines. Emphasise that the join of spheres is again a sphere of one higher dimension, and that the join is exactly the link, or unit tangent picture, that the k-cone produces at its apex.

Cones, Joins, and Curvature

Cone theorem

$C_0 X$ is $\mathrm{CAT}(0)$ exactly when $X$ is $\mathrm{CAT}(1)$.

Join theorem

$X \ast Y$ is $\mathrm{CAT}(1)$ exactly when both $X$ and $Y$ are.

Why it matters

A bound on a small link becomes a bound on the big cone.

Link to global passage

$$X \in \mathrm{CAT}(1)\;\Longrightarrow\; C_0 X \in \mathrm{CAT}(0)$$

Printed pp. 56-80Cones, Joins, and Curvature
State the central package: the Euclidean cone over X is CAT(0) precisely when X is CAT(1), and the spherical join of two spaces is CAT(1) precisely when both factors are. These two theorems convert a curvature bound on a small link into a curvature bound on the big cone, and they preview Berestovskii and the link condition.

Quotient Pseudometrics

Infimum over chains

Distance is the cheapest chain of admissible jumps and in-piece paths.

Only a pseudometric

Distinct classes can sit at distance zero before collapsing.

Make it a metric

Quotient by the zero-distance relation to recover a genuine metric.

Quotient pseudometric

$$\bar d(x,y)=\inf\sum_{i=1}^{n} d\big(x_i,y_i\big),\quad y_i\sim x_{i+1}$$

Printed pp. 56-80Quotient Pseudometrics
Gluing is run through the quotient pseudometric: the distance between two classes is the infimum over chains of admissible jumps and in-piece paths. Explain that the result is only a pseudometric in general, that collapsing the zero-distance relation makes it a genuine metric, and that this infimum-over-chains formula is the engine behind every gluing in the book.

Gluing Along Subspaces

Glue along a copy

Identify isometric copies of a common subspace $A$ inside $X_1$ and $X_2$.

Pieces embed

The quotient metric restricts isometrically to each piece and to the seam.

Reshetnyak gluing

Geodesics stay in one piece or cross the seam exactly once.

Glued space

$$X_1 \sqcup_A X_2 = (X_1 \sqcup X_2)\big/\!\sim,\quad A\hookrightarrow X_i \text{ isometric}$$

Printed pp. 56-80Gluing Along Subspaces
When two spaces are glued along an isometric copy of a common subspace, the quotient metric restricts isometrically to each piece and to the seam. Reissner's gluing lemma guarantees geodesics either stay in one piece or cross the seam once. This is how trees, complexes, and developments of polygons are assembled metrically.

Warped and Twisted Products

Rescaled fibre

A positive function $f$ on the base scales the second factor's metric.

Hyperbolic space

$\mathbb{H}^n$ appears as a warped product of a line and a horosphere.

Survival depends on $f$

Curvature can be redistributed; bounds now hinge on the warping.

Warped product metric

$$ds^2 = ds_B^2 + f(b)^2\, ds_F^2$$

Printed pp. 56-80Warped and Twisted Products
Warped products rescale the second factor by a positive function on the first, so the metric reads as base distance plus warped fibre distance. These twisted products produce hyperbolic space as a warped product of a line and a horosphere, and they let curvature be redistributed; flag that survival of bounds now depends on the warping function.
