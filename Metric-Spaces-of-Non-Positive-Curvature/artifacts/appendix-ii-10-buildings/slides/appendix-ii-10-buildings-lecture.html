Apartments are Coxeter Complexes

Spherical type

A finite reflection group $W$ tiles the sphere $\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$ into chambers.

Euclidean type

An affine reflection group $W_{\mathrm{aff}}$ tiles $\mathbb{E}^{n}$ into chambers.

Walls

The reflection hyperplanes; $W$ acts simply transitively on chambers.

Coxeter group

$$W = \langle s_i \mid (s_i s_j)^{m_{ij}} = 1\rangle$$

Printed pp. 342-346Apartments are Coxeter Complexes
Before buildings we need apartments, and an apartment is a Coxeter complex. A finite reflection group tiles a sphere; an affine reflection group tiles Euclidean space. Stress that the walls are the reflection hyperplanes and the chambers are the connected pieces of their complement, all permuted simply transitively by the group.

Chambers, Panels, and Galleries

Chamber

A top-dimensional simplex of the complex.

Panel

A codimension-one face shared by adjacent chambers.

Gallery

A chamber path crossing one wall per step.

Gallery distance

$d(C,C') = \min\{\text{walls a gallery crosses}\}$

Printed pp. 342-346Chambers, Panels, and Galleries
Here we fix the combinatorial vocabulary that runs through the appendix. Chambers are top-dimensional simplices, panels are their codimension-one faces, walls separate chambers, and a gallery is a chamber-to-chamber path crossing one wall at a time. Gallery distance counts crossings and will measure metric distance later.

The Building Axioms

Definition

A building is a simplicial complex $\Delta$ with a family $\mathcal{A}$ of apartments, each isomorphic to a fixed Coxeter complex.

Axiom 1

Any two simplices lie in a common apartment.

Axiom 2

If two apartments share simplices, an isomorphism between them fixes the overlap.

Printed pp. 342-346The Building Axioms
Now the definition. A building is a simplicial complex with a system of apartments, each isomorphic to a fixed Coxeter complex, such that any two simplices lie in a common apartment and any two apartments are isomorphic fixing their intersection. Emphasise that these two axioms force the strong homogeneity buildings are famous for.

Retractions onto an Apartment

Setup

Fix an apartment $A$ and a base chamber $C \subset A$.

The fold

$\rho_{A,C}\colon \Delta \to A$ folds the building onto $A$.

Why it matters

It preserves gallery distance from $C$, exporting computations to one Coxeter complex.

Distance preserving

$$d\big(C,\,D\big) = d\big(C,\,\rho_{A,C}(D)\big)$$

Printed pp. 342-346Retractions onto an Apartment
Retractions are the technical engine. Fixing an apartment and a chamber, one folds the whole building onto that apartment, preserving gallery distance from the base chamber. Explain that retractions let us transport metric and combinatorial computations from the complicated building into a single well-understood Coxeter complex.

Spherical Buildings are CAT(1)

Apartment metric

A round sphere $\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$ of curvature $+1$, tiled by a finite $W$.

Gluing

Apartments are joined isometrically along shared chambers.

Result

$\Delta$ spherical $\;\Rightarrow\; \Delta$ is $\mathrm{CAT}(1)$

Printed pp. 342-346Spherical Buildings are CAT(1)
For a spherical building the Coxeter complex is a sphere tiled by a finite reflection group, so each apartment carries the round metric of curvature one. The gluing of apartments along chambers is an isometric gluing, and the appendix records that the resulting complex is CAT(1), the spherical-curvature comparison space.

Euclidean Buildings are CAT(0)

Apartment metric

Flat $\mathbb{E}^{n}$ tiled by an affine group; the $A_2$ triangular tiling is the model.

Gluing

Isometric along chambers, giving a complete, contractible complex.

Result

$\Delta$ Euclidean $\;\Rightarrow\; \Delta$ is $\mathrm{CAT}(0)$

Printed pp. 342-346Euclidean Buildings are CAT(0)
Euclidean or affine buildings replace the sphere by Euclidean space tiled by an affine reflection group, the A2 triangular tiling being the prototype. Each apartment is flat, the gluing is isometric, and the building is CAT(0): complete, contractible, with unique geodesics. This is the non-positive-curvature payoff of the appendix.

Links and the Curvature Test

Link condition

A Euclidean building is $\mathrm{CAT}(0)$ iff every vertex link is $\mathrm{CAT}(1)$.

The interlock

Vertex links of a Euclidean building are themselves spherical buildings.

Consequence

The spherical theory certifies curvature one dimension up.

Printed pp. 342-346Links and the Curvature Test
Walk through why the curvature bound holds. By the link condition a Euclidean building is CAT(0) exactly when every vertex link is CAT(1), and those links are themselves spherical buildings. So the two halves of the appendix interlock: spherical buildings certify the curvature of Euclidean buildings one dimension up.

Buildings of Algebraic Groups

$\mathrm{SL}_n(k)$, field

Flags of subspaces give a spherical building; the Weyl group is $S_n$.

$\mathrm{SL}_n(K)$, local field

Lattice classes give a Euclidean building of affine type $\tilde{A}_{n-1}$.

Dictionary

Weyl group of the group $=$ Coxeter group tiling each apartment.

Flag building

$0 \subset V_1 \subset V_2 \subset \cdots \subset V_{n-1} \subset k^{n}$

Printed pp. 342-346Buildings of Algebraic Groups
The motivating examples come from algebra. SL(n) over a field gives a spherical building from the flags of subspaces; over a local field SL(n) gives a Euclidean building from lattice classes. Stress that the Weyl group of the algebraic group is exactly the Coxeter group tiling each apartment, tying combinatorics to representation theory.

Tits Boundary and Higher Rank

Boundary at infinity

The Tits boundary of a higher-rank symmetric space is a spherical building.

Euclidean too

$\partial_T$ of a Euclidean building is again a spherical building of directions.

Rigidity

Symmetry of the boundary building forces rigidity inside.

Printed pp. 342-346Tits Boundary and Higher Rank
Spherical buildings reappear at infinity. The Tits boundary of a higher-rank symmetric space or of a Euclidean building is a spherical building, encoding asymptotic directions. Rigidity theorems read symmetry of the boundary building back into the interior, which is why buildings sit at the center of higher-rank geometry.
