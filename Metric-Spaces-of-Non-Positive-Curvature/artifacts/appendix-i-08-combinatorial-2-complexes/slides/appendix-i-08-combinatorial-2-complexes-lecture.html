What is a Combinatorial 2-Complex?

Three strata

Vertices, oriented edges, and polygonal 2-cells.

Combinatorial attaching

Each 2-cell is glued along an edge path: a word in the edges.

Why discrete

Gluings become bookkeeping on words, ready for both algebra and metric.

Attaching map

$\partial e^2 \xrightarrow{\;\phi\;} X^{(1)},\qquad \phi(\partial e^2)=w$

Boundary word

Traversing $\partial e^2$ spells a word $w$ in the 1-skeleton $X^{(1)}$.

Printed pp. 153-156What is a combinatorial 2-complex
A combinatorial 2-complex is built from vertices, oriented edges, and polygonal 2-cells attached along edge paths by combinatorial maps. We insist attaching maps send edges to edges so every gluing is bookkeeping on words. This discrete framing lets the same object be inspected as a space, as a group, and later as a metric object.

Group Presentations

Generators

A set $A$ of letters; words are products of $a$ and $a^{-1}$.

Relators

A set $R$ of words declared equal to the identity.

Presented group

$G=\langle A\mid R\rangle = F(A)\,/\,\langle\!\langle R\rangle\!\rangle$

Example

$\langle a,b\mid aba^{-1}b^{-1}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^2$: one relator, the torus.

Printed pp. 153-156Group presentations
A presentation packages a group as generators modulo relators. Each generator names an edge, each relator names a word that must equal the identity. Remind students that many groups share a presentation and that the relators, read as boundary words, are exactly the data we will glue two-cells along in the next slide.

The Presentation 2-Complex $K_{\langle A\mid R\rangle}$

One vertex

A single 0-cell $\ast$ is the basepoint.

One loop per generator

Each $a\in A$ becomes an oriented edge-loop at $\ast$.

One 2-cell per relator

Each $r\in R$ is a polygon glued along the boundary word $r$.

Printed pp. 153-156The presentation 2-complex
The presentation complex has a single vertex, one oriented loop per generator, and one polygonal 2-cell per relator glued along the boundary word spelled by that relator. Stress that traversing the cell boundary reads the relator letter by letter, so the geometry literally encodes the algebra of the presentation.

$\pi_1$ Equals the Presented Group

Theorem

$\pi_1\!\big(K_{\langle A\mid R\rangle},\ast\big)\;\cong\;\langle A\mid R\rangle$

Proof roadmap

Van Kampen: the 1-skeleton gives free group $F(A)$; each 2-cell quotients by its boundary $r$.

Skeleton

$X^{(1)}=\bigvee_A S^1$, so $\pi_1=F(A)$.

Cells kill relators

Attaching $r$ sets $[r]=1$ in $\pi_1$.

Consequence

Topology of $K$ $\Leftrightarrow$ algebra of $G$.

Printed pp. 153-156Fundamental group theorem
By van Kampen's theorem the fundamental group of the presentation complex is exactly the presented group. The wedge of generator loops gives a free group; each attached two-cell kills its boundary word. Emphasise that this is the bridge making topology of the complex equivalent to algebra of the group.

The Cayley 2-Complex

Universal cover

$\widetilde{K}$ is simply connected; it covers $K_{\langle A\mid R\rangle}$.

1-skeleton

The Cayley graph: vertices are elements of $G$, edges are generators.

Free action

$G$ acts freely, cocompactly by deck transformations.

Printed pp. 153-156The Cayley 2-complex
The universal cover of the presentation complex is the Cayley 2-complex. Its one-skeleton is the Cayley graph, vertices are group elements, and lifted two-cells fill every loop spelling a relator. It is simply connected, and the group acts freely and cocompactly by deck transformations permuting the cells.

Words as Cellular Paths

Word = path

A word $w$ traces an edge path from $\ast$ in the 1-skeleton.

Reduction

Delete backtracks $aa^{-1}$; this is free reduction.

Word problem

$w=_G 1 \iff \tilde{w}\text{ lifts to a loop in }\widetilde{K}$

Reframing

Does the closed path bound? A 2-dimensional question, not just 1-dimensional.

Printed pp. 153-156Words and cellular paths
A word in the generators is an edge path from the basepoint; reduce by removing backtracks. The word represents the identity exactly when its path lifts to a loop in the Cayley complex. This reframes the word problem as a question about whether a closed path bounds inside the two-dimensional complex.

Van Kampen Diagrams

Definition

A planar, simply connected complex $D$ mapped combinatorially into $K$.

Tiling

Each 2-cell of $D$ maps onto a relator polygon.

Boundary

$\partial D$ spells the word $w$ in question.

Van Kampen's Lemma

$w=_G 1 \iff \exists\ \text{van Kampen diagram with }\partial D = w$

Certificate

The diagram is a finite, checkable witness that $w$ is trivial.

Printed pp. 153-156Van Kampen diagrams
A van Kampen diagram is a planar, simply connected complex mapped combinatorially into the presentation complex, tiled by relator polygons, whose boundary spells the given word. Such a diagram exists precisely when the word equals the identity, so the diagram is a finite combinatorial certificate of that fact.

Reading a Van Kampen Diagram

Interior cells

Each is a copy of a relator $r$.

Interior edges

Cancel in pairs; only $\partial D$ survives.

Area

$\mathrm{Area}(w)=\#\{\text{2-cells in }D\}$

Printed pp. 153-156Reading a diagram
Walk the example diagram: interior two-cells are relator copies, interior edges cancel in pairs, and the outer boundary reads the trivial word after free reduction. Point out that the number of two-cells used measures area, the combinatorial cost of proving the word trivial, foreshadowing isoperimetric functions.

Giving Cells an $M_k$-Metric

Polygonal model

Realise each 2-cell as a geodesic polygon in $M_k^2$.

Geometric data

Edge lengths and corner angles become genuine measurements.

$M_k$-complex

$K=\bigsqcup_i P_i \big/ \!\sim,\qquad P_i\subset M_k^2$

Length metric

Glue isometrically along edges to get a geodesic space (Bridson-Haefliger I.7).

Printed pp. 153-156Metrising the cells
Give each polygonal two-cell the metric of an M_k polygon so the complex becomes a geodesic M_k-complex. Edge lengths and corner angles are now genuine geometric data, and the same combinatorial object carries a length metric in which we can finally ask about local curvature.

The Link Condition

Link criterion

$K \text{ is locally CAT}(k) \iff \mathrm{Lk}(v)\text{ has no loop }<2\pi\ (k\le 0)$

Reading it

Large links $\Rightarrow$ non-positive curvature $\Rightarrow$ algebra controlled by geometry.

Printed pp. 153-156Link condition and curvature
By the link condition the metric complex is locally CAT(k) exactly when every vertex link is a metric graph with no short loops, girth at least two-pi for k at most zero. Thus a presentation becomes non-positively curved when its links are large, linking algebra to global geometry.

Recap: One Object, Three Readings

As a space

Presentation complex $K$ and its universal cover $\widetilde{K}$.

As a group

$\pi_1(K)=\langle A\mid R\rangle$; words are paths, identity = loops.

As geometry

Van Kampen diagrams give area; $M_k$-metric plus links give curvature.

The chain

$\langle A\mid R\rangle \rightsquigarrow K \rightsquigarrow \widetilde{K} \rightsquigarrow D \rightsquigarrow \mathrm{CAT}(0)$

Payoff

Combinatorial group theory and non-positive curvature meet on a single complex.

Printed pp. 153-156Recap and outlook
Recap the chain: presentation, presentation complex, fundamental group, Cayley complex, van Kampen diagram, metric and link condition. The payoff is that combinatorial group theory and non-positive curvature meet on one object. Diagrams are working records, and each picture is paired with an invariant the reader can check.
