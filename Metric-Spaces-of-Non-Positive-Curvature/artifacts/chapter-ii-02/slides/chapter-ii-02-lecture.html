The Metric Is Convex

Proposition

For geodesics $c_1,c_2:[0,1]\to X$ the separation is convex in the parameter.

Source

Apply the CAT(0) comparison to the Euclidean model triangle, where it is elementary.

Convexity inequality

$$d\big(c_1(t),c_2(t)\big)\le (1-t)\,d\big(c_1(0),c_2(0)\big)+t\,d\big(c_1(1),c_2(1)\big)$$

Reading

The distance map is the engine; the corollaries are squeezed out of it.

Printed pp. 175-183The Metric Is Convex
State the central proposition: for any two geodesics the distance between corresponding points is a convex function of the parameter. Derive it from the CAT(0) comparison inequality applied to the Euclidean model, where the same statement is elementary. Emphasise that convexity is the engine, not a corollary.

Uniqueness of Geodesics

Setup

Two geodesics with the same endpoints: $c_1(0)=c_2(0)$ and $c_1(1)=c_2(1)$.

Continuity

The same bound makes geodesics vary continuously with their endpoints.

Forced to coincide

$$d(c_1(t),c_2(t))\le 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c_1\equiv c_2$$

Why

A convex function vanishing at both ends is identically zero between them.

Printed pp. 175-183Uniqueness of Geodesics
Apply convexity to two geodesics sharing both endpoints. The distance between them is a convex function vanishing at both ends, hence identically zero. Conclude geodesics are unique and, by the same estimate, vary continuously with their endpoints. This is the first concrete payoff of the convex metric.

Convex Functions and Convex Sets

Convex function

$f$ is convex if $f\circ c$ is convex along every geodesic $c$.

Convex set

$C$ contains the geodesic between any two of its points.

Distance is convex

$$x\mapsto d(x,C)\quad\text{is a convex function}$$

Use

Convexity of $d(\cdot,C)$ powers the projection theorem next.

Printed pp. 175-183Convex Functions and Convex Sets
Define convex functions along geodesics and convex subsets as those containing the geodesic between any two of their points. Note that distance to a point and distance to a convex set are themselves convex functions. These definitions set up the projection theorem on the following slide.

Nearest-Point Projection

Theorem

Let $C\subseteq X$ be complete and convex. Each $x$ has a unique nearest point $\pi(x)\in C$.

Existence

Completeness plus convexity of $d(\cdot,C)$ yields a minimiser.

Defining property

$$d(x,\pi(x))=d(x,C)=\inf_{y\in C} d(x,y)$$

Uniqueness

The strict midpoint estimate kills any second minimiser.

Printed pp. 175-183Nearest-Point Projection
State the projection theorem: onto a complete convex subset of a CAT(0) space there is a unique nearest point. Existence uses completeness and the convexity of the distance function; uniqueness uses the strict midpoint estimate. The projection map is the central construction of the chapter.

Projection Is a Retraction

Non-expanding

The projection never increases distance between points.

Retraction

It fixes $C$ pointwise and is continuous, so it retracts $X$ onto $C$.

Printed pp. 175-183Projection Is a Retraction
Show the projection does not increase distance. Compare the two segments from a pair of points to their projections using the convex quadrilateral estimate. Because the map fixes the convex set pointwise and is continuous, it is a retraction onto it. This non-expanding property is used repeatedly.

CAT(0) Spaces Are Contractible

Geodesic retraction

Slide each point toward a basepoint along its unique geodesic.

Homotopy

Continuity of geodesics in endpoints makes $H$ continuous in both variables.

Straight-line homotopy

$$H(x,s)=c_x(s),\quad H(x,0)=x,\; H(x,1)=p$$

Consequence

Contractible, hence aspherical: $\pi_n(X)=0$ for all $n\ge 1$.

Printed pp. 175-183CAT(0) Spaces Are Contractible
Use the geodesic retraction to a basepoint: slide every point along its unique geodesic to the centre. Continuity of geodesics in their endpoints makes this a genuine homotopy. Hence CAT(0) spaces are contractible, therefore aspherical, with all higher homotopy groups vanishing.

The Center of a Bounded Set

Circumradius

The infimum of radii of balls containing a bounded set $B$.

Unique center

Two distinct centers give a strictly better midpoint center — contradiction.

Printed pp. 175-183The Center of a Bounded Set
Introduce the circumradius of a bounded set as the infimum of radii of enclosing balls. Convexity forces the centre realising it to be unique: two distinct centres would yield a strictly better midpoint centre. This circumcenter construction is canonical and isometry-equivariant.

The Bruhat–Tits Fixed-Point Theorem

Hypothesis

A group $\Gamma$ acts by isometries with a bounded orbit $\Gamma\!\cdot\!x$.

Equivariance

Isometries permute the orbit, so they fix its unique circumcenter.

Conclusion

$$\exists\, c\in X:\quad \gamma\cdot c = c \ \ \forall\,\gamma\in\Gamma$$

Why it matters

Bounded orbit $\Rightarrow$ global fixed point: the cornerstone for actions on CAT(0) spaces.

Printed pp. 175-183Bruhat-Tits Fixed Point
Combine uniqueness of the circumcenter with equivariance. A group acting by isometries with a bounded orbit fixes the circumcenter of that orbit, since isometries permute the orbit and hence fix its unique centre. This is the Bruhat-Tits fixed-point theorem, a cornerstone for actions on CAT(0) spaces.

Flat Subspaces: Where Convexity Saturates

Flat

An isometrically embedded Euclidean piece $\mathbb{E}^n\hookrightarrow X$.

Equality

Along a flat the convexity inequality becomes an equality.

Saturation

$$d(c_1(t),c_2(t)) = (1-t)\,d_0 + t\,d_1$$

Looking ahead

Flats mark zero curvature and foreshadow the flat torus theorem.

Printed pp. 175-183Flat Subspaces
Mention flat subspaces: isometrically embedded Euclidean pieces along which the convexity estimate becomes an equality. They mark where curvature is exactly zero and foreshadow the flat torus theorem. Tie this back to convexity being an inequality that flats saturate.
