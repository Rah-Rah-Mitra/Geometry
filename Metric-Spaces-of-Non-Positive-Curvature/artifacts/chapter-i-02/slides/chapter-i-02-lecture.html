$M_k^n$: the reference geometries of constant curvature $k$

One family

For each $k\in\mathbb{R}$ a complete, simply connected space of constant curvature $k$.

Three signs

Sphere ($k>0$), Euclidean plane ($k=0$), hyperbolic space ($k<0$).

Purpose

This chapter, printed pages 15 through 31, builds one family of reference geometries. For every real number k there is a complete, simply connected space of constant curvature k. One Construction, Three Signs

Uniform build

The spaces $M_k^n$ are defined together; the sign of $k$ selects which geometry appears.

Reduction by scale

Rescaling sends any $k>0$ to the unit sphere and any $k<0$ to standard $\mathbb{H}^n$.

Curvature dictionary

$k>0:\;\mathbb{S}^n_k \qquad k=0:\;\mathbb{E}^n \qquad k<0:\;\mathbb{H}^n_k$

Only sign and scale matter

Every theorem proved for the three reference values transfers by a similarity.

Printed pp. 15-31One Construction, Three Signs
Stress the uniformity: positive, zero and negative curvature are not three unrelated theories but one construction read off at three signs of k. Rescaling reduces any positive k to the unit sphere and any negative k to standard hyperbolic space, so only the sign and a scale factor ever matter in practice.

The Sphere $\mathbb{S}^n$ — positive curvature

Distance = subtended angle

$d(x,y)=\arccos\langle x,y\rangle,\quad |x|=|y|=1$

Geodesics

Great-circle arcs of the unit sphere.

Antipodes

Printed pp. 15-31The Sphere
On the unit sphere distance is the angle subtended at the centre, computed from the Euclidean dot product through arccosine. Geodesics are great-circle arcs. Highlight the finite diameter pi and the fact that antipodal points are joined by infinitely many minimizing geodesics, unlike the other two models.

Hyperbolic Space $\mathbb{H}^n$ — negative curvature

Minkowski model

$\langle x,y\rangle_- = x_1y_1+\dots+x_ny_n-x_{n+1}y_{n+1}$

Distance

$\cosh d(x,y) = -\langle x,y\rangle_-$

Geodesics

Sections of the hyperboloid by planes through $0$; infinite diameter.

Printed pp. 15-31Hyperbolic Space
Hyperbolic space lives on the upper sheet of a hyperboloid under the Lorentzian form. Distance uses the inverse hyperbolic cosine of minus the Minkowski product. Geodesics are intersections with planes through the origin. The space is unbounded, and triangles will turn out to be thinner than their Euclidean comparisons.

Rescaling to General $k$

Positive $k$

$M_k^n$ is $\mathbb{S}^n$ with metric scaled by $1/\sqrt{k}$.

Negative $k$

$M_k^n$ is $\mathbb{H}^n$ with metric scaled by $1/\sqrt{-k}$.

Zero $k$

$M_0^n=\mathbb{E}^n$, the fixed flat baseline.

Diameter of $M_k^n$

$D_k=\dfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{k}}\ (k>0),\qquad D_k=\infty\ (k\le 0)$

Printed pp. 15-31Rescaling to General k
Explain the scaling dictionary. Multiplying the metric of the unit sphere or standard hyperbolic space by one over root k reproduces every nonzero curvature. So results proved for the three reference values transfer automatically, and the diameter formula pi over root k for positive k drops straight out of this rescaling.

The Law of Cosines in Each Model

Sides $a,b,c$, angle $\gamma$ opposite $c$

Euclidean $k=0$

$c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\gamma$

Spherical $k=1$

$\cos c=\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b\cos\gamma$

Hyperbolic $k=-1$

$\cosh c=\cosh a\cosh b-\sinh a\sinh b\cos\gamma$

Continuity in $k$

As $k\to 0$ both curved rules degenerate to the Euclidean template, so the family varies continuously.

Printed pp. 15-31The Law of Cosines
Present the three cosine rules side by side. The Euclidean rule is the linear template; the spherical and hyperbolic rules replace lengths by sine or hyperbolic sine and cosine of the curvature-scaled sides. Point out that letting k tend to zero recovers the flat rule, confirming the family is continuous in k.

How Triangle Thickness Depends on $k$

Positive $k$

Sides bulge outward; angle sum $>\pi$.

Zero $k$

Straight comparison triangle; angle sum $=\pi$.

Negative $k$

Sides cave inward; angle sum $<\pi$.

Printed pp. 15-31Triangle Thickness vs k
This is the geometric punchline. With sides fixed, a larger curvature fattens a triangle and shrinks its angle sum below the bound on the sphere side, while negative curvature thins it and pushes the angle sum under pi. Comparison geometry will pin abstract triangles between these reference shapes.

Isometry Groups — full homogeneity

$\mathbb{S}^n$

Isometries $=O(n+1)$, acting on the unit sphere.

$\mathbb{E}^n$

Isometries $=\mathbb{R}^n\rtimes O(n)$, translations and rotations.

$\mathbb{H}^n$

Isometries from the Lorentz group $O(n,1)$.

Transitivity

$\text{Isom}(M_k^n)\ \text{acts transitively on points and frames}$

Consequence

The geometry is identical at every point and in every direction, so one comparison triangle, placed anywhere, captures the model.

Printed pp. 15-31Isometry Groups
Each model is highly symmetric: its isometry group acts transitively on points and on frames, so the geometry looks identical everywhere and in every direction. Name the orthogonal, Euclidean and Lorentz groups. This homogeneity is why a single comparison triangle, placed anywhere, captures the whole model.

Why $M_k^n$ Are the Rulers

Comparison triangle

Given $\triangle(p,q,r)$ in any space, draw $\bar\triangle$ in $M_k$ with the same side lengths.

CAT($k$) condition

Real distances inside $\triangle$ are no larger than in $\bar\triangle$.

Curvature $\le k$

Triangles are no fatter than their model comparisons.

Comparison inequality

$d(x,y)\ \le\ d_{M_k}(\bar x,\bar y)$

Printed pp. 15-31Why the Model Spaces Matter
Close by stating the payoff. The model spaces are the fixed measuring instruments: a geodesic triangle in an arbitrary space is compared to a triangle with the same side lengths drawn in M_k. Curvature bounded above by k means real triangles are no fatter than their model comparisons. That comparison is the whole game.
