Local versus Global CAT(k)

Locally CAT(k)

Every point has a ball in which all geodesic triangles satisfy the comparison inequality.

Globally CAT(k)

The inequality holds for every triangle, with geodesics between any two points.

The gap

Local control says nothing about far-apart points until we add hypotheses.

Comparison inequality

$$d(x,y)\le d_{\bar M_k}(\bar x,\bar y)$$

Distances do not exceed those in the model space $\bar M_k$.

Printed pp. 193-204Local versus Global CAT(k)
Here we separate two notions students often conflate. Local CAT(k) means every point owns a small ball satisfying the comparison inequality, while global CAT(k) demands the inequality everywhere on geodesics joining arbitrary points. The chapter is exactly the bridge between these two scales when curvature is non-positive.

The Theorem Statement

Cartan-Hadamard (metric form)

Let $k\le 0$. If $X$ is complete, simply connected, and locally CAT(k), then $X$ is globally CAT(k).

Four hypotheses

Completeness, simple connectivity, local CAT(k), and the sign $k\le 0$ — each is essential.

Read it as

The local upper bound, freed from topological obstructions, becomes a global upper bound.

Printed pp. 193-204The Theorem Statement
We state the metric Cartan-Hadamard theorem precisely. For k at most zero, a complete, simply connected, locally CAT(k) space is globally CAT(k). I stress each hypothesis aloud: completeness, simple connectivity, the local bound, and the sign restriction on k, since dropping any one of them breaks the conclusion.

Why Simple Connectivity?

The flat torus

$T^2$ is locally CAT(0) but has infinitely many geodesics between two points.

Not globally CAT(0)

Competing geodesics violate uniqueness, so the global comparison fails.

The fix

Pass to the universal cover $\mathbb{E}^2$, which is simply connected and globally CAT(0).

Printed pp. 193-204Why Simple Connectivity?
This slide motivates the topological hypothesis with the flat torus. The torus is locally CAT(zero) yet has many geodesics between points and is not globally CAT(zero). Its universal cover, the Euclidean plane, repairs everything. Simple connectivity is precisely what rules out the homotopically distinct competing geodesics that monodromy would create.

Covering map

Loops unroll

Local Geodesics and Development

Local geodesic

A path $c$ that is a geodesic on each small subinterval: locally distance-minimising.

Developing

Straighten $c$ patch by patch against the model $\bar M_k$, gluing comparisons along it.

Construction

Overlapping local comparisons chain into a single global comparison for $c$.

The Local-to-Global Principle

Principle

In a complete, simply connected, locally CAT(k) space ($k\le0$), every local geodesic is a global geodesic and the CAT(k) inequality holds globally.

Engine

Small loops contract, so local convexity propagates with no obstruction.

Propagation

Convexity of the metric on each patch chains along a path into global convexity.

Printed pp. 193-204The Local-to-Global Principle
This is the engine of the proof. The local-to-global principle says that in a complete simply connected space where the comparison holds locally and small loops contract, local geodesics are globally unique and the CAT(k) inequality propagates. I describe how convexity of the local metric chains along a path to a global convexity statement.

Uniqueness of Geodesics

Output

In $\tilde X$ any two points are joined by a unique geodesic.

Continuity

That geodesic varies continuously with its endpoints.

Reason

Two rival geodesics with the same endpoints would violate metric convexity.

Uniqueness

$$\exists!\;\gamma:[0,1]\to\tilde X,\ \gamma(0)=x,\ \gamma(1)=y$$

Printed pp. 193-204Uniqueness of Geodesics
Here we record the central output: in the universal cover any two points are joined by a unique geodesic, varying continuously with its endpoints. I explain that uniqueness follows from convexity of the distance function, which forbids two distinct geodesics with the same endpoints, and underwrites the contraction we build next.

Convexity of the Metric

Convexity inequality

$$d\big(\gamma(t),\sigma(t)\big)\le (1-t)\,d(p,\sigma(0))+t\,d(p,\sigma(1))$$

Distance between geodesics from a common point grows convexly.

Certificate

Convexity is exactly the CAT(0) thinness on the comparison triangle.

Tool

It produces unique geodesics and the continuous geodesic contraction.

Printed pp. 193-204Convexity of the Metric
We make convexity quantitative. Along two geodesics issuing from a common point the distance grows convexly, captured by the CAT(zero) inequality on the comparison triangle. This convex behaviour of the metric is both the certificate of the upper bound and the tool that produces unique geodesics and the geodesic contraction.

Contractibility and Asphericity

Contractible cover

Sliding along unique geodesics to a basepoint deformation-retracts $\tilde X$ to a point.

Aspherical base

Hence $\pi_n(X)=0$ for $n\ge2$; $X$ is a $K(\pi_1,1)$.

Consequence

$$\pi_n(\tilde X)=0\ \ \forall n,\quad \pi_n(X)=0\ (n\ge2)$$

Printed pp. 193-204Contractibility and Asphericity
We harvest the topological consequences. Because geodesics to a basepoint are unique and depend continuously on the endpoint, sliding along them gives a deformation retraction, so the cover is contractible. Hence the original space is aspherical: all higher homotopy groups vanish and it is a classifying space for its fundamental group.

Generalisation and Scope

General form

The universal cover of any complete, connected, locally CAT(k) space with $k\le0$ is globally CAT(k).

Drop simple connectivity

For a general base, pass to $\tilde X$; the conclusion holds upstairs.

Why $k\le0$

Positive $k$ forces the model-sphere diameter bound $\pi/\sqrt{k}$, blocking unrestricted lifting.

Printed pp. 193-204Generalisation and Scope
We state the general form. The universal cover of any complete, connected, locally CAT(k) space with k at most zero is globally CAT(k). I note where the sign matters: positive k forces a diameter bound from the model sphere, so the unrestricted lifting argument is special to non-positive curvature.
