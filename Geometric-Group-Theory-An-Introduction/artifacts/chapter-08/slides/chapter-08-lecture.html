Boundary As A Functor

Wish-list

Functorial Goal

A boundary mechanism assigns \(X \mapsto X_\infty\) and sends quasi-isometric embeddings to continuous maps.

Quasi-Isometry Test

If \(X \simeq_{\mathrm{QI}} Y\), then the induced boundary spaces should be homeomorphic.

The lecture studies two ray-based answers: coarse components for ends, and finite-distance ray classes for the Gromov boundary.

Boundary constructions are used here as quasi-isometry invariants, not as decorative compactifications.

Use this slide to make the wish-list concrete. A useful boundary construction should assign a topological space to a metric space and should send a quasi-isometric embedding to a continuous boundary map. The important payoff is that quasi-isometries become homeomorphisms at infinity, so coarse geometry can be tested topologically.

Why Three Ends Cannot Occur

Proof geometry

The Obstruction

A finitely generated group cannot have a finite number of ends larger than two.

  1. Choose three rays whose tails separate outside a ball.
  2. Translate by elements far out on the first ray.
  3. Trivial action on the finite end set forces the translated tails back through the ball.
  4. Left-invariance contradicts the required separation of the two other rays.

The standard conclusion: a finitely generated group has 0, 1, 2, or infinitely many ends.

Present the proof idea visually rather than line by line. If a finitely generated group had a finite number of ends bigger than two, translates far along one ray would force two other far-out rays to pass near the origin after translation. Left invariance then contradicts their required separation outside the ball.

Stallings Reads Branching Algebraically

Ends to splitting

Stallings Decomposition

Infinitely many ends occur exactly when the group splits over a finite subgroup, in the amalgamated or HNN sense.

Why It Matters

A topological signal at infinity tells us that the group can be decomposed into smaller algebraic pieces.

branching at infinity → action on a tree → Bass-Serre splitting

Application focus: ends detect algebraic decomposition and quasi-isometry rigidity for virtually free behavior.

Use Stallings' theorem as the bridge from coarse topology back to group structure. Infinitely many ends mean the large-scale geometry has genuine branching at infinity, and Bass-Serre theory packages that branching as an action on a tree with finite edge stabilizers. This is why ends are crude but powerful.

Gromov Boundary Refines Ends

Ray classes

Boundary Class

The Gromov boundary \(\partial X\) records quasi-geodesic rays up to finite Hausdorff distance.

\( \gamma \sim \gamma' \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad d_{\mathrm{Haus}}(\mathrm{im}\,\gamma,\mathrm{im}\,\gamma') < \infty \)

Refinement

Ends ask which component contains the tail. The Gromov boundary asks which direction the ray shadows.

Gromov boundary is most useful in the hyperbolic setting, where quasi-geodesics are controlled.

Contrast the two equivalence relations. Ends remember only which component a ray eventually occupies, while the Gromov boundary remembers a finite-Hausdorff-distance class of quasi-geodesic rays. In a one-ended hyperbolic space this can still leave a rich boundary, so the invariant sees more than component count.

Hyperbolicity Makes Rays Rigid

Geodesic models

Morse Stability

In a proper hyperbolic space, quasi-geodesic rays stay within bounded distance of geodesic rays.

Basepoint Control

Every boundary point can be represented by a geodesic ray from a chosen basepoint, up to finite distance.

Examples

\(\partial \mathbb R\) has two points, a regular tree has Cantor boundary, and \(\partial H^n\) is a sphere.

Chapter 7's hyperbolicity results become the control mechanism for Chapter 8's boundary.

The boundary definition is broad, but hyperbolicity is what makes it manageable. Morse stability says quasi-geodesic rays stay near geodesic rays, and properness lets one choose representatives from a common basepoint. Ask students to connect the disk picture with the previous chapter's thin-triangle technology.

Boundary Maps Carry Quasi-Isometries

Invariance engine

Embedding

A quasi-isometric embedding induces a continuous injective map \(\partial X \to \partial Y\).

Finite Distance

Maps that stay a bounded distance apart induce the same map at infinity.

Equivalence

A quasi-isometry promotes to a homeomorphism of Gromov boundaries.

Boundary maps convert coarse map data into topological obstructions and rigidity tools.

This is the technical engine behind the applications. A quasi-isometric embedding sends quasi-geodesic rays to quasi-geodesic rays, so it induces a boundary map. Finite-distance maps agree on the boundary, and a quasi-isometry gives a homeomorphism. This is how coarse equivalence becomes a topological obstruction.

Read the examples as a spectrum of how much infinity can remember. Finite groups have no boundary, the integers have two points, free groups have Cantor-set behavior, and closed hyperbolic n-manifold groups inherit a sphere. The contrast with ends explains why boundaries are the sharper invariant.

Independent Boundary Points

Ping-pong at infinity

Boundary Pair Of An Element

An infinite-order element \(g\) determines attracting and repelling points \(g^\infty\) and \(g^{-\infty}\) in \(\partial G\).

Mini Tits Alternative

Disjoint boundary pairs allow high powers to generate a free subgroup. Overlap forces virtual cyclic behavior.

\(\{g^\infty,g^{-\infty}\} \cap \{h^\infty,h^{-\infty}\} = \emptyset \quad \Rightarrow \quad \langle g^m,h^n\rangle \cong F_2\)

Boundary dynamics supply the ping-pong domains used to find free subgroups in non-elementary hyperbolic groups.

Frame the mini Tits alternative as a boundary ping-pong argument. Infinite-order elements define two boundary points each. If two such pairs are disjoint, high powers push complementary domains back and forth, producing a free subgroup. If the pairs overlap, hyperbolicity forces the generated subgroup toward virtual cyclic behavior.

Rigidity From Boundary Data

Mostow route
homotopy equivalence→ lift to \(H^n\)→ quasi-isometry
boundary homeomorphism→ conformal sphere map→ isometry

Mostow Rigidity, Lecture Form

For closed hyperbolic \(n\)-manifolds with \(n \ge 3\), the weak topological equivalence forces the strong geometric equivalence.

The boundary is not a side object. It is the place where the lifted quasi-isometry becomes rigid enough to descend to geometry.

Application: boundary methods turn coarse equivalence of universal covers into geometric rigidity.

Mostow rigidity is the chapter's showcase for boundary methods. A homotopy equivalence between closed hyperbolic manifolds lifts to a quasi-isometry of hyperbolic space, hence to a sphere homeomorphism at infinity. In dimension at least three, the boundary map is forced to be conformal and therefore comes from an isometry.

What The Invariant Can And Cannot See

Calibration
Ends

Stable And Coarse

Excellent for detecting finite, two-ended, and infinitely branching group geometries.

Limitation: many interesting spaces are one-ended.

Gromov boundary

Sharp In Hyperbolic Worlds

Tracks ray directions and supports boundary maps, dimension obstructions, and dynamics.

Limitation: outside hyperbolicity, quasi-rays can be too unconstrained.

Hypotheses

The Fine Print Matters

Properness, geodesicity, and hyperbolicity decide whether rays, topologies, and compactifications behave cleanly.

Boundary arguments are strongest when the class of spaces controls quasi-geodesics.

A boundary invariant is only as useful as the geometric class that controls its ray representatives.

Calibrate the method before the recap. Ends are stable and easy to compute but often collapse interesting spaces to one point of information. Gromov boundaries are much sharper for hyperbolic spaces, but outside the hyperbolic setting they can become too large or poorly behaved. The hypotheses matter.
