Review of group actions

An action is a homomorphism into transformations

Two equivalent interfaces

\( \rho : G \longrightarrow \operatorname{Aut}(X) \)

The map preserves multiplication: applying \(h\) and then \(g\) matches applying \(gh\).

\( (gh)\cdot x = g\cdot(h\cdot x), \qquad e\cdot x=x \)

The first line is categorical. The second line is how computations are checked.

The diagram is a pointwise version of the homomorphism law.
Start with the grammar before examples. The homomorphism to an automorphism group is the compact definition, while the pointwise action law is the calculation interface students will actually use. Keep both visible so later stabilizer, quotient, and ping-pong statements do not feel like new notation.
Free actions

Fixed points are the first obstruction

Set action

\(G\curvearrowright X\) is free when \(g\cdot x=x\) forces \(g=e\).

Graph action

The chapter uses the stronger graph convention: no nonidentity element fixes a vertex or an edge as an unordered edge.

Left translation on vertices is always free; edges remember whether \(s^2=e\).
Use the two rows of the visual to separate free actions on sets from free actions on graphs. On vertices, a nonidentity element cannot leave a point unmoved. On graphs, the stronger convention also prohibits flipping an edge setwise, which is exactly where order-two generators create trouble.
Orbits and stabilizers

Motion splits into reachable points and leftover symmetry

The square symmetry action makes stabilizers visible: corners, edges, and center have different leftover symmetry.

Orbit

\(G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x \mid g\in G\}\)

The orbit is the reachable set of \(x\).

Stabilizer and fixed set

\(G_x=\{g\in G\mid g\cdot x=x\}\)
\(X^g=\{x\in X\mid g\cdot x=x\}\)
For finite actions: \(|G\cdot x|\,|G_x|=|G|\)
Treat the square as a small laboratory for the orbit-stabilizer relationship. Moving a corner by all symmetries produces four corners, while only two symmetries preserve the selected corner. The center behaves differently because every symmetry fixes it, so stabilizers record hidden redundancy in the motion.
Counting via group actions

Burnside averaging counts configurations up to symmetry

Double-count the fixed-pair ledger

\(F=\{(g,x)\mid g\cdot x=x\}\)
\(\displaystyle |G\backslash X|=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|X^g|\)

Instead of listing orbit representatives, ask each symmetry how many configurations it leaves unchanged.

The ledger is a reusable diagnostic: each row measures a symmetry class by its fixed set.
Present the counting formula as a change of viewpoint rather than a trick. Counting orbits directly can be messy, but averaging fixed points over group elements lets each symmetry contribute a measurable number. The ledger visual is deliberately synthetic and is meant to train the double-counting reflex.
Transitive actions on graphs

A free transitive graph action is a Cayley graph in disguise

Recipe

  1. Choose a base vertex \(x\).
  2. Identify \(g\in G\) with \(g\cdot x\).
  3. Declare \(S=\{s\in G\mid \{x,s\cdot x\}\in E\}\).

Conclusion

If the action is free and transitive on vertices, the graph is isomorphic to \(\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)\).

This slide explains why transitive graph actions recover Cayley graphs. Choose a base vertex, identify every other vertex with the unique group element carrying the base there, then read adjacent base-neighbors as generators. A path in the graph becomes a word in those generators.
Free groups and tree actions

Free groups are exactly groups that act freely on trees

Theorem 4.2.1, lecture form

A group is free if and only if it admits a free action on a nonempty tree.

Reverse direction

Start with a free action on a tree, choose orbit representatives, contract the redundant parts, then use the transitive-action criterion to identify a Cayley tree.

State the central equivalence with the tree picture in view. One direction is familiar from free groups and their Cayley trees. The reverse direction is more geometric: a free action on a tree has enough structure to build a Cayley tree and therefore recover a free generating set.
Spanning trees for actions

Choose one vertex from each orbit, then collapse translates

Spanning tree of an action

A subtree containing exactly one vertex from every orbit of the vertex action.

Why it matters

Contracting the spanning tree and its translates creates a graph on which \(G\) acts freely and transitively on vertices.

essential edges \(\longrightarrow\) generators
Explain the spanning tree as a fundamental set of orbit representatives, not as an arbitrary red subgraph. The action translates this tree through the ambient tree. When each translate is collapsed, the remaining essential edges become the generator candidates in the proof that the acting group is free.
Application: Nielsen-Schreier

Subgroups of free groups inherit tree actions

Geometric proof idea

Let \(F\) be free. Its Cayley graph is a tree. Any subgroup \(H\leq F\) acts on the same tree by restricting the action.

free action on a tree \(\Rightarrow H\) is free

Rank bookkeeping

For finite-index subgroups, quotient graph data count how many independent loops survive after choosing a spanning tree.

Use Nielsen-Schreier as the payoff for geometric thinking. A subgroup of a free group inherits the free action on the original Cayley tree. The theorem from the previous slide then turns an algebraic subgroup question into the geometry of a restricted tree action.
Ping-pong lemma

Disjoint attracting domains certify free generators

Hypothesis pattern

Find nonempty disjoint regions \(A^+,A^-,B^+,B^-\) in a space where \(a\) and \(b\) act.

Conclusion pattern

Every reduced word has a visible final destination, so the subgroup generated by \(a\) and \(b\) is free.

geometry of domains \(\Rightarrow\) algebraic nontriviality
Introduce ping-pong as a proof machine for freeness. Each generator has a domain that attracts everything outside the opposite domain. If the domains are disjoint, a reduced word has a visible last move and cannot collapse to the identity because it sends a test point into a prescribed region.
Ping-pong proof intuition

The last surviving letter determines the final region

Read a reduced word as itinerary

\(w=a^{m_1}b^{n_1}a^{m_2}\cdots b^{n_k}\)

Choose a test point outside the forbidden starting region. Each block sends the point into the domain named by that block.

final domain \(\neq\) starting point
The proof tracks domains, not coordinates. That is why ping-pong is portable across actions.
Use this slide to rehearse the proof without drowning in notation. Read the word from right to left as a sequence of forced domain transfers. The final letter determines the final domain, so a nonempty reduced word moves the chosen point and therefore represents a nontrivial group element.
Free subgroups of matrix groups

Matrix actions turn slopes into ping-pong domains

Classical shear pair

\[ a=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad b=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix} \]

Act on projective slopes by fractional linear transformations.

Interpretation

Large powers push most slopes into narrow attracting regions. Disjoint regions provide the ping-pong certificate for a free rank-two subgroup.

Connect the abstract domains to a concrete matrix group. The shear matrices act on slopes by fractional linear transformations, and large powers push most slopes toward their favored regions. The point is not numerical magic; it is the same ping-pong mechanism now visible on the projective line.
Application: regular graphs of large girth

Short cycles are short relations in a quotient Cayley graph

Cycle relation dictionary

A closed path labeled by generators spells a word equal to the identity in the quotient.

Residual finite move

Choose a finite quotient where all nontrivial words up to a length threshold stay nontrivial.

free subgroup + finite quotients \(\Rightarrow\) regular graphs with large girth
Frame the graph application as a diagnostic consequence of freeness plus finite quotients. A short cycle in a quotient Cayley graph would represent a short relation. Residual finiteness lets one choose a quotient where the relevant short nontrivial words remain nontrivial, forcing the graph to have large girth.
Tits alternative outlook

Eigenlines provide ping-pong domains in projective space

Projective dynamics

A proximal matrix has an attracting line and a repelling hyperplane. Iterates push most projective points toward the attracting line.

separated dynamics \(\Longrightarrow\) free subgroup

The chapter uses this as the bridge from explicit examples toward the Tits alternative.

Use the eigenline diagram to prepare the Tits-alternative outlook. Hyperbolic or proximal matrices have attracting and repelling directions in projective space. When two elements place these directions in separated regions, powers of the elements satisfy ping-pong and generate a free subgroup.
