Growth Types of Groups

Growth asks how quickly word-metric balls fill the Cayley graph. The answer is a coarse invariant, so the lecture tracks what survives changed generators, quasi-isometries, and finite diagnostic windows.

Lecture Target

  • Define growth functions from word-metric balls.
  • Compare functions coarsely rather than exactly.
  • Read \( \mathbb Z \), \( \mathbb Z^2 \), and \(F_2\) as model behaviors.
  • Explain why growth type is preserved by quasi-isometry.
Open by treating growth as a measuring device rather than a new algebraic operation. Students already know Cayley graphs and word metrics from earlier chapters, so the new move is to count metric balls and then deliberately forget small-scale constants. Preview the examples because they anchor every formal comparison that follows.
Definition level

Growth Functions Count Word-Metric Balls

Working Definition

\( \gamma_{G,S}(r)=|B_S(e,r)| = |\{g\in G: |g|_S\le r\}| \)

Choose a finite generating set \(S\). The ball contains exactly those group elements represented by words of length at most \(r\).

Make the distinction between spheres and balls explicit. Sphere size is often easier to see in a tree, but the chapter's growth function is cumulative. This monotonicity matters later when functions are compared up to constants, because a small change in radius should not change the asymptotic class.
Equivalence of functions

Exact Counts Are Too Fragile; Growth Type Is Coarse

Comparison Relation

\( f \preceq g \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists C>0:\ f(r)\le C\,g(Cr+C)+C \)

Use \(f\simeq g\) when both \(f\preceq g\) and \(g\preceq f\). Constants absorb generator changes and bounded local distortion.

same type: \(r\) and \(3r+7\) same type: \(r^2\) and \(5r^2+20r\) different type: \(r^d\) versus \(2^r\)
Explain that coarse comparison is a deliberate loss of information, not imprecision. Exact values depend on the chosen generators and on small radii, but the comparison relation keeps the part relevant to large-scale geometry. This is the same philosophical move as quasi-isometry: ignore bounded errors and linear rescalings, then ask what remains.
Quasi-isometry preview

Finite Generating Sets Change the Grid, Not the Growth Type

Metric Consequence

For two finite generating sets \(S\) and \(T\), each generator in \(S\) has bounded \(T\)-length and each generator in \(T\) has bounded \(S\)-length.

\( |g|_T \le A|g|_S,\qquad |g|_S \le B|g|_T \)

So the identity map between word metrics is a quasi-isometry.

Use this slide to connect Chapter 05 with the growth chapter. A new finite generating set may tilt and round the apparent ball shape, but every old step can be simulated by a bounded number of new steps and conversely. The balls nest inside linearly rescaled balls, which is exactly the comparison relation for growth functions.
Model example: \(\mathbb Z\)

The Integer Line Has Linear Growth

Counting Formula

\( \gamma_{\mathbb Z,\{\pm1\}}(r)=2r+1 \)

Every point in the ball is one of \(-r,\ldots,0,\ldots,r\). The sphere has two new points for \(r\ge 1\).

degree 1 two ends no branching
Give students one example where nothing mysterious is hidden in the notation. In the integer group, word length is ordinary distance from zero, so the radius r ball is the interval from minus r to r. The formula also shows why growth is a large-scale statement: changing a few initial values cannot change linear behavior.
Model example: \(\mathbb Z^2\)

The Square Lattice Has Quadratic Growth

Counting Formula

\( \gamma_{\mathbb Z^2}(r)=1+2r(r+1) \)

The sphere of radius \(r\) has \(4r\) lattice points. Summing spheres gives a quadratic ball count.

The displayed check records \(\gamma(8)=145\) and \(\gamma(8)/8^2=2.265625\), already close to a stable quadratic scale.

Contrast this example with the line by focusing on spheres. The radius r sphere in the standard word metric is a diamond with four sides, each contributing linearly many points. Summing those linear sphere sizes creates a quadratic ball count, so the visual shape and the formula tell the same story.
Model example: \(F_2\)

The Free Group Has Exponential Growth

Reduced Words Branch

\( \gamma_{F_2}(r)=1+4(3^r-1)/2 \)

After the first letter, each reduced word has three choices at the next step because the immediate inverse is forbidden.

successive ratio near 3 tree-like balls
Stress that the free group example is not just larger than the lattice examples; it grows by a different mechanism. Reduced words branch persistently because cancellation only forbids one next letter. This creates sphere sizes proportional to powers of three, and cumulative balls inherit the same exponential scale.

Growth class

Polynomial Growth Means Ball Volume Is Bounded by a Power

Coarse Polynomial Bound

\( \gamma_G(r) \preceq r^d \)

For finitely generated groups, polynomial growth is a strong structural signal. The degree records the large-scale volume dimension of the Cayley graph.

The deeper theorem in the subject says finitely generated groups of polynomial growth are virtually nilpotent.

Present polynomial growth as a constraint rather than merely a slow rate. The line and lattice examples suggest dimension, and more complicated nilpotent examples can have degrees that do not simply equal a visible Euclidean dimension. The key lecture message is that polynomial bounds reveal algebraic organization, not just a small graph.
Growth class

Exponential Growth Means New Words Keep Multiplying

Coarse Exponential Lower Bound

\( a^r \preceq \gamma_G(r) \quad\text{for some } a>1 \)

Free substructures, persistent branching, and many independent word choices are common sources of exponential behavior.

Uniform exponential growth strengthens the idea by asking for a lower rate that does not collapse under changed finite generators.

Connect the formal inequality to the tree picture. Exponential growth is visible when each layer produces a fixed multiplicative supply of new elements, up to coarse constants. The uniform version is more subtle because it asks whether that supply is robust over all finite generating sets, not just the one used in the drawing.
Invariant argument

Quasi-Isometries Preserve Growth Type

Proof Roadmap

  1. A quasi-isometry sends an \(r\)-ball into an \((Ar+B)\)-ball.
  2. Bounded geometry controls how many original vertices can land near one target vertex.
  3. The quasi-inverse gives the reverse inequality.
This is the conceptual proof of invariance, not a line-by-line estimate. The map may distort distances, but only linearly and with bounded error, so an r ball cannot spread beyond a linearly rescaled ball. Bounded geometry controls multiplicity, and the quasi-inverse supplies the opposite comparison. Together these yield equality of growth type.

Applied lab

A Responsible Growth Diagnostic Has a Workflow

1. Choose data

Select a finite generating set and compute balls or spheres for a transparent radius range.

2. Predict class

State whether the evidence should look polynomial, exponential, or ambiguous before plotting.

3. Plot two scales

Compare ordinary counts with logarithmic counts so exponential slope is visible.

4. Save a check

Record a small numerical invariant that can be re-read without rerunning the whole notebook.

Frame the lab as mathematical hygiene. A finite ball can be persuasive, but it can also mislead if the graph has not reached its asymptotic regime. The workflow asks students to predict, compute, visualize, and save a check so the experiment remains tied to an invariant question instead of becoming an attractive isolated picture.
Takeaways

Growth Turns Cayley Graph Geometry Into Algebraic Evidence

What Survives Coarsening

  • Growth type does not depend on the chosen finite generating set.
  • Quasi-isometric finitely generated groups have the same growth type.
  • Polynomial and exponential behavior mark genuinely different large-scale geometries.

What To Keep Inspecting

  • Ball and sphere counts across radii.
  • Ordinary versus logarithmic diagnostic plots.
  • Boundary-to-volume behavior as a bridge to amenability.
Close by naming the through-line from earlier chapters to later ones. Word metrics made groups into spaces, quasi-isometries told us which large-scale features are meaningful, and growth now supplies a countable invariant of that geometry. The boundary preview should remain in memory because it becomes a natural bridge to amenability.
