Compact Symmetric Spaces: Weyl Alcoves And Rank-One Geometry

A compact symmetric space does not lose the local \( \mathfrak{k} \oplus \mathfrak{p} \) model. It loses the one-to-one exponential chart, and the replacement is a diagram of roots, walls, lattices, and alcoves.

The Chapter's Control Problem

What Replaces The Noncompact Polar Chart?

Local data

A compact symmetric pair gives \( \mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p} \), a base point \(o=eK\), and geodesics \(t \mapsto \operatorname{Exp}_o(tH)\) for \(H\in\mathfrak{p}\).

\( U/K,\qquad \mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{p},\qquad H\mapsto \operatorname{Exp}_o(H) \)

The problem is global: many \(H\)'s describe the same point, and some directions are singular.

Diagram data
  • Restricted roots cut \( \mathfrak{a} \) into reflecting walls.
  • The Weyl group identifies equivalent regular directions.
  • Affine root hyperplanes mark conjugate and periodic behavior.
  • A compact quotient remembers unit lattices, centers, and possible local isometry types.
The chapter is best read as a navigation manual: choose a maximal flat, fold it by a finite Weyl group, then quotient further by the lattice information that compactness makes unavoidable.
Compact type roadmapprinted pages 281-351
Use this slide to name the central replacement. In noncompact type the exponential coordinates on p are globally clean. In compact type that same map is periodic and singular along predictable walls. The point of the chapter is not a collection of isolated facts; it is a diagrammatic machine for deciding when two flat directions represent the same geometric point and where geodesic coordinates fail.
Section 1

The Compact Exponential Map Folds

Jacobi warning

Along a compact rank-one model, normal angular variations carry a sine factor. The schematic derivative behaves like

\( J(r) \sim \frac{\sin r}{r} \)

When the factor vanishes, nearby geodesics meet again. The local chart is not wrong; it has reached a conjugate level.

In higher rank, the same idea appears one root at a time through factors governed by \( \alpha(H) \).

Compact exponential foldingnormal coordinates remain local
A sphere is the friendliest model for the global change. Near the base point, geodesic coordinates behave just as expected. At the antipodal level, angular Jacobi fields collapse and the exponential map stops being a coordinate chart. Tell students to keep this simple sine factor in mind, because the higher-rank chapter repeats it root by root rather than only radially.
Sections 1-2

Reduce The Geometry To A Maximal Flat

Flat reduction

Choose a maximal abelian subspace \( \mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{p} \). Its exponential image is a compact flat torus or quotient flat inside \(U/K\).

Restricted roots are linear functionals on \( \mathfrak{a} \); each root gives reflection data and a family of walls.

\( \mathfrak{a}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\{H:\alpha(H)\ne 0\ \text{for every restricted root } \alpha\} \)
Maximal abelian subspacerestricted-root walls
This slide should make maximal flats feel like a deliberate compression rather than a loss of information. The isotropy group moves many tangent vectors into a chosen a, and regular points in a avoid all root walls. Once students accept this reduction, the rest of the chapter becomes a study of how the finite and affine reflection groups carve this flat into meaningful regions.
Section 2

Weyl Groups Identify Equivalent Directions

Definition

The Weyl group of the compact symmetric pair is

\( W=N_K(\mathfrak{a})/Z_K(\mathfrak{a}) \)

It acts orthogonally on \( \mathfrak{a} \), is generated by root reflections, and lets every regular direction choose a representative inside one closed Weyl chamber.

The A2 picture is a working model: two simple reflections generate six chambers.

Finite Weyl groupregular directions modulo isotropy
Define the Weyl group as a quotient of a normalizer by a centralizer, but immediately translate that quotient into a geometric action on the flat. A regular geodesic direction does not care which chamber copy we happened to draw. The finite Weyl group is the first layer of redundancy, and its chamber picture is the finite part of the later affine alcove tiling.
Section 3

Conjugate Points Sit On Affine Root Walls

Diagnostic rule

The compact differential of \( \operatorname{Exp}_o \) has sine-type root factors. A conjugate level appears when a nonzero affine root condition is met:

\( \alpha(H)=m\pi,\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\} \)

Root walls through the origin describe singular directions; translated walls describe later conjugate levels.

Conjugate and singular diagramaffine root hyperplanes
The key teaching move is to separate two meanings of a wall. A wall through the origin says a direction has extra symmetry and is singular. A translated wall says the exponential map has moved far enough along a regular direction for a root Jacobi factor to vanish. Students should see that the same root system controls both phenomena, but with different offsets.
Section 3

Regular Points Are The Interior Of The Diagram

Regular

A regular \(H\in\mathfrak{a}\) avoids all root walls. Its centralizer is minimal, and its orbit type is stable under small perturbations.

Singular

A singular \(H\) lies on at least one root wall. Extra symmetry appears because some root directions no longer move freely.

Conjugate

A conjugate point records failure of the exponential differential. It may occur after leaving the origin and hitting an affine wall.

Practical distinction

For calculations, first classify the point inside the chamber. Then ask whether it also lies on a translated affine wall.

QuestionTest
Is the direction regular?\(\alpha(H)\ne 0\) for all roots
Is a conjugate level reached?\(\alpha(H)=m\pi\) for some nonzero integer
Regular, singular, conjugatesame roots, different tests
Pause here because students often merge singular and conjugate into one vague idea. A singular direction has extra centralizer at the point of departure or along a wall through the origin. A conjugate point is about the differential of the exponential map losing rank after travel. The diagram lets us compare these conditions without pretending they are the same condition.
Sections 4-6

Compact Groups Add Lattice And Center Data

Global compact group habits
  • A maximal torus records commuting compact directions.
  • The unit lattice \( \Gamma=\{H:\exp H=e\} \) records periods.
  • The center records which deck transformations survive as global symmetries.
  • Locally isometric compact spaces can differ by central quotients.
Maximal tori and centerscompact group bookkeeping
This is the chapter's reminder that Lie algebra data alone does not specify every compact global form. A maximal torus makes periodic directions visible, the unit lattice says when exponentials return to the identity, and the center determines possible quotients. Keep the language concrete: the same local symmetric-pair geometry can lead to different globally compact spaces.
Sections 7-8

The Affine Weyl Group Tiles The Flat

Finite plus translations

The finite Weyl group reflects the flat. Compactness adds translations coming from the unit lattice, producing an affine reflection group.

\( W_{\mathrm{aff}}\simeq W\ltimes \Gamma \)

A closed alcove is the compact-type replacement for a unique global normal coordinate domain.

Affine Weyl groupalcove as compact coordinate domain
Now assemble the finite and periodic information. The finite Weyl group alone folds a neighborhood of the origin, but compact geodesics also return after lattice translations. The affine Weyl group combines both operations. A closed alcove is not just a pretty triangle; it is the chosen coordinate domain after all the finite reflection and periodic identifications have been accounted for.
Highest-Root Boundary

An Alcove Is A System Of Inequalities

A2 normalized model

In simple-root coordinates, a first compact alcove can be modeled by

\( a_1\ge 0,\quad a_2\ge 0,\quad a_1+a_2\le 1 \)

The third wall is the highest-root wall. It is where the first translated constraint closes the compact domain.

The highest root is the first root combination that closes the compact chamber; it predicts shortest closed-geodesic thresholds in many examples.
Alcove inequalitieshighest-root wall
Make the affine picture computational. A student should be able to test a sample point by checking nonnegative simple-root coordinates and the highest-root bound. In the A2 normalization, the highest root is alpha1 plus alpha2, so the slanted wall is the first place the compact domain closes. This is also the lab pattern used by the notebook.

Rank-One Geometry

Shortest Closed Geodesics See The First Wall

Geometric reading

The first affine wall tells where a geodesic stops being uniquely minimizing. A later lattice period tells when it closes.

In rank one, this distinction is visible as a cut or antipodal locus. In higher rank, it is distributed across root directions and highest-root constraints.

first wall \( \Rightarrow \) cut behavior, period \( \Rightarrow \) closed geodesic
Shortest closed geodesicsfirst wall versus period
Students may expect compact geodesics to be periodic and minimizing for the same reason, but those are different thresholds. The first wall is the cut or conjugate warning, while the full lattice period records closure. In rank one the distinction is easy to draw on a line. In higher rank, root directions decide which wall arrives first.
Section 12 Appendix

Small Singular Sets Do Not Derail Global Arguments

Why an appendix appears

The singular set is geometrically important, but it is often small enough to avoid in path and homotopy arguments.

codimension at least 2generic paths can be pushed away
codimension at least 3loop homotopies can be controlled

Dimension theory appendixavoidance as a geometric safety net
Treat this appendix as a safety net for the chapter's global arguments. Singular sets are not ignored because they are unimportant. They are controlled because their dimension is low enough that generic paths and sometimes homotopies can avoid them. This explains why arguments can work on the regular part and then extend to the compact space with carefully stated exceptions.
Applied Lab

Classify Points In A Compact Flat

Point test

For normalized A2 coordinates, compute three booleans:

  • inside closed alcove: \(a_1\ge0\), \(a_2\ge0\), \(a_1+a_2\le1\)
  • on Weyl wall: \(a_1=0\) or \(a_2=0\)
  • on highest affine wall: \(a_1+a_2=1\)

This is a small version of the notebook's compact flat classifier.

The lab habit is deliberately simple: draw the chamber, mark the walls, and translate a qualitative geometry question into inequalities.
Compact flat classifierregular, wall, boundary, outside
This applied slide turns the previous theory into a classroom exercise. Give students a point in simple-root coordinates and ask them to classify it before discussing the geometry. The important habit is not the A2 numbers themselves; it is the translation from geometric language into wall inequalities and then back into statements about regularity, conjugacy, and quotient representatives.
Proof Architecture

How The Chapter's Arguments Tend To Move

1. Choose a flatMove tangent data into a maximal abelian subspace of \( \mathfrak{p} \).
2. Cut by rootsUse restricted roots to find chambers, singular walls, and Jacobi factors.
3. Fold by groupsQuotient by Weyl reflections and lattice translations to find representatives.
4. Restore topologyAdd center and quotient data before declaring a compact global space.
Instructor move

When a theorem looks forbidding, ask which of the four moves it uses. Most results in this span are variations on flat reduction, wall detection, group identification, and global quotient correction.

Common mistake

Do not stop after the Lie algebra calculation. Compact type has finite and affine identifications, and locally isometric quotients may require center data.

Reusable proof roadmapfrom infinitesimal to global
This is the synthesis slide before the final checklist. It gives students a way to read dense theorem statements without losing the plot. First put everything in a maximal flat. Then let roots cut the flat. Then quotient by finite and affine Weyl symmetries. Finally bring back the center and quotient information needed for the actual compact space. That four-step route explains many proofs in the chapter.
Recap

Compact Type Checklist

What to remember
  • Compact exponential maps fold; they do not give one global chart.
  • Restricted roots and Weyl groups organize maximal flats.
  • Affine walls encode conjugate levels and lattice periodicity.
  • Centers and quotients distinguish local from global compact spaces.
  • Rank-one examples are the quick geometric test bed.
