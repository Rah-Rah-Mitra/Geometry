The Atlas-By-Identification Idea

Working Definition

An \(n\)-manifold is a topological space where each point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open set in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

Practical Move

Specify the manifold by a polygon (or polyhedron) together with a list of side (or face) identifications.

Habit to build

The arrows on the sides are the manifold. Any embedding picture is an after-the-fact convenience.

Definition: local model + transition compatibilityWorking tool: gluing presentation
Before drawing anything, fix the mental model. A manifold near a point looks Euclidean. Globally it is assembled by gluing local pieces along instructions. Stress that the gluing data is the manifold; the embedding is optional. The notebook treats the square-to-torus and octagon-to-genus-two pairings as the first laboratory.
First laboratory
chi = 0, genus 1

Square To Torus

Gluing Word

\(a\,b\,a^{-1}\,b^{-1}\)

Bookkeeping

Vertices \(V=1\), edges \(E=2\), faces \(F=1\). Euler characteristic \(\chi = V-E+F = 0\). Genus \(g = 1\).

Why this is enough

The arrows determine the manifold up to homeomorphism. No embedding in three-space is required to know we have a torus.

Atlas-by-identification: arrows are the manifoldThe square is the first trustworthy laboratory
Walk through the square with opposite sides identified. Match the arrows on top and bottom, and on the two vertical sides. All four corners collapse to a single point. The result is a torus with Euler characteristic zero and genus one. The slide deliberately leaves the abstract surface unembedded; the arrows are the manifold.
Scaling up
chi = -2, genus 2

Octagon To Genus-Two Surface

Side Word

\(a\,b\,a^{-1}\,b^{-1}\,c\,d\,c^{-1}\,d^{-1}\)

Euler Count

\(V=1\), \(E=4\), \(F=1\), so \(\chi = 1-4+1 = -2\) and \(g=2\).

Pattern

The standard \(4g\)-gon word produces an orientable surface of genus \(g\) with \(\chi = 2-2g\).

Polygon words classify orientable surfacesThe bookkeeping scales linearly with genus
Repeat the bookkeeping for the side word a b a-inverse b-inverse c d c-inverse d-inverse. Eight sides, four arrow types, all eight vertices identified to one. The Euler-characteristic formula gives 1 minus 4 plus 1 equals minus two, so the surface has genus two. This is the first hint that polygon words classify orientable surfaces.
Hyperbolic surfaces
Poincare disk

Hyperbolic Surfaces Through The Disk

Geodesics

In the Poincare disk, hyperbolic lines are Euclidean arcs that meet the boundary circle at right angles (diameters are the degenerate case).

Surfaces By Quotient

A hyperbolic surface of genus \(g \ge 2\) is the disk with a hyperbolic \(4g\)-gon, identified by a discrete group of disk isometries.

Notebook check

The notebook draws several disk geodesics and verifies the boundary-orthogonality condition numerically.

Hyperbolic surfaces appear before formal machineryThe disk makes geodesics testable
Switch from polygon pictures to the Poincare disk. Hyperbolic lines are Euclidean arcs orthogonal to the boundary circle, and isometries are Mobius maps preserving the disk. A genus-two hyperbolic surface is a disk octagon with hyperbolic side identifications. The orthogonality is checkable numerically in the notebook.
Invariants
three faces of chi

Euler Characteristic Ledger

Cell subdivision

Adding a vertex on an edge increases both \(V\) and \(E\) by one; \(\chi\) does not move.

\(\chi\) depends on the surface, not the chosen cellulation.

Connected sum

Forming the connected sum of two surfaces subtracts two from the sum of their Euler characteristics.

\(\chi(\Sigma_1 \# \Sigma_2) = \chi(\Sigma_1) + \chi(\Sigma_2) - 2\).

Vector-field index

For a tangent vector field with isolated zeros, the sum of indices equals \(\chi\).

Hairy-ball theorem is the sphere case: \(\chi(S^2) = 2 \ne 0\).

Every operation preserves chi or changes it predictablyThat is what makes it a classification tool
Pause on Euler characteristic. It is invariant under cell subdivision, additive on connected sums up to a correction, and equal to the sum of vector-field indices on the surface. The slide presents these three facts as one ledger: every operation either preserves chi or changes it predictably. That is what makes it a classification tool.
Vector fields
sum of indices = chi

Vector Field Singularities And Index

Poincare-Hopf

\(\sum_{p \in \mathrm{zeros}(X)} \mathrm{ind}_p(X) \;=\; \chi(\Sigma).\)

Read-back

The index count gives a dynamical certificate of \(\chi\). On the sphere it forbids nonvanishing fields; on the torus it allows them.

Index is local; chi is global; the equation links themSphere vs torus is the cleanest contrast
Introduce vector-field indices as the dynamical witness for Euler characteristic. A sphere admits no nonvanishing vector field; a torus does. The slide draws representative singularities (source, sink, saddle) with their index values plus or minus one, and reminds students that the sum equals chi for any tangent vector field with isolated zeros.
Up one dimension
first 3-manifold

Cube With Opposite Faces Identified

Local model

An open 3-ball. The cube interior is one chart.

Gluing Recipe

Identify the three pairs of opposite square faces by parallel translation. The result is the three-torus \(T^3\).

New bookkeeping

Which edges and vertices identify? Twelve edges collapse to three edge classes, eight vertices to one. The Euler-characteristic of \(T^3\) is zero.

Local: open 3-ball. Global: paired faces.T^3 is the three-dimensional analog of the square-to-torus
Move the gluing imagination to three dimensions. A cube with opposite faces identified produces the three-torus. Emphasize that the local model is the open three-ball; the gluing is on the boundary. The slide also names the new bookkeeping question: which edges and vertices identify, and what does the resulting universe look like?

First Three-Manifold Zoo

Three-torus \(T^3\)

Cube with opposite faces identified by translation.

Flat geometry; simplest closed example.

\(\mathbb{RP}^3\)

Closed 3-ball with antipodal boundary points identified.

Same idea as projective plane, one dimension up.

Lens spaces

Solid torus glued to a solid torus along a slope \(p/q\) on the boundary.

Discrete parameter family.

Dodecahedral example

Closed dodecahedron with face pairings producing a closed 3-manifold.

Face pairings can encode hyperbolic structure.

Four recipes, four universesNo classification claimed yet; only variety

Applied lab
predict the surface

Predict A Surface From Its Pairing Word

Standard \(4g\)-gon Recipe

Word \(a_1 b_1 a_1^{-1} b_1^{-1} \cdots a_g b_g a_g^{-1} b_g^{-1}\). All vertices identify to one; \(F=1\), \(E=2g\), so \(\chi = 2-2g\).

Quick test

Apply the recipe to a hexagon with word \(a\,b\,c\,a^{-1}\,b^{-1}\,c^{-1}\): six sides, three edge classes, all vertices identify, so \(\chi = 1-3+1 = -1\)? Recount: \(F=1, E=3, V=2\) here, giving \(\chi = 0\) and genus 1 — match against the torus.

Caveat

The lab does not replace the classification theorem. It is a fast checkable witness for individual examples.

The lab distills the first half of the chapter into a computationUse it before tackling classification proofs
Walk through the applied lab. For a standard orientable polygon word with four-g sides, all vertices identify and Euler characteristic equals two minus two-g. Apply the formula to a hexagon with a b c a-inverse b-inverse c-inverse to get genus one. The lab gives students a fast checkable test before tackling the classification theorem.
