Section 1
local but not global

Negative Curvature Before the Abstract Model

Pseudosphere

Surface of revolution of the tractrix with constant Gaussian curvature \(K = -1\).

Failure

The surface develops an edge (cusp circle); it cannot be the whole hyperbolic plane.

Hilbert's theorem

No complete \(C^2\)-isometric immersion of \(\mathbb{H}^2\) into \(\mathbb{R}^3\) exists.

A local model with a global obstructionMotivates intrinsic and projective pictures
Begin with the pseudosphere. It has constant negative curvature but develops an edge and cannot be the whole hyperbolic plane. The lesson is that no surface embedded in Euclidean three-space realizes the entire hyperbolic plane isometrically. This failure motivates the move to intrinsic and projective models in the sections that follow.

Section 2a
conformal model

Poincare Disk: Conformal Hyperbolic Plane

Metric

\(ds^2 = \frac{4(dx^2+dy^2)}{(1-x^2-y^2)^2}\)

Geodesics

Euclidean arcs that meet the boundary circle at right angles (diameters are the degenerate straight case).

Conformality

Angles in the model equal hyperbolic angles. Sizes do not: the boundary is at infinity.

Angles honest; sizes distorted near the boundaryIsometries: Mobius maps preserving the disk
Move to the Poincare disk. Hyperbolic lines are Euclidean circles orthogonal to the boundary, and the model is conformal: angles are honest. The metric scale factor blows up near the boundary, which is why the same Euclidean segment is hyperbolically much longer near the edge. This is the conformal half of the inversive picture.
Section 2b
scale by 1/y

Upper Half-Plane Model

Metric

\(ds^2 = \frac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2}\)

Geodesics

Vertical lines and Euclidean semicircles with endpoints on the real axis.

Isometry group

\(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})\) acting by \(z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}\). Boundary is the real axis plus the point at infinity.

Conformal, again; same hyperbolic plane, different chartMobius classification: parabolic / elliptic / hyperbolic
Present the upper half-plane model as the half-plane companion to the disk. Both are conformal. The metric scale is one over y, which makes the boundary the line at infinity. Mobius maps that preserve the upper half-plane are the isometry group, and parabolic, elliptic, and hyperbolic isometries are visible by their fixed-point behavior on the boundary.
Section 3
three projections, one geometry

Hyperboloid, Klein, Poincare Projections

Hyperboloid Model

\(-x_0^2 + x_1^2 + x_2^2 = -1,\ x_0 > 0\)

Distance recovered by \(\cosh d(u,v) = -\langle u, v\rangle_{\mathrm{Mink}}\).

Klein Disk

Project from origin to plane \(x_0 = 1\). Geodesics become straight chords; angles are distorted.

Poincare Disk

Project to \(x_0 = 0\) by a different rule. Geodesics bend; angles preserved.

Distance algebraic on the hyperboloid; geometric in projectionsThe same H^2 viewed three ways
Add the hyperboloid model. Points sit on the upper sheet of a Minkowski hyperboloid, and the hyperbolic distance is recovered from the Minkowski inner product by an inverse hyperbolic cosine. Project from the origin to get the Klein disk model, where geodesics are straight chords but angles are distorted. Project to a paraboloid to recover the Poincare disk; both pictures are projections of the same hyperboloid.
Section 4
invariant checks

Hyperbolic Computations as Invariant Checks

Distance

\(d(u,v) = \mathrm{arccosh}\bigl(-\langle u,v \rangle_{\mathrm{Mink}}\bigr)\)

Law of Cosines

\(\cosh c = \cosh a \cosh b - \sinh a \sinh b \cos C\)

Workflow

Compute an invariant in two models; demand exact agreement up to numerical tolerance. Disagreement signals a model-translation bug.

Notebook test

Triangle with legs \(a,b\) and included angle \(C\): law of cosines is checked in disk, half-plane, and hyperboloid coordinates.

Reading

This is the chapter's main discipline: numbers, not just pictures, witness that the models agree.

Algebra cross-checks geometryModel translations must preserve invariants
Show that model changes are safe only when invariant formulas agree. In the hyperboloid model the points satisfy the Minkowski quadric, and cosh of the distance equals the negative inner product. The hyperbolic law of cosines is checkable numerically, and the chapter's invariant-check workflow is to verify the same number across two model representations.
Section 5
complex coordinates

Isometries and the Hermitian Picture of \(\mathbb{H}^3\)

Disk isometries

\(\mathrm{Isom}^+(\mathbb{H}^2) \cong \mathrm{PSU}(1,1)\), Mobius maps preserving the disk.

Upper half-space

\(\mathbb{H}^3 = \{(z,t): t > 0\}\) with metric \((|dz|^2 + dt^2)/t^2\).

Action

\(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})\) acts as orientation-preserving isometries of \(\mathbb{H}^3\) via boundary Mobius extension.

Hermitian form view

Hyperbolic three-space sits inside the projectivized space of Hermitian \(2\times 2\) matrices with positive determinant; the action of \(\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})\) is congruence.

Friend

This is the complex coordinate friend of hyperbolic geometry: it embeds smoothly into projective complex geometry.

Hyperbolic 3-space = complex linear algebra mod centerThe friend opens the door to Kleinian groups
Cover isometries. In the disk the isometry group is the group of Mobius transformations preserving the disk; in upper half-space it acts as PSL of two over the complex numbers. The Hermitian-form picture of hyperbolic three-space makes this action natural and connects to complex coordinates. This is the friend of hyperbolic geometry called complex projective.
Section 6
positive curvature friend

The Three-Sphere and the Hopf Fibration

Sphere

\(S^3 = \{(z,w) \in \mathbb{C}^2 : |z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1\}\), homogeneous under \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\).

Hopf fibration

\(S^3 \to S^2,\quad (z,w) \mapsto z/w\)

fibers are great circles, pairwise linked.

Stereographic view

Project from one Hopf fiber to \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Other fibers appear as linked circles foliating Euclidean three-space.

Three-dimensional geometry includes positive curvatureSame chapter, opposite sign
Introduce the three-sphere as the positively curved friend. It is a homogeneous space with its own isometry group, and the Hopf fibration decomposes it into linked great circles. Stereographic projection lets us inspect several Hopf fibers in ordinary three-space. The chapter uses this to argue that three-dimensional geometry includes positive curvature alongside hyperbolic.
Applied lab
disk distances

Lab: Disk Distances Versus Euclidean

Setup

Two points on the same Euclidean radius of the disk, at distances \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) from the center.

Euclidean

\(d_{\mathrm{Euc}} = |r_2 - r_1|\)

Hyperbolic

\(d_{\mathrm{hyp}} = \left|\,\log\!\frac{1+r_2}{1-r_2} - \log\!\frac{1+r_1}{1-r_1}\,\right|\)

Observation

For points near the center, \(d_{\mathrm{hyp}} \approx 2 \, d_{\mathrm{Euc}}\). For points near the boundary, \(d_{\mathrm{hyp}}\) diverges while \(d_{\mathrm{Euc}}\) stays bounded.

Reading

Euclidean intuition inside the disk is only safe after the conformal factor is included.

Numerical witnesses for the metric scaleThe boundary really is at infinity
Walk through the applied lab. Vary two points on the same Euclidean radius of the disk. Euclidean distance changes monotonically; hyperbolic distance reacts nonlinearly because of the metric scale factor. The table and plot make concrete the warning that Euclidean intuition inside the disk is useful only after the metric is included.
Recap

Recap: Move Among Models, Trust the Invariants

  • Embedded surfaces give local intuition but cannot be the global model.
  • Conformal models (disk, half-plane) preserve angles; sizes distort near the boundary.
  • Projective and hyperboloid models trade angle honesty for algebraic distance or straight geodesics.
  • Hermitian model of \(\mathbb{H}^3\) connects hyperbolic geometry to complex projective geometry.
  • The three-sphere introduces a positively curved friend with its own homogeneous structure.
