Curves are surfaces

Generic meeting

Two real surfaces in real dimension four meet generically in points.

Homological count

$A\cdot B = \sum_{p}\, m_p(A,B), \qquad m_p \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$

Local controls global

The class fixes $A\cdot B$; analysis fixes each local term $m_p$.

We work in an almost complex four-manifold where curves are real two-dimensional and generically meet in points. Positivity of Intersections

Isolated

Distinct simple curves meet in finitely many points.

Strictly positive

Each local multiplicity satisfies $m_p \ge 1$.

No cancellation

Intersections cannot cancel against one another.

Positivity theorem

$A\cdot B \;=\; \sum_p m_p \;\ge\; \#\{\text{intersection points}\}$

Printed pp. 653-694 | PDF pp. 668-709Positivity of Intersections
Two distinct closed J-holomorphic curves meet in finitely many isolated points, and every local intersection multiplicity is a strictly positive integer. Summing these local indices recovers the homological number A dot B, so distinct curves can never cancel intersections the way smooth surfaces with opposite signs might.

The Local Model

Graphs over $z$

Each branch is a graph $w = f_i(z)$ over a common coordinate.

Leading terms

Compare lowest-order complex terms of $f_1$ and $f_2$.

Local multiplicity

$f_2(z) - f_1(z) = c\,z^{m} + o(z^m), \quad m_p = \mathrm{ord}_0(f_2 - f_1)$

Holomorphic engine

The difference vanishes to order $m$; that order is the index.

Printed pp. 653-694 | PDF pp. 668-709The Local Model
Near a meeting point we model the two branches as graphs over a common coordinate, comparing their leading complex terms. The difference of branches behaves like z to a power m, and the multiplicity equals the order of vanishing of that difference. This local holomorphic picture is the engine behind every positivity statement.

Transverse Versus Tangent

Transverse

Distinct tangent lines give $m_p = 1$, the minimal positive value.

Tangent

Higher-order contact gives $m_p \ge 2$.

Printed pp. 653-694 | PDF pp. 668-709Transverse Versus Tangent
A transverse intersection contributes multiplicity exactly one, the minimal positive value. Tangency or higher-order contact raises the multiplicity above one, since the branches agree to higher order before separating. The local index thus measures precisely how strongly the two curves cling together at the shared point.

Winding and Contact Order

Small circle

Track $f_2 - f_1$ on a tiny loop about the point.

Winding $=$ order

The winding number equals the contact order $m$.

Winding count

$\mathrm{wind}_0\big(f_2 - f_1\big) = m \;>\; 0$

Printed pp. 653-694 | PDF pp. 668-709Winding and Contact Order
On a small circle around the meeting point, the difference of the two branches winds positively, and its winding number equals the contact order. This positive winding is the computational face of positivity: the local index can never be negative because a genuine winding count is always nonnegative for distinct branches.

Perturbing to Transverse Points

Split the contact

Solve $z^m = \varepsilon$ for small $\varepsilon$.

$m$ simple points

One tangency becomes $m$ transverse crossings.

Perturbation

$z^m - \varepsilon = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; z = \varepsilon^{1/m}\,e^{2\pi i k/m}, \; k=0,\dots,m-1$

Perturbing the contact equation z to the m minus epsilon splits a single tangential meeting into exactly m simple transverse intersections, each of multiplicity one. This shows the local index m counts hidden transverse crossings, confirming that the contribution is both positive and an honest integer equal to the contact order.

The Adjunction Formula

Self-intersection

$A\cdot A$ records how a curve meets a pushoff of itself.

Defect $\delta$

Counts double points and cusps with positive weights.

Adjunction formula

$A\cdot A \;=\; 2g - 2 + c_1(A) + 2\,\delta(A), \qquad \delta \ge 0$

An exact ledger

Topology and $c_1$ balance against local singularity cost.

Printed pp. 653-694 | PDF pp. 668-709The Adjunction Formula
The adjunction formula ties the homological self-intersection A dot A to the genus and the first Chern number through a nonnegative singularity term delta. Delta counts double points and cusps with positive weights, so the formula becomes an exact ledger relating topology, the canonical class, and the curve's local defects.

The Adjunction Inequality

Drop $\delta$

Nonnegativity of $\delta$ yields a genus bound.

Equality test

Equality holds iff the curve is embedded.

Adjunction inequality

$2g - 2 \;\le\; A\cdot A - c_1(A), \quad \text{equality} \iff \delta = 0 \iff \text{embedded}$

Printed pp. 653-694 | PDF pp. 668-709The Adjunction Inequality
Dropping delta yields the adjunction inequality, a bound on the genus in terms of self-intersection and the Chern number. Equality holds exactly when the singularity term vanishes, which happens precisely when the curve is embedded. The inequality is therefore a sharp embeddedness test driven by local positivity.

Micallef-White Theorem

Topological model

A singularity looks like a holomorphic one after a homeomorphism.

Transfer

Classical plane-curve singularity theory carries over.

Normal form

$u(z) = \big(z^k,\; a\,z^{k+1} + \cdots\big), \quad \text{leading term} + \text{higher order}$

Printed pp. 653-694 | PDF pp. 668-709Micallef-White Theorem
The Micallef-White theorem states that a J-holomorphic singularity is, after a homeomorphism of a neighborhood, topologically identical to a holomorphic curve singularity. This transfers the entire classical theory of plane-curve singularities into the almost complex world and justifies treating delta exactly as in algebraic geometry.
