The Nonlinear Cauchy-Riemann Section

Base

A Banach manifold $\mathcal{B}$ of maps $u:\Sigma\to M$ in class $A$.

Fibre

Antiholomorphic one-forms $\Omega^{0,1}(u^*TM)$.

Section

$\bar\partial_J(u) = \tfrac12\big(du + J(u)\,du\,j\big)$

Moduli space

$\mathcal{M}(A,J) = \bar\partial_J^{-1}(0)$.

Printed pp. 39-74The Nonlinear Cauchy-Riemann Section
We frame the moduli problem as a section of an infinite-dimensional bundle. The base is a Banach manifold of maps, the fibre carries the antiholomorphic one-forms, and the curves we want are exactly the points where the nonlinear Cauchy-Riemann section meets the zero section of this bundle.

The Linearized Operator $D_u$

Vertical differential

$D_u$ is the linearization of $\bar\partial_J$ at a solution $u$.

Principal part

The first-order $\bar\partial$ operator; lower terms depend on $J$, $du$.

Linearization

$D_u\xi = \bar\partial\xi + \tfrac12 J(\nabla_\xi J)\,\partial_J(u)$

Type

A real Cauchy-Riemann operator, hence elliptic.

Printed pp. 39-74The Linearized Operator
At a solution the vertical differential of the section is the linearized operator D-u. Its principal part is the standard first-order Cauchy-Riemann operator, with lower-order zeroth terms depending on J and the derivative of u. That principal part is precisely why the operator is Fredholm between Sobolev completions.

Fredholm Index and Riemann-Roch

Fredholm

$\dim\ker D_u<\infty$, $\dim\mathrm{coker}\,D_u<\infty$.

Expected dimension

$\mathrm{index}\,D_u$ is the virtual dimension.

Riemann-Roch

$\mathrm{ind}\,D_u = 2n(1-g) + 2\langle c_1(A),[\Sigma]\rangle$

Printed pp. 39-74Fredholm Index and Riemann-Roch
Because D-u is Fredholm it has finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel, and its index is computed by Riemann-Roch. The index gives the expected or virtual dimension of the moduli space, here twice n times one minus genus plus twice the first Chern number, before quotienting by automorphisms.

Regularity Means Surjectivity

Regular curve

$D_u$ is onto, so $\mathrm{coker}\,D_u=0$.

Local chart

The moduli space looks like $\ker D_u$ near $u$.

Implicit function theorem

$T_u\mathcal{M}(A,J) = \ker D_u$

Elliptic regularity

Kernel and cokernel are independent of the Sobolev completion.

Printed pp. 39-74Regularity Means Surjectivity
Regularity has one precise meaning: the linearized operator D-u is onto, so its cokernel vanishes. When that holds the implicit function theorem identifies a neighbourhood of u in the moduli space with the kernel of D-u, a smooth chart of exactly the expected dimension.

Cutting Out a Smooth Manifold

Transverse section

$\mathrm{im}\,D\bar\partial_J + T(\text{zero section}) = $ everything.

Consequence

$\mathcal{M}^*(A,J)$ is a smooth manifold of the expected dimension.

Printed pp. 39-74Cutting Out a Smooth Manifold
The geometric picture is a section meeting the zero section transversally. Transverse intersection means the section's image and the zero section together span the ambient tangent space, and the implicit function theorem in Banach spaces then carves out a smooth manifold whose dimension equals the Fredholm index.

The Universal Moduli Space

Let $J$ vary

$\mathcal{M}(A,\mathcal{J}) = \{(u,J): \bar\partial_J u = 0\}$.

Extra input

An infinitesimal change $Y$ of the almost complex structure.

Universal linearization

$(\xi,Y)\mapsto D_u\xi + \tfrac12 Y(u)\,du\,j$

Goal

The new $Y$-term reaches directions $D_u$ alone misses.

Printed pp. 39-74The Universal Moduli Space
To find regular J we let J vary alongside the map and form the universal moduli space. At a pair the linearization gains a new input, the infinitesimal change Y of the almost complex structure, contributing a term Y of u acting through du and j that the fixed-J operator could not reach.

Injective Points Fill the Cokernel

Simple curve

Has a dense set of injective points where $du\neq0$.

Localize $Y$

Choose $Y$ supported near the image to hit any missing direction.

Printed pp. 39-74Injective Points Fill the Cokernel
The universal linearization is onto because simple curves carry injective points. Near such a point du is nonzero and one can localize the variation Y to push the section into any missing cokernel direction. Hahn-Banach and elliptic regularity convert this local freedom into global surjectivity.

Sard-Smale and Generic $J$

Projection

$\pi:\mathcal{M}(A,\mathcal{J})\to\mathcal{J}$ is a Fredholm map.

Regular values

Sard-Smale: a residual (Baire-generic) set of $J$.

Main theorem

$J\in\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{reg}}(A)\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal{M}^*(A,J)\text{ smooth, }\dim=\mathrm{ind}\,D_u$

Fibre over $J$

$\pi^{-1}(J)=\mathcal{M}^*(A,J)$ is a smooth manifold.

Printed pp. 39-74Sard-Smale and Generic J
Projecting the universal moduli space to the space of almost complex structures gives a Fredholm map. The Sard-Smale theorem supplies a residual set of regular values, and over each such generic J the fibre is a smooth moduli space of simple curves with the expected dimension.

Multiple Covers Break Transversality

No injective points

A multiple cover $u=v\circ\phi$ has none, so the argument fails.

Persistent cokernel

The $Y$-localization collapses; $\mathrm{coker}\,D_u$ may survive for all $J$.

Printed pp. 39-74Multiple Covers Break Transversality
The argument needs injective points, so it fails for multiply covered curves. A multiple cover has no injective points, the localization of Y collapses, and the cokernel may persist for every J. This obstruction is the origin of virtual techniques developed in later chapters.

Chern Splitting: Automatic Regularity

Grothendieck splitting

$u^*TM \cong \bigoplus_k \mathcal{O}(k_i)$ over $\mathbb{CP}^1$.

Onto criterion

$D_u$ surjective $\iff$ every degree $k_i \ge -1$.

Dimension four

$\deg(\text{normal}) = c_1(u^*TM)-2 \ \ge\ -1$

Failure mode

Normal degree $-2$: cokernel appears, curve not regular.

Printed pp. 39-74Chern Splitting for Spheres
For spheres regularity can sometimes be read from topology. A bundle over the projective line splits into line bundles, and the real Cauchy-Riemann operator is onto exactly when each summand has degree at least minus one. In dimension four this becomes a normal-degree threshold for embedded spheres.

Constraints and the Quantitative IFT

Evaluation map

$\mathrm{ev}_w(u)=(u(w_1),\dots,u(w_m))$, transverse to $X$.

Constrained dimension

$\dim\mathcal{M} - \mathrm{codim}(X)$.

Quantitative IFT

$\|\bar\partial_J u\|\ \text{small},\ \ \|Q_u\|\le c\ \Rightarrow\ \exists!\,\xi,\ \bar\partial_J(\exp_u\xi)=0$

Engine for gluing

Right inverse $Q_u$ + quadratic estimate correct an approximate curve.

Printed pp. 39-74Constraints and the Quantitative IFT
Point constraints enter through evaluation maps, and transversality to a constraint submanifold cuts the dimension by its codimension. The chapter closes with a quantitative implicit function theorem: a bounded right inverse plus a quadratic estimate turn an approximate solution into a genuine nearby curve.
