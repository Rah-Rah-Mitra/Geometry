The von Mises-Fisher Distribution

Sphere's Gaussian

Unimodal, rotationally symmetric about the mean direction $\mu$.

Concentration $\kappa$

Large $\kappa$ tightens; $\kappa=0$ gives the uniform law.

Density on $S^d$

$f(x) = c_d(\kappa)\,\exp\!\big(\kappa\,\mu^{\mathsf T} x\big)$

Parameters

Mean direction $\mu\in S^d$, concentration $\kappa\geq 0$.

Printed pp. 225-228The von Mises-Fisher Distribution
The von Mises-Fisher law is the sphere's Gaussian analogue. Its log density is linear in the observation through the mean direction mu, so the distribution is unimodal and rotationally symmetric about mu. The concentration kappa controls spread: large kappa tightens the cloud, kappa zero gives the uniform law.

The Bingham Distribution

Quadratic exponent

Uses $x^{\mathsf T} A x$, so it is unchanged by $x\mapsto -x$.

Axial, not directional

A law on real projective space $\mathbb{RP}^d$.

Density on $S^d$

$f(x) \propto \exp\!\big(x^{\mathsf T} A x\big),\quad A = A^{\mathsf T}$

Symmetric matrix $A$

Encodes the principal axis and the anisotropy of spread.

Printed pp. 225-228The Bingham Distribution
The Bingham density uses a quadratic exponent x-transpose A x, so replacing x by its antipode leaves it unchanged. This antipodal symmetry makes it a model for axes rather than directions, a law on real projective space. The symmetric matrix A encodes both the principal axis and the anisotropy of the spread.

The Watson Distribution

Rank-one special case

$f(x) \propto \exp\!\big(\kappa\,(\mu^{\mathsf T} x)^2\big)$

One parameter

$\kappa>0$ clusters at the axis; $\kappa<0$ forms a girdle.

Printed pp. 225-228The Watson Distribution
The Watson law is the rotationally symmetric special case of Bingham, where A is rank-one up to a scalar. A single concentration parameter governs whether mass clusters around an axis, a girdle, or spreads uniformly. It is the simplest axial model and the natural one-parameter benchmark for symmetric axial data.

Direction Versus Axis

Directional (vMF)

Distinguishes $x$ from $-x$; one pole carries the mass.

Axial (Bingham, Watson)

Identifies $x$ with $-x$; symmetric across the equator.

Symmetry test

$\text{vMF: } f(-x)\neq f(x)\quad\text{axial: } f(-x)=f(x)$

Where they live

vMF on $S^d$; axial laws descend to $\mathbb{RP}^d$.

Printed pp. 225-228Direction Versus Axis
The key contrast is symmetry. A directional model like von Mises-Fisher distinguishes x from minus x and concentrates at one pole. An axial model like Bingham or Watson identifies x with minus x, so its density is symmetric across the equatorial plane and lives naturally on projective space, not the sphere.

Planar Shapes as $\mathbb{CP}^{k-2}$

Complex landmarks

$k$ planar landmarks become a unit complex pre-shape vector $z$.

Quotient

Rotation and scale act as multiplication by a unit complex scalar.

Kendall planar shape space

$\Sigma_k^2 = \mathbb{CP}^{k-2},\quad z \sim e^{i\theta} z$

Invariance

Valid models must descend through the unit-scalar phase.

Printed pp. 225-228Planar Shapes as $\mathbb{CP}^{k-2}$
Kendall's planar shape space Sigma-k-2 is the complex projective space CP to the k minus two. Landmark configurations become unit complex vectors on the pre-shape sphere, and rotation and scaling quotient out as multiplication by a unit complex scalar. Parametric models must respect this complex projective invariance to be well defined.

Complex Bingham and Complex Watson

Hermitian form

Exponent $z^{*} A z$ with $A$ Hermitian.

Phase invariance

$z^{*}Az$ is unchanged by $z\mapsto e^{i\theta}z$, so it lives on $\mathbb{CP}^{k-2}$.

Planar-shape kernels

$f(z) \propto \exp\!\big(z^{*} A z\big),\quad A = A^{*}$

Complex Watson

Rank-one unimodal case: the primary kernel for planar shapes.

Printed pp. 225-228Complex Bingham and Complex Watson
On the planar shape space the Bingham and Watson laws reappear with a Hermitian quadratic form z-star A z. Because A is Hermitian the density is invariant under the unit-scalar phase, so it descends to CP. The complex Watson is the unimodal rank-one case used as the primary kernel for planar shapes.

Normalizing Constants and MLEs

Normalizers

Confluent hypergeometric / Bessel ratios for vMF and Watson; eigenvalue constants for Bingham.

Mean estimate

vMF: $\hat\mu$ = normalized resultant of the data.

vMF MLE

$\hat\mu = \tfrac{\sum_i x_i}{\lVert\sum_i x_i\rVert},\quad \tfrac{c_d'(\hat\kappa)}{c_d(\hat\kappa)} = \bar R$

Axial MLE

Mean axis = dominant eigenvector of the scatter matrix.

Printed pp. 225-228Normalizing Constants and MLEs
Each density needs a normalizing constant: confluent hypergeometric functions for vMF and Watson, eigenvalue-dependent constants for Bingham. Maximum likelihood sends the mean direction to the resultant vector and the concentration to a solved ratio of those normalizers, while axial models read the mean axis from the dominant eigenvector of the scatter.

These parametric laws are the kernels of the book's nonparametric Bayes mixtures.
