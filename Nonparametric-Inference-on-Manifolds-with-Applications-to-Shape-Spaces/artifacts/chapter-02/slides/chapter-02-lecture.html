Why Not Just Use $\mathbb{R}^n$?

The trap

Coordinates make the data look Euclidean, but the sample space is not.

Broken invariants

Wraparound, scale, and rotation are lost when you average raw coordinates.

Symptom

$\overline{(350^\circ,\,10^\circ)} = 180^\circ$  (wrong!)

Fix

Compute the mean on the manifold itself, respecting its structure.

Printed pp. 8-20Why Not Just Use R^n
The unifying lesson is that the sample space carries geometry the data must respect. Treating a wind direction or a landmark shape as an ordinary vector silently breaks wraparound, scale invariance, or the quotient structure, so means and tests computed in the wrong space can be meaningless or biased.

The Circular Mean

Naive average fails

Numbers wrap at the $0/2\pi$ seam, so arithmetic means jump.

Embed, average, project

Send each angle to a unit vector, average, take its direction.

Resultant & mean direction

$\bar C=\tfrac1n\sum\cos\theta_i,\;\bar S=\tfrac1n\sum\sin\theta_i$

$\bar\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(\bar S,\bar C),\quad \bar R=\sqrt{\bar C^2+\bar S^2}$

Prototype

This extrinsic mean lives on $S^1$ by construction.

Printed pp. 8-20The Circular Mean
Averaging angles as raw numbers fails near the wraparound seam. Instead we embed each angle as a unit vector, average in the plane, and read off the direction of the resultant. This extrinsic mean lives on the circle by construction and is the prototype for every manifold mean to come.

Directional Data on the Sphere $S^2$

Examples

Paleomagnetic field directions, surface normals, star fields.

Project back

Mean of embedding vectors has norm $\approx 0.93$; renormalize to $S^2$.

Printed pp. 8-20Directional Data on the Sphere
Paleomagnetic field directions and remote-sensing normals are points on the two-sphere. The sample mean of the embedding vectors generally has norm below one, so we project it back to the sphere. Our check reports a mean resultant norm near zero point nine three, confirming concentrated directions.

Axial Data on $\mathbb{RP}^d$

Undirected axes

Crystal orientations, rock fabric, line measurements: no head or tail.

Identify antipodes

$x$ and $-x$ are the same datum, so we quotient $S^d$ by $\pm$.

Printed pp. 8-20Axial Data and Real Projective Space
Some measurements are undirected axes: a crystal orientation or a rock-fabric line has no head or tail, so a direction and its antipode are identical. Identifying antipodal points on the sphere yields real projective space, the correct quotient manifold for axial statistics and a recurring example throughout the book.

Shape = Configuration modulo Similarity

Remove three nuisances

Translation, rotation, and uniform scaling carry no shape information.

Same shape

Two configurations agree in shape iff one is a similarity image of the other.

Printed pp. 8-20Similarity Invariance
Shape is what survives translation, rotation, and uniform scaling. We center the configuration to remove translation, divide by centroid size to remove scale, and quotient by rotation to remove orientation. Two configurations have the same shape exactly when one maps to the other under such a similarity transformation.

Kendall's Shape Space

The quotient

Center, scale to unit size, then quotient the pre-shape sphere by rotation.

Planar case

For $m=2$, the space is complex projective space $\mathbb{CP}^{k-2}$.

Construction

$\Sigma_m^k=\big\{[X]:X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times m}\big\}/\big(\text{trans},\text{scale},SO(m)\big)$

$\Sigma_2^k\cong \mathbb{CP}^{\,k-2}$

Mean shape

A manifold mean, not a coordinatewise landmark average.

Printed pp. 8-20Kendall's Shape Space
Quotienting centered, scaled configurations of k landmarks in m dimensions by rotation produces Kendall's shape space. For planar landmarks this space is a complex projective space, a smooth curved manifold. The mean shape is then a manifold mean, not a coordinatewise average of landmark positions.
