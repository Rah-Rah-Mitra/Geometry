The Extrinsic Idea

Curved object, flat ambient

$M$ is curved, but the surrounding $\mathbb{R}^N$ is flat and familiar.

Borrow Euclidean tools

Means, covariances, and limit theorems are inherited from $\mathbb{R}^N$.

Strategy

$M \xrightarrow{\;j\;} \mathbb{R}^N,\quad \text{stat on } j(M)$

One projection

The only nonlinear step is mapping the answer back to the surface.

Printed pp. 36-56The Extrinsic Idea
We start with the guiding picture. A manifold sits as a curved surface inside a high-dimensional Euclidean space through an embedding. Distances are measured by ambient chords rather than along the surface, which turns hard manifold averaging into Euclidean averaging followed by a single projection step back onto the image.

Choosing an Embedding

Immersion

$j:M\to\mathbb{R}^N$ smooth, injective, with injective differential.

Equivariance

Respect the symmetry group $G$ acting on $M$.

Equivariant embedding

$j(g\cdot m) = \rho(g)\,j(m),\quad g\in G$

Examples

Sphere $S^d\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$; Veronese-Whitney for shapes.

Printed pp. 36-56Choosing an Embedding
Here we make the embedding concrete. The map j must be a smooth injective immersion onto its image. A good embedding is equivariant under the relevant symmetry group, so that the induced statistics transform correctly. We give the sphere and the Veronese-Whitney embedding of shape spaces as motivating examples.

Extrinsic Distance

Chordal distance

Measure with the straight ambient line, not the surface arc.

Cheap to compute

Just the Euclidean norm of the difference of images.

Definition

$\rho_E(p,q) = \lVert j(p) - j(q) \rVert_{\mathbb{R}^N}$

Printed pp. 36-56Extrinsic Distance
This slide defines the extrinsic distance as the chordal Euclidean distance between embedded points. Because it is inherited from the ambient norm, it is easy to compute and compare. We contrast it briefly with the intrinsic geodesic distance and note when the two give similar local geometry.

The Euclidean Mean Lives Off the Manifold

Average the images

Form $\bar{y} = \tfrac1n\sum_i j(X_i)$ in $\mathbb{R}^N$.

Not on $j(M)$

A convex combination of surface points leaves the surface.

Ambient mean

$\mu = \mathbb{E}\,[\,j(X)\,]\in\mathbb{R}^N,\quad \mu\notin j(M)$

Raw material

This off-surface point is what the projection will correct.

Printed pp. 36-56The Euclidean Mean Lives Off the Manifold
We emphasize a crucial subtlety. Averaging embedded data in the ambient space produces a point that almost never lies on the image of the manifold. That off-surface mean is the raw material; the projection in the next slides is what restores a valid manifold-valued estimate from it.

Nearest-Point Projection

Closest image point

$P(u)$ minimizes ambient distance to $j(M)$.

Smooth where unique

$P$ is differentiable at points with a single minimizer.

Projection

$P(u) = \arg\min_{y\in j(M)} \lVert u - y \rVert$

Printed pp. 36-56Nearest-Point Projection
Here we introduce the projection map P that sends an ambient point to its nearest point on the embedded manifold. When the minimizer is unique the projection is well defined and smooth. We set up the variational definition and stress that uniqueness is exactly the nonfocal condition discussed next.

Nonfocal vs Focal Points

Nonfocal

Unique nearest point; projection $P$ well defined.

Focal

Several equidistant minimizers; $P$ is ambiguous.

Condition

$\mu \text{ nonfocal} \iff P(\mu) \text{ unique}$

Printed pp. 36-56Nonfocal vs Focal Points
This slide draws the key distinction. A nonfocal point has a single nearest neighbor on the manifold, so projection is unambiguous; a focal point is equidistant from several, making the nearest point set-valued. The extrinsic mean is defined precisely when the Euclidean mean is nonfocal, guaranteeing a unique projection.

The Extrinsic Mean

Population mean

Project the ambient expectation, then pull back through $j$.

Sample mean

Replace expectation with the empirical average $\bar{y}$.

Definition

$\mu_E = j^{-1}\!\big(P(\mathbb{E}\,[\,j(X)\,])\big)$

Estimator

$\hat\mu_E = j^{-1}\!\big(P(\bar{y})\big),\quad \bar{y}=\tfrac1n\!\sum_i j(X_i)$

Printed pp. 36-56The Extrinsic Mean
We assemble the definition. The extrinsic mean is the projection of the ambient expectation of the embedded distribution onto the manifold image, pulled back through the embedding. We state existence and uniqueness under nonfocality and define the sample version by replacing the expectation with the empirical average.

Strong Consistency

Ambient LLN

$\bar{y}\to\mu$ almost surely by the Euclidean law of large numbers.

Continuity of $P$

At a nonfocal $\mu$, $P$ is continuous, so it transports the limit.

Theorem

$\mu \text{ nonfocal} \;\Rightarrow\; \hat\mu_E \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu_E$

Why it works

Composition of an a.s. limit with a continuous map stays a.s.

Printed pp. 36-56Strong Consistency
This slide states the law of large numbers for the estimator. As sample size grows, the embedded sample mean converges almost surely to the embedded population mean; continuity of the projection at a nonfocal point then forces the extrinsic sample mean to converge almost surely to the extrinsic population mean.

Central Limit Theorem

Ambient CLT

$\sqrt{n}(\bar{y}-\mu)$ is asymptotically Gaussian in $\mathbb{R}^N$.

Differential of $P$

$d_\mu P$ maps fluctuations onto the tangent space.

Delta method

$\sqrt{n}\,(\hat\mu_E - \mu_E) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\big(0,\,\Sigma_T\big)$

Covariance

$\Sigma_T = d_\mu P\,\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\big(j(X)\big)\,(d_\mu P)^{\!\top}$

Printed pp. 36-56Central Limit Theorem
Here we linearize. The differential of the projection map transports ambient Gaussian fluctuations of the sample mean onto the tangent space of the manifold. The result is asymptotic normality of the extrinsic sample mean, with a covariance built from the projected ambient covariance restricted to tangent directions.

Two-Sample Tests

Compare means

Test $H_0:\mu_E^{(1)}=\mu_E^{(2)}$ for two populations.

Tangent coordinates

Express the difference in a shared tangent chart.

Statistic

$T_n = (\Delta)^{\!\top}\,\hat\Sigma^{-1}\,(\Delta) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{d}$

In practice

Bootstrap calibration sharpens small-sample shape comparisons.

Printed pp. 36-56Two-Sample Tests
We apply the theory. To compare two populations we test equality of their extrinsic means using the tangent-space coordinates of the difference and the asymptotic covariances from the central limit theorem. This yields a chi-squared statistic that powers shape-comparison and bootstrap procedures used throughout the book.

Recap and Outlook

Pipeline

Embed, average, project, linearize, test.

Payoff

Closed-form estimators with clean limit theory.

Caveat

Validity hinges on the ambient mean being nonfocal.

One line
