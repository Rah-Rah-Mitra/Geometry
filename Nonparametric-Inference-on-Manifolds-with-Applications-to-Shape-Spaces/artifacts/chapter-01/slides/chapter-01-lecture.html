When Data Leave $\mathbb{R}^n$

Directions

Unit vectors on the sphere $S^{d}$.

Axes

Lines through the origin: $\mathbb{RP}^{d}$.

Shapes

Landmarks modulo similarity.

Common feature

$M = S^d / G$   (quotient by a group)

Consequence

Averaging coordinates leaves the space $M$.

Printed pp. 1-7When Data Leave R^n
Three running examples motivate the geometry: unit directions on a sphere, undirected axes in projective space, and configurations of landmarks viewed modulo similarity. Each lives on a manifold, so adding points or averaging coordinates leaves the space. The chapter uses these to show why ordinary moments are ill defined.

Directions on the Sphere

Directional data

Wind headings, orientations, migration angles as unit vectors.

Failure of the vector mean

$\big\|\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i x_i\big\| < 1$

Printed pp. 1-7Directions on the Sphere
Wind directions, geological orientations, and migration headings are unit vectors on the two-sphere. Their arithmetic average has length below one and points into the interior, off the manifold. This simple failure foreshadows the central lesson that meaningful summaries must respect the curved geometry of the sample space.

Axes and Shapes

Axial data

A direction and its negative are the same: $x \sim -x$.

Projective space

$\mathbb{RP}^{d} = S^{d}/\{\pm 1\}$

Shape data

Configurations equal up to rotation, translation, and scale.

Similarity quotient

$\Sigma_2^k = \big(\text{config}\big)/\big(\text{rot}\times\text{trans}\times\text{scale}\big)$

Printed pp. 1-7Axes and Shapes
Axial data identify a direction with its negative, living in real projective space, while shape data identify configurations differing by rotation, translation, and scale. Both are quotients of a sphere by a group action, producing the manifolds and metric spaces that occupy the remainder of this book.

Shape as a Quotient

Landmarks

$k$ labelled points capture an object's geometry.

Preshape

Center and rescale to a sphere of unit size.

Shape

Quotient the preshape sphere by rotation.

Printed pp. 1-7Shape as a Quotient
Kendall's construction takes k labelled landmarks in the plane, centers and rescales them to a preshape sphere, then quotients by rotation. The resulting shape space is a smooth manifold away from singular configurations, and its non-flat geometry is precisely what makes naive coordinate averaging invalid.

The Euclidean Mean Fails

The chord average

Averaging in the ambient space lands between the points, not on $M$.

Off the manifold

$\bar{x} = \tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i x_i \notin M$

Printed pp. 1-7The Euclidean Mean Fails
Averaging landmark coordinates or unit vectors componentwise generally yields a point that is not a valid shape or not on the sphere. The chord-based Euclidean mean sits off the manifold, so it cannot serve as a population center. This breakdown is the concrete motivation for the Frechet mean.

The Fréchet Mean

Variational definition

The mean is the minimizer of expected squared distance.

Faithful

On $\mathbb{R}^n$ it recovers the ordinary mean.

Definition

$\mu_F = \arg\min_{p\in M}\; \mathbb{E}\,\rho^2(X,p)$

Generality

Needs only a metric $\rho$; works on any metric space.

Printed pp. 1-7The Frechet Mean
The Frechet mean replaces the moment definition of average with a variational one: it minimizes the expected squared distance to a random point. On a metric space this object always makes sense, and on Euclidean space it recovers the ordinary mean, so it is a faithful generalization rather than a substitute.

Extrinsic vs Intrinsic

Extrinsic

Embed, average in $\mathbb{R}^N$, then project back onto $M$.

Extrinsic mean

$\mu_E = P_M\big(\mathbb{E}\,j(X)\big)$

Intrinsic

Use the geodesic distance; coordinate-free and curvature-aware.

Intrinsic mean

$\mu_I = \arg\min_p \mathbb{E}\, d_g^2(X,p)$

Printed pp. 1-7Extrinsic vs Intrinsic
Two distances yield two Frechet means. The extrinsic mean embeds the manifold in a Euclidean space, averages there, and projects back, which is fast and stable. The intrinsic mean uses the geodesic Riemannian distance and is coordinate free. The book develops the theory for both throughout.

Goals of the Theory

Consistency

Sample Fréchet mean converges to the population mean.

Central limit theorem

Asymptotic normality in a tangent chart.

Confidence & tests

Regions and hypothesis tests on $M$.

Consistency

$\hat{\mu}_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu_F$

CLT in the tangent space

$\sqrt{n}\,\log_{\mu}\hat{\mu}_n \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$

Printed pp. 1-7Goals of the Theory
For each notion of mean the book seeks the same statistical triad: consistency of the sample estimator, a central limit theorem describing its fluctuations in a tangent chart, and resulting confidence regions and tests. Establishing these on curved spaces is the technical heart of the nonparametric program.

Two-Sample and Bayes

Two-sample test

Compare Fréchet means of two populations on $M$.

Null hypothesis

$H_0:\; \mu_F^{(1)} = \mu_F^{(2)}$

Nonparametric Bayes

Place a prior directly on densities over the manifold.

Prior on densities

$f \sim \Pi \;\text{ on }\; \mathcal{D}(M)$

Printed pp. 1-7Two-Sample and Bayes
Beyond a single mean, the program extends to comparing two populations through a two-sample test of their Frechet means and to nonparametric Bayesian models that place priors directly on densities over the manifold. These tools answer the applied questions in directional, axial, and shape data analysis.
