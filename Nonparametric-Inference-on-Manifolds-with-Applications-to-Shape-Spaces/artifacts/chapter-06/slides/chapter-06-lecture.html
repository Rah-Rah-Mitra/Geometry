Configurations as Matrices

Encoding

Stack the $k$ landmarks as rows of a $k\times m$ matrix.

Labelling

Row $i$ is the same feature in every specimen — correspondence is fixed.

Why a matrix

Linear algebra then drives every centring and alignment step.

Configuration matrix

$X = \begin{bmatrix} x_1^\top \\ \vdots \\ x_k^\top \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times m}$

Configuration space

The set of all such $X$ before any nuisance is removed.

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96Configurations as Matrices
A configuration of k labelled landmarks in m dimensions is stored as a k by m matrix, one row per point. Labelling matters: landmark i must mean the same anatomical or geometric feature across every specimen, otherwise correspondence is lost and no downstream shape comparison is meaningful.

What Is Shape?

Informal

Shape is the geometry left after discarding location, scale, and orientation.

Same shape

$X$ and $Y$ share a shape if a chosen transformation carries one to the other.

Modelling choice

Which transformations are nuisance decides which shape space results.

Equivalence by a group

$X \sim Y \iff Y = g\cdot X \text{ for some } g\in G$

Examples of $G$

Similarity, similarity with reflection, affine, or projective.

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96What Is Shape?
Shape is the geometric information left after we discard location, scale, and orientation. Two configurations have the same shape when one maps to the other by an allowed transformation. Choosing which transformations count as nuisance is a modelling decision that fixes exactly which shape space we build.

The Quotient Idea

Group action

$G$ acts on configuration space, moving each $X$ within its orbit.

Orbit = one shape

All configurations reachable by $G$ represent the very same shape.

Shape space

The collection of orbits, written as the quotient $\Sigma = C/G$.

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96The Quotient Idea
We let a transformation group act on configuration space and declare two configurations equivalent when they lie in the same orbit. Shape space is the set of orbits, the quotient of configuration space by the group. Each point of the quotient is one shape, represented by any member of its orbit.

Step 1 · Remove Translation

Centring

Subtract the centroid $\bar{x}$ from every landmark.

Result

Centred configurations have rows summing to zero.

Dimension

They form a linear subspace of dimension $(k-1)m$.

Centring operator

$X_c = X - \tfrac{1}{k}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top X, \qquad \mathbf{1}^\top X_c = 0$

Universal

Translation is always quotiented out, whatever group $G$ we pick.

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96Step 1: Remove Translation
Subtracting the centroid from every landmark removes translation and centres the configuration at the origin. The centred configurations form a linear subspace of dimension k minus one times m. This is the first and least controversial quotient, applied universally before scale and rotation are addressed.

Step 2 · Remove Scale

Normalise size

Divide the centred matrix by its Frobenius norm.

Pre-shape

Every specimen now lives on a unit sphere of fixed size.

What remains

Location and scale are gone; only rotation still separates equivalents.

Pre-shape sphere

$Z = \dfrac{X_c}{\lVert X_c\rVert}, \qquad \lVert Z\rVert = 1$

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96Step 2: Remove Scale
Dividing the centred configuration by its Frobenius norm removes uniform scaling and places every specimen on a unit sphere, the pre-shape sphere. Pre-shape retains orientation but has fixed size and location, so only rotation still separates configurations that ought to share one shape.

Step 3 · Remove Rotation

Quotient by $SO(m)$

Identify pre-shapes that differ only by a rotation.

Kendall shape space

The resulting quotient $\Sigma_m^k$ of similarity shapes.

Orbits

Rotational orbits are circles or higher-dimensional sets.

Similarity shape space

$\Sigma_m^k = \big\{\,[Z] : Z \sim ZR,\ R\in SO(m)\,\big\}$

Manifold

Generically smooth, with singular shapes possible in low dimension.

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96Step 3: Remove Rotation
Quotienting the pre-shape sphere by the rotation group collapses each rotational orbit to a single point and yields Kendall's similarity shape space. The orbits are circles or higher dimensional, and the quotient is generically a smooth manifold, although singular shapes can occur in low dimensions.

The Full Pipeline

Configuration → centred → pre-shape → shape

Centre

Removes $m$ translation directions.

Scale

Removes one size direction onto the pre-shape sphere.

Rotate

Removes $\dim SO(m)$ orientation directions.

Choosing the Group

Similarity

Translation, scale, rotation → Kendall shape space.

Reflection

Also quotient reflections → reflection shape space.

Affine

Remove all invertible linear maps → affine shape space.

Projective

Remove projective maps → projective shape space for camera views.

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96Choosing the Group
Different choices of nuisance group give different shape spaces. Similarity uses rotations; reflection shape adds reflections; affine shape removes all invertible linear maps; projective shape removes projective transformations for camera views. The later chapters study each of these quotients and their distinct geometry in detail.

Manifolds and Singularities

Generic case

The quotient is a smooth Riemannian manifold.

Why it matters

Manifold structure is what makes nonparametric inference possible.

Degenerate landmarks

Collinear or coincident points shrink the orbit's stabilizer.

Singular shapes

$\text{Stab}(Z) \neq \{I\} \;\Rightarrow\; [Z] \text{ may be singular}$

Takeaway

Treat shape data as manifold-valued, never plain Euclidean.

Printed pp. 77-81 · PDF pp. 92-96Manifolds and Singularities
For generic landmark counts the shape space is a smooth Riemannian manifold, which is what makes nonparametric inference on it possible. In degenerate cases, such as collinear landmarks, orbits have smaller stabilizers and the quotient acquires singular points where the manifold structure breaks down.
