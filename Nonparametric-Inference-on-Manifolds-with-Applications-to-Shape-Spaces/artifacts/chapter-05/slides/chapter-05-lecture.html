Geodesic Distance

Shortest path

$d_g(p,q)$ is the length of the minimizing geodesic on $(M,g)$.

True metric

It is intrinsic: it depends only on $g$, not on any embedding.

Cut locus

Beyond the cut locus, minimizers stop being unique.

Definition

$d_g(p,q) = \inf_{\gamma}\int_0^1 \sqrt{g_{\gamma}(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)}\,dt$

On the sphere

Great-circle arc length; antipodes sit at distance $\pi$.

Printed pp. 57-76Geodesic Distance
The geodesic distance is the length of the shortest curve joining two points on the manifold, obtained by integrating the Riemannian speed along candidate paths. It respects the curved geometry, unlike the ambient chord. Every intrinsic estimator here is built from this distance, so its locality and cut-locus behaviour matter.

The Intrinsic Frechet (Karcher) Mean

Variance functional

The Frechet function $F(p)=E\,d_g(p,X)^2$ measures squared spread about $p$.

The mean set

Minimizers of $F$ form the intrinsic mean set; a single point is a Karcher mean.

Euclidean limit

When $d_g$ is Euclidean, the minimizer is the ordinary average $E[X]$.

Definition

$\mu = \arg\min_{p\in M}\; E\big[d_g(p,X)^2\big]$

Sample version

$\widehat\mu_n=\arg\min_p \tfrac1n\sum_i d_g(p,X_i)^2$.

Printed pp. 57-76The Intrinsic Frechet Mean
The intrinsic Frechet or Karcher mean minimizes the expected squared geodesic distance to a random point. It generalizes the Euclidean average, where squared distance recovers the ordinary mean. On a curved space the minimizer need not be unique, so existence and uniqueness require the careful hypotheses we develop in the slides that follow.

Exponential and Logarithm Maps

Exp map

$\mathrm{Exp}_p(v)$ travels the geodesic from $p$ with velocity $v$ for unit time.

Log map

$\mathrm{Log}_p(q)$ returns the initial velocity reaching $q$; it inverts $\mathrm{Exp}_p$.

Isometry of length

$\lVert\mathrm{Log}_p(q)\rVert_g = d_g(p,q)$ inside the injectivity radius.

Printed pp. 57-76Exponential and Logarithm Maps
The exponential map shoots a geodesic from a base point along a tangent vector for unit time, while the logarithm map inverts it, recording the initial velocity that reaches a target. Together they pass data between the flat tangent space and the curved manifold, the engine of every intrinsic computation in this chapter.

Tangent-Space Linearization at the Mean

Normal coordinates

At $\mu$, $\mathrm{Log}_\mu$ gives a chart where geodesics through $\mu$ are straight.

Lengths preserved

$\lVert\mathrm{Log}_\mu(X_i)\rVert = d_g(\mu,X_i)$, so spread is read off directly.

Linear algebra

Averaging, covariance, and tests become flat vector operations.

Printed pp. 57-76Tangent-Space Linearization
At the mean, the logarithm map sends each sample to a tangent vector whose length equals its geodesic distance. Because tangent vectors add like ordinary vectors, statistics that are hard on the manifold become linear algebra in normal coordinates centered at the Karcher mean, which is exactly what makes intrinsic inference tractable.

Existence and Uniqueness

Regular ball

Support inside a geodesic ball $B(p,r)$ on which $d_g^2$ stays geodesically convex.

Radius bound

Karcher / Kendall / Le / Afsari: $r$ below the injectivity radius and $\tfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\kappa}}$.

Consequence

$F$ is strictly convex there, so $\mu$ exists and is unique in the ball.

Afsari condition (curvature $\le \kappa$)

$r < r^* = \tfrac12\min\!\Big\{\mathrm{inj}(M),\; \tfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\Big\}$

Reading it

Positive curvature and a far cut locus are exactly what these bounds tame.

Printed pp. 57-76Existence and Uniqueness
Karcher, Kendall, Le, and Afsari give sufficient conditions: if the support lies in a regular geodesic ball whose radius is bounded by the injectivity radius and the inverse square root of the curvature, the squared distance stays convex and the intrinsic mean exists and is unique within that ball.

When Uniqueness Fails

Spread-out data

Leave the regular ball and convexity is lost; minimizers multiply.

Antipodal pair

Two opposite poles on $S^2$ have an entire equator of Frechet means.

Uniform on $S^2$

Every point minimizes $F$ equally; the mean set is the whole sphere.

Printed pp. 57-76When Uniqueness Fails
Spread out the data and uniqueness breaks. The uniform distribution on a sphere has every point as a Frechet minimizer, and an antipodal pair has a whole equatorial circle of means. The cut locus and positive curvature are what destroy the convexity that small regular balls otherwise enjoy.

Computing the Karcher Mean

1. Lift

At current $\mu_k$, compute $v_i=\mathrm{Log}_{\mu_k}(X_i)$ for every sample.

2. Average

Form the tangent mean $\bar v=\tfrac1n\sum_i v_i$ in the flat chart.

3. Retract

Update $\mu_{k+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{\mu_k}(\bar v)$ and repeat until $\bar v\to 0$.

Fixed-point condition

$\sum_i \mathrm{Log}_{\mu}(X_i) = 0$

Why it works

The tangent average is the first-order optimality residual of $F$.

Printed pp. 57-76The Karcher Mean Iteration
We compute the mean by a tangent-space fixed point. Lift every sample through the logarithm at the current estimate, average those tangent vectors, then push the average back with the exponential map. Repeating drives the tangent average to zero, which is the first-order optimality condition for the Karcher mean.

Gradient and Newton Updates

Riemannian gradient

$\mathrm{grad}\,F(p)=-2\,E\,\mathrm{Log}_p(X)$; the tangent average is gradient descent.

Hessian step

Newton uses the Hessian of $d_g^2$, which carries curvature corrections.

Convergence

Linear for the simple iteration, quadratic for Newton near $\mu$.

Gradient iteration

$\mu_{k+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{\mu_k}\!\Big(\tfrac1n\sum_i \mathrm{Log}_{\mu_k}(X_i)\Big)$

Curvature note

The Hessian shrinks in positive curvature, stretches in negative.

Printed pp. 57-76Gradient and Newton Steps
The averaged log vectors are exactly the negative gradient of the Frechet functional, so the iteration is Riemannian gradient descent. Replacing the step with the Hessian of the squared distance, which carries curvature corrections, yields a Newton method that converges quadratically near the mean rather than merely linearly.

Central Limit Theorem for $\widehat\mu_n$

Normal coordinates

Express $\widehat\mu_n$ in the chart $\mathrm{Log}_\mu$ centered at the true mean.

Asymptotic normality

$\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{Log}_\mu(\widehat\mu_n)\Rightarrow N(0,\Sigma)$.

Inference

Confidence regions and bootstrap two-sample tests live in the tangent chart.

Sandwich covariance

$\Sigma = H^{-1}\,\mathrm{Cov}\!\big(\mathrm{Log}_\mu X\big)\,H^{-1}$

$H$

$H$ is the Hessian of $F$ at $\mu$, encoding curvature through the data.

Printed pp. 57-76Central Limit Theorem
In normal coordinates at the population mean, the sample Karcher mean is asymptotically normal. Root-n times its coordinate vector converges to a Gaussian whose covariance is a sandwich involving the Hessian of the Frechet functional, enabling confidence regions and bootstrap two-sample tests directly in the tangent chart.

Intrinsic versus Extrinsic Analysis

Extrinsic

Embed $M$, average in ambient space, project the mean back onto $M$.

Intrinsic

Use the true geodesic metric; coordinate free but needs iteration.

Trade-off

Extrinsic is fast and unique; intrinsic is faithful but needs concentration.

Printed pp. 57-76Intrinsic versus Extrinsic
Extrinsic analysis embeds the manifold and projects the ambient mean back, which is fast and always unique. Intrinsic analysis uses the true geodesic metric and is coordinate free but costs iteration and needs concentration. The two often agree closely for tight data and diverge as the spread of the sample grows.
