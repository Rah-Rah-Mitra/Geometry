Why Density Estimation on a Manifold

The problem

Shape and directional data live on $S^d$ or quotient manifolds, not on $\mathbb{R}^d$.

Goal

A density respecting the metric, allowing many modes, with full uncertainty.

Target

$f : M \to [0,\infty), \quad \int_M f\, d\mu = 1$

Beyond a mean

Estimate the whole density, then read off Frechet mean and modes.

Printed pp. 156-181Why Density Estimation on a Manifold
Shape coordinates and directional data live on spheres or quotient manifolds, not Euclidean space, so ordinary kernel density estimation distorts distances and mass. We want a fully Bayesian density that respects the metric, supports multiple modes, and quantifies uncertainty about the whole density, not just a single Frechet mean.

Location-Concentration Kernels

Two parameters

Location $\mu \in M$ and concentration $\kappa > 0$.

On the sphere

Complex Watson and Bingham kernels peak at $\mu$, tighten as $\kappa$ grows.

Watson kernel

$K(x;\mu,\kappa) = c(\kappa)\, e^{\kappa\, |x^{*}\mu|^{2}}$

Printed pp. 156-181Location-Concentration Kernels
The building block is a parametric kernel indexed by a location on the manifold and a concentration controlling spread. On the sphere the complex Watson and Bingham kernels play this role, peaking at their location parameter and tightening as concentration grows, giving a smooth probability bump anchored to a manifold point.

The Mixture Density

Mixing measure

Integrate the kernel against a measure $P$ on $(\mu,\kappa)$.

Discrete case

A discrete $P$ gives a weighted sum of kernels.

Mixture form

$f_P(x) = \int_M K(x;\mu,\kappa)\, dP(\mu,\kappa)$

Discrete mixture

$f(x) = \sum_{j\ge 1} w_j\, K(x;\mu_j,\kappa_j)$

Printed pp. 156-181The Mixture Density
A flexible density is an integral of the kernel against a mixing measure over location and concentration. With a discrete mixing measure this becomes a weighted sum of kernels, so countably many bumps at different manifold locations build an arbitrarily rich multimodal density that still integrates to one against the manifold volume measure.

Dirichlet Process Prior

Prior over $P$

$P \sim \mathrm{DP}(\alpha, P_0)$ with precision $\alpha$ and base $P_0$.

Properties

Conjugate, almost surely discrete, full weak support.

Finite-dimensional law

$\big(P(A_1),\dots,P(A_k)\big) \sim \mathrm{Dir}\big(\alpha P_0(A_1),\dots\big)$

Role

The canonical nonparametric prior over the mixing distribution.

Printed pp. 156-181Dirichlet Process Prior
We place a Dirichlet process prior on the mixing distribution, parameterized by a precision and a base measure on the parameter space. The Dirichlet process is the canonical nonparametric prior over distributions: it is conjugate under sampling, almost surely discrete, and gives full support over candidate mixing measures.

Stick-Breaking Weights

Sethuraman construction

$w_j = v_j\!\!\prod_{l<j}(1-v_l), \quad v_l \sim \mathrm{Beta}(1,\alpha)$

Atoms

Each weight $w_j$ attaches to $(\mu_j,\kappa_j)\sim P_0$.

Printed pp. 156-181Stick-Breaking Weights
Sethuraman's stick-breaking construction makes the prior explicit. We break a unit stick by independent Beta fractions, producing a decreasing sequence of weights that sum to one, each attached to an atom drawn from the base measure. This shows concretely why Dirichlet process draws are discrete with countably many atoms.

Polya Urn and Clustering

Marginalize $P$

Integrating out the mixing measure gives a prediction rule.

Random clusters

A data-driven number of components emerges.

Chinese restaurant rule

$\theta_{n+1} \mid \theta_{1:n} \sim \frac{1}{\alpha+n}\Big(\alpha P_0 + \sum_{j} n_j\,\delta_{\theta_j^{*}}\Big)$

Reading

Join cluster $j$ with weight $n_j$, or open a new one with weight $\alpha$.

Printed pp. 156-181Polya Urn and Clustering
Marginalizing the mixing measure yields the Polya urn, or Chinese restaurant, prediction rule. A new observation joins an existing cluster with probability proportional to its current size or starts a fresh cluster with probability proportional to the precision, inducing an automatic, data-driven number of mixture components.

Posterior Computation

Collapsed Gibbs

Cycle cluster labels using the Polya urn weights.

Blocked sampler

Truncate stick-breaking, update weights and atoms jointly.

Predictive density

$\hat f(x) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} f_{P^{(t)}}(x)$

Output

Each sweep is one posterior density draw; averaging estimates $f$.

Printed pp. 156-181Posterior Computation
Posterior sampling uses Gibbs and blocked samplers. The Polya urn gives a collapsed Gibbs scheme that cycles cluster labels, while truncated stick-breaking enables blocked samplers updating weights and atoms jointly. Each sweep yields one posterior draw of the density, and averaging draws estimates the predictive density on the manifold.

The Kullback-Leibler Support Condition

Key hypothesis

The truth $f_0$ must lie in the KL support of the prior $\Pi$.

Reach

Continuous, fully supported densities are approximable by mixtures.

KL support

$\Pi\big\{\, f : \textstyle\int f_0 \log\frac{f_0}{f}\, d\mu < \varepsilon \,\big\} > 0$

Why it matters

Without prior mass near $f_0$, the posterior cannot find it.

Printed pp. 156-181Kullback-Leibler Support
Consistency rests on a support condition: the true density must lie in the Kullback-Leibler support of the prior, meaning the prior places positive mass on every neighborhood where the average log-ratio to the truth is small. Continuous, fully supported densities are reached by mixtures, so this condition holds broadly.

Weak Posterior Consistency

Schwartz's theorem

KL support plus a test gives weak consistency.

Conclusion

Posterior mass of every weak neighborhood of $f_0$ tends to one.

Weak consistency

$\Pi\big(\, U_{\text{weak}}(f_0) \mid X_{1:n} \,\big) \to 1 \quad \text{a.s.}$

Printed pp. 156-181Weak Posterior Consistency
Schwartz's theorem delivers weak consistency: if the truth is in the prior's Kullback-Leibler support, the posterior mass of every weak neighborhood of the true density tends to one almost surely as the sample size grows. The kernel mixture prior on the manifold satisfies the hypotheses, so weak consistency holds.

Strong Posterior Consistency

Stronger topology

Upgrade to total variation neighborhoods.

Tool

Sieves with bounded metric entropy and small prior tails.

Strong consistency

$\Pi\big(\, \|f - f_0\|_{1} > \epsilon \mid X_{1:n} \,\big) \to 0 \quad \text{a.s.}$

Payoff

The estimated density converges in total variation distance.

Printed pp. 156-181Strong Posterior Consistency
Strengthening to the total variation, or strong, topology needs entropy control. Combining the Kullback-Leibler support condition with sieves of bounded metric entropy and exponentially small prior tails, the posterior concentrates in strong neighborhoods of the true density, so the estimated density converges in total variation distance.

Frechet Means, Modes, and Clusters

Functionals follow

Density consistency transfers to the Frechet mean and the modes.

Antimodes

Valleys of $\hat f$ separate clusters on the manifold.

Frechet mean

$\hat\mu = \arg\min_{p\in M}\int_M d(p,x)^2\, \hat f(x)\, d\mu(x)$

Classification

Modes label groups; antimodes are the cluster boundaries.

Printed pp. 156-181Estimating Means and Modes
Consistency of the density transfers to functionals. The Frechet mean and the modes computed from the estimated density converge to those of the truth, and antimodes separate clusters. This certifies that Bayesian density estimation also yields consistent location summaries and a principled clustering of the manifold data.
