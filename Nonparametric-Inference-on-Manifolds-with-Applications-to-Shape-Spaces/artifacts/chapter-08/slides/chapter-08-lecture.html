Landmarks as a Complex Vector

Read $\mathbb{R}^2$ as $\mathbb{C}$

Landmark $(x_j,y_j)\mapsto z_j = x_j + i\,y_j$.

Remove translation

Subtract the centroid: $\sum_j z_j = 0$.

Dimension drops

Centered vectors fill a complex hyperplane of dimension $k-1$.

Complex configuration

$z = (z_1,\dots,z_k)\in\mathbb{C}^k, \quad \tilde z_j = z_j - \bar z$

Why complex

One scalar multiplication will encode both scaling and rotation at once.

A planar configuration of k labelled landmarks becomes one complex vector once each point is read as a complex number. Centering by subtracting the centroid removes translation, so the configuration lives in a complex hyperplane of dimension k minus one, the natural arena before scale and rotation are quotiented away.

The Complex Preshape Sphere

Normalize size

Divide by the Hermitian norm $\lVert\tilde z\rVert$.

Land on a sphere

Unit preshapes form $S^{2k-3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{k-1}$.

Preshape

$w = \dfrac{\tilde z}{\lVert\tilde z\rVert}, \qquad \langle w,w\rangle = w^* w = 1$

Dividing the centered complex vector by its Hermitian norm removes scale and lands us on the unit sphere inside complex Euclidean space of dimension k minus one. This complex preshape sphere is the stage on which the rotation group will act by unit-modulus scalar multiplication in the next step.

Rotation Acts as a Single Phase

One angle, one scalar

A rotation by $\theta$ multiplies every landmark by $e^{i\theta}$.

The orbit

$\{e^{i\theta} w : \theta\in[0,2\pi)\}$ is a circle fiber.

Hopf structure

Quotienting the circle is the Hopf fibration $S^1\hookrightarrow S^{2k-3}$.

Group action

$w \;\sim\; e^{i\theta}\,w, \qquad e^{i\theta}\in U(1)$

Printed pp. 87-109 · PDF pp. 102-124Rotation as Complex Phase
In the plane a rotation by an angle multiplies every landmark by a single unit complex number. So the whole rotation orbit of a preshape is the circle of unit scalars times that vector. Quotienting by this circle is precisely the Hopf-style construction behind complex projective space.

Collapse the fibers

Planar shape space

$\Sigma_k^2 \;=\; S^{2k-3}/U(1) \;=\; \mathbb{CP}^{k-2}$

Fubini-Study Geometry

Inherited metric

The round sphere metric descends to $\mathbb{CP}^{k-2}$.

Homogeneous

Constant holomorphic sectional curvature; every shape looks alike.

Fubini-Study metric

$g_{FS}(u,v) = \mathrm{Re}\,\langle u,v\rangle - \mathrm{Re}\langle u,w\rangle\,\mathrm{Re}\langle w,v\rangle$

Horizontal space

Tangent vectors are taken orthogonal to the phase fiber $iw$.

Printed pp. 87-109 · PDF pp. 102-124Fubini-Study Geometry
Complex projective space carries the canonical Fubini-Study Riemannian metric descended from the round sphere through the Riemannian submersion. It makes the planar shape space a homogeneous, constant holomorphic sectional curvature manifold, the geometric backbone for measuring how far apart two planar shapes truly are.

Geodesic = Procrustes Distance

Best alignment

Optimize over rotation, then measure the residual angle.

Hermitian inner product

Distance depends only on $|\langle w_1,w_2\rangle|$.

Full Procrustes distance

$d([w_1],[w_2]) = \arccos\,\big|\langle w_1, w_2\rangle\big|$

Printed pp. 87-109 · PDF pp. 102-124Procrustes Distance
The Fubini-Study geodesic distance between two shapes is the Procrustes distance: the arccosine of the modulus of the Hermitian inner product of unit representatives. It optimally aligns one configuration onto the other over all rotations and reports the residual angular separation between the two complex lines.

Veronese-Whitney Embedding

Map to matrices

Send a shape $[w]$ to the rank-one projection $ww^*$.

Well defined

Phase cancels: $(e^{i\theta}w)(e^{i\theta}w)^* = ww^*$.

Equivariant

Respects rotation and relabelling for extrinsic analysis.

The embedding $j$

$j([w]) = w\,w^* \in \mathcal{H}(k-1), \quad \mathrm{tr}=1,\ \mathrm{rank}=1$

Printed pp. 87-109 · PDF pp. 102-124Veronese-Whitney Embedding
For extrinsic analysis we embed each shape into Hermitian matrices by sending a unit representative z to the rank-one projection z times z conjugate transpose. This map is well defined on lines, equivariant under rotation and relabelling, and isometric enough to carry the Fubini-Study structure faithfully into linear space.

Average in the Embedded Space

Linear average

Average the projection matrices over the sample.

Leaves the image

$\bar J$ is Hermitian but generally not rank one.

Mean Hermitian matrix

$\bar J = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i w_i^{*} \;=\; \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\,ww^*\,]$

Projection needed

Snap $\bar J$ back to the nearest rank-one projection to get a shape.

Printed pp. 87-109 · PDF pp. 102-124The Mean Hermitian Matrix
Averaging the embedded projection matrices over a sample gives a single mean Hermitian matrix in linear space. This Euclidean average generally leaves the embedded image of rank-one projections, so it must be projected back to the nearest rank-one projection to return a legitimate planar shape.

Extrinsic Mean = Top Eigenvector

Nearest projection

Closest rank-one projection to $\bar J$ is its leading eigenprojection.

One eigenproblem

No iteration: solve a single Hermitian eigendecomposition.

Uniqueness

Well defined whenever the top eigenvalue has a gap.

Veronese-Whitney mean

$\hat\mu_E = [\,\mathbf{v}_1\,], \quad \bar J\,\mathbf{v}_1 = \lambda_{\max}\mathbf{v}_1$

Printed pp. 87-109 · PDF pp. 102-124Extrinsic Mean is Top Eigenvector
The closest rank-one projection to the mean Hermitian matrix is the projection onto its leading eigenvector. Hence the extrinsic, or Veronese-Whitney, mean shape is exactly the top eigenvector of the average of z times z conjugate transpose, a fast and unique estimator under a simple eigengap.

Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Means

Intrinsic

Minimizes squared Fubini-Study distance; needs a Karcher iteration.

Extrinsic

One-shot eigenproblem; closed form and globally cheap.

Two CLTs

$\sqrt{n}\,(\hat\mu - \mu) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N(0,\Sigma) \ \text{ in tangent coords}$

Practice

Extrinsic gives cleaner covariances and bootstrap confidence regions.

Printed pp. 87-109 · PDF pp. 102-124Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Means
The intrinsic mean minimizes squared Fubini-Study distance and needs iteration, while the extrinsic mean solves a one-shot eigenproblem. Both admit central limit theorems in tangent coordinates, but the extrinsic version yields cleaner asymptotic covariances and the bootstrap confidence regions used in real practice.
