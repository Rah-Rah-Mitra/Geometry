Why Affine Invariance?

Affine camera

A planar scene viewed at a distance maps by $x \mapsto Ax + b$.

Nuisance

Shear, scale, rotation, and shift differ across views.

Goal

Keep only what every affine image shares.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161Why Affine Invariance
An affine camera maps a flat scene by an invertible linear map plus a shift, so the same object yields sheared and scaled landmark sets across views. Affine shape discards exactly that nuisance, retaining only the structure shared by every affine image of the configuration in question.

Configurations and Centering

Raw data

$k$ labelled landmarks in $\mathbb{R}^m$ stacked as a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times m}$.

Remove translation

Subtract the centroid; rows now sum to zero.

Centering

$$\tilde{X} = X - \mathbf{1}\,\bar{x}^{\top}, \qquad \mathbf{1}^{\top}\tilde{X} = 0$$

Centered space

$\tilde{X}$ lives in a $(k-1)$-dimensional coordinate space per axis.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161Configurations and Centering
We start from k labelled landmarks in real m space, stacked as a matrix. Subtracting the centroid removes translation and lands us in the centered subspace, the first quotient step before the general linear group acts on the remaining centered coordinate columns of the landmark matrix.

The Affine Group Action

Full group

Translation $b$ together with every invertible $A \in GL(m)$.

After centering

$A$ acts on the right of $\tilde{X}$, mixing coordinate columns.

Equivalence

$$\tilde{X} \sim \tilde{X} A, \qquad A \in GL(m)$$

Orbit

The affine shape is the entire orbit under this right action.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161The Affine Group Action
Translation plus the general linear group acts on landmark matrices. After centering, an invertible linear map acts on the right of the centered coordinate matrix, mixing its columns. Two configurations share an affine shape precisely when one centered matrix equals the other times such an invertible matrix.

Affine Shape as a Subspace

Invariant

Right multiplication by $GL(m)$ never changes the column span.

General position

Full rank $m$ keeps the span genuinely $m$-dimensional.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161Affine Shape as a Subspace
Right multiplication by invertible matrices never changes the column span of the centered configuration. The orbit is therefore captured exactly by that span, an m dimensional subspace of the centered coordinate space, provided the landmarks are in general position so that the rank stays full and well defined.

The Grassmann Identification

Identification

$A\Sigma_k^m$ is the set of $m$-planes in $\mathbb{R}^{k-1}$.

Manifold

A compact Grassmannian with smooth, symmetric geometry.

Affine shape space

$$A\Sigma_k^m \;\cong\; G_m(k-1)$$

Dimension

$\dim G_m(k-1) = m\,(k-1-m)$.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161The Grassmann Identification
Collecting all such spans identifies the affine shape space with a Grassmann manifold of m dimensional subspaces inside a k minus one dimensional space. This compact manifold inherits a clean geometry, turning affine shape analysis into honest statistics on a Grassmannian rather than on raw coordinates.

Projection-Matrix Embedding

Represent a plane

By the orthogonal projection onto it, using any orthonormal basis $U$.

Equivariant

The embedding commutes with the orthogonal group action.

Embedding

$$P = U U^{\top}, \quad P^{\top}=P, \quad P^2=P, \quad \operatorname{tr}P = m$$

Why it helps

$P$ is basis-free, so averaging is well defined.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161Projection-Matrix Embedding
Each subspace is represented uniquely by the orthogonal projection onto it, the matrix P equal to U times U transpose for any orthonormal basis U. This equivariant embedding into symmetric matrices is the bridge that makes extrinsic mean computation a linear-algebra exercise on averaged projection matrices.

The Extrinsic Mean

Average

Average projection matrices in ambient Euclidean space.

Project back

Take the top $m$ eigenvectors of $\bar{P}$.

Recipe

$$\bar{P} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j} P_j, \quad \hat{\mu} = \mathrm{span}\!\big(\text{top-}m\ \text{eigvecs of }\bar{P}\big)$$

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161Extrinsic Mean
Embed each affine shape as its projection matrix, average those matrices in the ambient Euclidean space, then project the average back. The extrinsic mean is the subspace spanned by the dominant eigenvectors of the mean projection, the directions carrying the most shared affine structure across the sample.

Existence and Uniqueness

Condition

A spectral gap separates the top $m$ eigenvalues of $\bar{P}$.

Consequence

The dominant eigenspace, hence $\hat{\mu}$, is uniquely determined.

Uniqueness criterion

$$\lambda_m(\bar{P}) > \lambda_{m+1}(\bar{P})$$

Failure mode

Degenerate eigenvalues blur the mean subspace.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161Existence and Uniqueness
The extrinsic mean exists and is unique when the average projection matrix has a clear eigenvalue gap separating its top m eigenvalues from the rest. A spectral gap guarantees a well defined dominant eigenspace, so the projection back to the Grassmannian becomes single valued and stable.

CLT and Two-Sample Tests

Tangent CLT

The sample mean is asymptotically Gaussian in the tangent space at $\mu$.

Inference

Confidence regions and tests come from the asymptotic covariance.

Asymptotics

$$\sqrt{n}\,\big(\hat{\mu}-\mu\big) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)$$

Two-sample

Compare affine mean shapes across groups, model-free.

Printed pp. 135-146 · PDF 150-161CLT and Two-Sample Tests
Under the embedding the sample extrinsic mean obeys a central limit theorem in the tangent space at the population mean. Its asymptotic covariance powers confidence regions and two sample tests that compare affine mean shapes between groups without assuming any parametric model for the landmarks.
