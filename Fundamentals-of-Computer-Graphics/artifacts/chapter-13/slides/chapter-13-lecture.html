Learning arc

A Sample Is A Measured Geometric Claim

1. Name The Measure

  • Area, weighted area, solid angle, or line measure.
  • Zero-measure sets should not distort the calculation.
  • Fairness means invariance under the right motions.

2. Build The Warp

  • Inverse CDF for one-dimensional and separable densities.
  • Rejection and Metropolis for difficult targets.
  • Geometric maps for disks, spheres, and hemispheres.

3. Audit The Samples

  • Probability density integrates to one.
  • Empirical CDF and marginal checks match theory.
  • Estimator variance reveals poor density choices.
measure + density + warp + estimator = debuggable sampling
Use the map to name the three layers that recur all hour. First we choose a measure on a geometric domain. Then we build a sampler from canonical uniform numbers. Finally we test the resulting points by variance, coverage, or a distribution diagnostic from the notebook.
Section 13.1

Measures Tell The Integral What Counts As Size

Measure Rules

Empty sets have zero measure, and disjoint pieces add. These two rules make size compatible with integration.

$$d\nu(x)=w(x)\,d\mu(x),\qquad w(x)\ge 0$$

Average With Respect To A Measure

The denominator matters: changing the measure changes which parts of the domain dominate the average.

Slow down on the word measure, because it is the bookkeeping that tells an integral what counts as size. The same square can be counted uniformly by area or with a weight that favors the far corner. Sampling errors often begin when this measure is left implicit.
Integral geometry

Fair Lines Need A Measure On Line Space

Bad Coordinates Can Bias Lines

Slope-intercept coordinates overweight some bundles after rotation or translation.

Normal Coordinates

A line becomes \((\theta,r)\) through \(x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta=r\), and the natural differential is \(dr\,d\theta\).

$$d\mu_{\mathrm{line}}=dr\,d\theta$$
Lines are the first place where ordinary image coordinates become misleading. Slope-intercept coordinates do not treat rotations fairly. Normal coordinates give a cleaner picture: a line becomes a point in angle-distance space, and the fair differential behaves like a flat area element there.
Continuous probability

A Density Is A Normalized Weight Against A Measure

Density Conditions

\(p(x)\ge 0\) everywhere and \(\int_S p(x)\,d\mu(x)=1\).

$$\Pr(X\in A)=\int_A p(x)\,d\mu(x)$$

Expected Value

The average of \(f(X)\) is the integral of \(f\) weighted by the density.

Connect density to measure before writing any estimator. A probability density is not just a curve; it is a nonnegative weighting whose total measure is one. The shaded interval is the probability of landing there, and expectation is the same integral with a function attached.
Estimator mechanics

Importance Sampling Reweights By The Chosen Density

$$\int_S g(x)\,d\mu(x)\approx {1\over N}\sum_{i=1}^N {g(X_i)\over p(X_i)}$$

Variance Target

Choose \(p\) with the same shape as \(g\), so the ratio \(g/p\) becomes flatter.

Notebook Diagnostic

For \(\int_0^4 x\,dx=8\), the N=512 uniform RMSE is 0.196, while matched importance is constant.

This is the estimator slide. When samples come from density p, each sample must be reweighted by one over p. Good importance sampling makes g divided by p almost flat, so the average stops swinging. Linearity of expectation explains why the average remains unbiased.
Section 13.4.1

Inverse CDF Sampling Bends Uniform Numbers Into A Density

$$U\sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1),\qquad X=P^{-1}(U)$$

One-Dimensional Recipe

Integrate the density into a cumulative distribution, then invert it analytically or numerically.

Check

If \(X\) is correct, then \(P(X)\) behaves like the original uniform samples.

Inverse CDF sampling is the cleanest construction because it turns a uniform number into a target random variable through a monotone warp. Regions with high density get stretched in sample space. If applying the target CDF to warped samples looks uniform, the warp is doing its job.
Disk sampling

Uniform Radius Is Not Uniform Area

$$\phi=2\pi u_1,\qquad r=R\sqrt{u_2}$$

Common Mistake

Using \(r=Ru\) places too much probability near the center because annuli grow with radius.

Notebook Check

The inner half-radius probability is about 0.25 for the square-root warp, but about 0.50 for uniform radius.

The disk example is a useful trap. Uniform radius sounds plausible, but polar area includes an extra factor of r. The square-root radius is what equalizes annular area. Ask students to compare the left and right scatters by looking for excess center mass.
Section 13.4.2

Rejection Sampling Trades Simplicity For Wasted Proposals

Algorithm

Sample from an easy enclosing distribution, test membership or height, and repeat until a point is accepted.

Cost

Disk proposals accept with probability near \(\pi/4\). Sphere-in-cube proposals accept near \(\pi/6\).

Stratification Warning

The keep-or-discard step breaks the regularity of a carefully stratified input pattern.

Rejection sampling is attractive because the implementation is almost embarrassingly simple. Propose in a box or cube, keep the points that land in the target, and discard the rest. The cost is wasted proposals and the loss of any carefully arranged stratified pattern.
Section 13.4.3

Metropolis Sampling Follows A Density By Local Mutations

$$a(x\to y)=\min\left(1,{f(y)t(y\to x)\over f(x)t(x\to y)}\right)$$

Strength

The target density only needs to be evaluated up to a proportional scale.

Price

Samples are correlated, and early steps may need to be discarded as burn-in.

Metropolis should be framed as a fallback for target shapes that can be evaluated but not sampled directly. The chain mutates a current point and balances flow between states through acceptance probabilities. Its strength is flexibility; its price is correlation and burn-in judgment.
Coverage diagnostics

Stratification Makes Coverage A Design Choice

Coverage Before Warp

Start with a better pattern in the unit square, then map it through the sampling warp.

Variance Evidence

At N=512, the notebook reports stratified RMSE 0.00039 for the test integral, far below iid uniform.

Where It Matters

Pixel, lens, time, disk, and direction samples all benefit when the preimage is well covered.

Stratification attacks coverage before the geometric warp is applied. Random points can leave cells empty even when the count is high. Jittered and low-discrepancy patterns deliberately cover the square, which is why they are valuable for pixels, lenses, disks, and direction samples.
Direction sampling

Spheres And Hemispheres Are Solid-Angle Domains

$$\phi=2\pi u_1,\qquad z=1-2u_2,\qquad r=\sqrt{1-z^2}$$

Uniform Sphere

Choose \(z\) uniformly on \([-1,1]\), not \(\theta\) uniformly in polar angle.

Uniform Hemisphere

Use \(z=u\) on \([0,1]\), then set the radial component in the tangent plane.

For directions, the surface measure is the important part. Uniform sphere sampling makes z uniform on negative one to one, while a uniform hemisphere makes z uniform on zero to one. The formulas are short because the solid-angle measure has already been accounted for.
Rendering integral

Cosine-Weighted Hemispheres Flatten Diffuse Irradiance

$$p(\omega)={n+1\over 2\pi}z^n,\qquad z=(1-u)^{1/(n+1)}$$

Diffuse Case

For \(n=1\), \(p(\omega)=z/\pi\). The integrand \(z\) divided by \(z/\pi\) is constant.

Lab Result

The chapter lab estimates \(I=\int_H z\,d\omega=\pi\); cosine weighting has zero RMSE in exact arithmetic.

Cosine-weighted hemisphere sampling is the rendering payoff. A diffuse irradiance integrand contains the cosine z, so using density z over pi makes every contribution equal to pi. The slide shows why the notebook reports zero variance for the exact cosine estimator.
Section 13.4.4

Random Lines In A Square Are Sampled In Normal Space

$$p(\theta)={r_{\max}(\theta)\over \int r_{\max}(\theta)\,d\theta},\qquad r=u\,r_{\max}(\theta)$$

Design Principle

Sample the region of \((\theta,r)\) values whose lines hit the unit square, not an arbitrary slope box.

Notebook Check

The normal-coordinate hitting region has area 4, and every sampled line clips the square.

Return to lines as a full sampler design problem. In normal coordinates, lines hitting the unit square occupy a bounded region under r max of theta. Sampling theta with density proportional to that boundary and then choosing r uniformly below it preserves the line measure.
