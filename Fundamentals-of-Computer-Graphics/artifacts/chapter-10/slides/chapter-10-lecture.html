Fundamentals of Computer Graphics

Chapter 10: Signal Processing

A graphics system stores finite arrays, but the world of images, sound, texture, motion, and light is modeled by functions. This lecture is about what survives sampling, what is lost, and what filtering can honestly repair.

Open by naming the central tension of the chapter: graphics code stores arrays, but the visual quantities we care about are functions of time, position, direction, or surface coordinates. The lecture should make sampling feel like an engineering contract rather than a file format. Every later image operation will ask which frequencies, averages, and invariants survived the conversion to samples.
Representations

Sampling Turns Continuous Questions Into Indexed Data

Common graphics signals
  • Audio-like time signal: \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\)
  • Image irradiance or color: \(I:\mathbb{R}^2\to C\)
  • Volume density or signed distance: \(v:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}\)
  • Texture, motion, shadow, and display pipelines all reuse the same logic.
The trap

The plotted curve is not the stored signal. Samples are measurements at selected lattice points; anything between those points is a reconstruction choice.

Samples are data; reconstruction is an interpretation.2 / 18
Use this slide to separate mathematical type from storage type. An image array is familiar, but it is only one example of a sampled function. Emphasize that the same ideas apply to digitizer motion, volume data, texture maps, shadow maps, and displays. The warning matters because students often judge an algorithm by a plotted line even though the algorithm only received discrete measurements.
Sampling

Aliasing: One Sample Set Can Fit Two Stories

Notebook invariant

Sampling a 7 cycle signal at 10 samples per unit gives the same samples as a 3 cycle signal.

\(f_{\text{alias}}=\left| f-\operatorname{round}(f/f_s)f_s \right|\)
Engineering consequence

After sampling, no later filter can know whether the high-frequency or low-frequency explanation was the original.

Aliasing is ambiguity created by undersampling.3 / 18
Spend time on the word story. The samples are identical, so the distinction between the two continuous curves is no longer present in the data. This is stronger than saying the reconstruction looks bad. It says the renderer has destroyed information before reconstruction begins. The practical fix is to avoid admitting frequencies that the sampling lattice cannot represent.
Reconstruction

Reconstruction Filters Decide What Happens Between Samples

Discrete-continuous idea

Each sample contributes a shifted copy of a continuous filter. The reconstructed value is their weighted sum.

\(r(x)=\sum_i a_i\,h(x-i)\)
What changes
  • Box filters preserve constant plateaus but create steps.
  • Tent filters are continuous but still cannot recover lost high frequencies.
  • Wider filters may reduce alias leakage at the cost of blur or ringing.
The same samples can produce many reconstructed functions.4 / 18
After aliasing, this slide makes a second distinction: even when the samples are accepted as the data, a renderer still has to define values between them. Zero-order hold, linear reconstruction, cubic filters, and windowed sinc filters all answer the same question differently. Remind students that smoother is not automatically more correct if the missing frequency content was never sampled.
Convolution

One Output Sample Is A Weighted Local Average

Discrete formula
\((a*b)_i=\sum_j a_{i-j}b_j\)

The reversed index is what makes the operation line up with the algebra of shifted filters.

Normalization check

If the kernel weights sum to one, constant signals stay constant. This is the brightness-preservation test for many image filters.

Convolution turns local neighborhoods into one filtered value.5 / 18
Introduce convolution first as something concrete: place the filter over nearby samples, multiply, and add. The flipped index can be acknowledged without making it the whole lecture. The essential instructor point is the invariant. A blur kernel whose weights sum to one should not darken a constant image, and that simple check catches many implementation mistakes.
Algebra

Convolution Also Means A Sum Of Shifted Copies

Useful identities
  • Commutative: \(a*b=b*a\)
  • Associative: \((a*b)*c=a*(b*c)\)
  • Distributive over addition
  • Impulse identity: \(a*\delta=a\)
Why graphics cares

Repeated filters can sometimes be combined, sharpening can be written as an identity minus a blur, and separable 2D filters save work because of associativity.

The shifted-copy view explains reconstruction and splines.6 / 18
Switch viewpoints deliberately. The local average view explains how to compute one output; the shifted-copy view explains the whole output and prepares students for reconstruction filters. This is a good moment to connect algebra to debugging. If a convolution implementation is not commutative, associative in the tested cases, or identity-preserving with an impulse, there is likely an indexing or boundary mistake.
Bridge

Discrete-Continuous Convolution Reconstructs A Function

Reconstruction recipe

Start with a sequence \(a_i\). Center a continuous basis or filter \(h\) at every integer location. Add the shifted copies with sample values as coefficients.

Spline connection

Uniform splines use exactly this structure: coefficients multiply shifted basis functions, and the basis smoothness becomes the reconstructed curve smoothness.

Reconstruction is convolution between a sequence and a continuous filter.7 / 18
Use this as the conceptual bridge from arrays to curves and images. Students should see that reconstruction is not magic interpolation between neighboring points. It is a sum of continuous basis functions. The filter support controls how many samples participate at each position, while the filter smoothness controls the regularity of the reconstructed function.
Filter family

Useful Filters Trade Cost, Smoothness, Interpolation, And Ringing

Cheap and local

Box is discontinuous and produces blocky reconstruction. Tent is continuous, interpolating, and ripple-free for unit-spaced samples.

Smooth averages

Gaussian has smooth frequency decay, good for prefiltering. B-spline is smooth and local, but it does not pass exactly through samples.

Sharper but riskier

Catmull-Rom, Mitchell-Netravali, and Lanczos can preserve more apparent sharpness, but negative lobes can produce ringing near edges.

Filter shape predicts visual artifacts.8 / 18
Do not present the gallery as a ranking. Each filter answers a different design pressure. Box and tent are simple and cheap. Gaussian filtering is an excellent way to reduce high frequencies before sampling. Cubic and windowed-sinc families can look sharper, but their negative lobes make ringing a predictable risk. The goal is to read the artifact from the filter shape.
Filter diagnostics

Five Properties Make A Filter Auditable

Core checks
  1. Area or sum equals one for constant preservation.
  2. Integer samples decide whether the filter interpolates.
  3. Support controls runtime and boundary behavior.
  4. Smoothness controls continuity of the reconstruction.
  5. Negative lobes predict overshoot and ringing.
Ripple-free test

A reconstruction filter is ripple-free when shifted copies sum to one at every fractional offset.

Tie this slide directly to the notebook ledger. A filter diagram alone is not enough; students should ask how it behaves on constants, at integer locations, at boundaries, and near discontinuities. The ripple-free condition is especially useful because it turns an abstract reconstruction property into a concrete sum over shifted filters.
Images

Images Use The Same Convolution Algebra In Two Dimensions

2D formula
\((I*K)_{i,j}=\sum_m\sum_n I_{i-m,j-n}K_{m,n}\)
Performance pattern

If \(K(x,y)=p(x)q(y)\), apply one 1D filter across rows and one across columns. A large radius changes from area-like work to two line-like passes.

Separable filtering is algebra turned into performance.10 / 18
Now move from signals to images without changing the mental model. A 2D convolution still multiplies a neighborhood by weights and sums. The separability point is worth emphasizing because it shows why mathematical structure matters in production code. A Gaussian-like blur can be dramatically cheaper when implemented as horizontal and vertical passes.
Image operations

Blur, Sharpen, And Shadows Are Filtered Signals

Blur

Low-pass filtering reduces local variation. The notebook checks this by measuring that blur lowers image variance.

Sharpen

Unsharp masking adds high-pass detail back to the original: smooth first, subtract, then add a scaled detail image.

Drop shadow

Shift a mask, blur it, and composite. The same convolution language handles the soft edge.

Image effects are often small convolution programs.11 / 18
This slide should make image processing feel less like a list of software features and more like reusable signal algebra. Blurring, sharpening, and shadows differ in purpose, but each can be described by filtering, adding, shifting, or compositing filtered signals. Point out that the notebook uses a synthetic image so visual observations can be paired with measurable variance and high-pass energy checks.
Antialiasing

Before Image Sampling, High Frequencies Must Be Reduced

Aliasing in images

Regular textures can beat against the sample grid, producing moire patterns. Sharp edges become stair steps when a binary decision is made at one point per pixel.

Sampling filter

Antialiasing is prefiltering: average the continuous image over the pixel footprint before writing a sample.

Antialiasing trades sharpness for representability.12 / 18
Make the prefiltering order explicit. Antialiasing is not a cosmetic blur added after the wrong samples have already been chosen. The filter must be part of the sampling process so the stored value represents an average over a footprint. This slide also prepares students for texture filtering, where the footprint may be much larger or more elongated than one pixel.
Resampling

Resizing Combines Reconstruction And Sampling Into One Filter

Conceptual operation

First reconstruct a continuous image from the input grid. Then sample that function on the output grid. In code, the two filters are collapsed into one resampling filter.

When shrinking

The output grid is coarser, so the filter footprint must widen. Pixel dropping and unscaled reconstruction filters keep high frequencies that the new grid cannot represent.

\(g*(f*a)=(g*f)*a\)
Downsampling requires a filter scaled to the new spacing.13 / 18
Frame resizing as a sampling problem, not as a pixel-selection problem. The input samples define a reconstructed function, and the output pixels sample that function. When the target has fewer samples, the filter must become wider to remove frequencies that would alias. This explains why nearest neighbor can look crisp while being spectrally wrong.
Frequency domain

The Fourier Transform Makes Filtering Easier To Reason About

Convolution theorem
\(\mathcal{F}\{f*g\}=\widehat f\,\widehat g\)

Filtering by convolution in space is multiplication by a response curve in frequency.

What the spectrum shows
  • DC is the average component.
  • High frequencies encode rapid variation and sharp edges.
  • Low-pass filters reduce spectral width before sampling.
Frequency response predicts which details survive a filter.14 / 18
Introduce the Fourier view as a diagnostic tool, not as a new topic disconnected from filtering. In space, convolution is a weighted average and can be visually local. In frequency, the same operation is multiplication by the filter response. That makes it much easier to explain why a Gaussian damps high frequencies smoothly and why poor reconstruction filters leak alias spectra.
Sampling theory

Sampling Repeats The Spectrum; Aliasing Is Overlap

Frequency-domain picture

Multiplying by an impulse train in space creates shifted copies of the spectrum. If the copies overlap the base band, different frequencies become mixed in the samples.

Nyquist criterion

To reconstruct a bandlimited signal exactly in principle, the highest frequency must be below half the sample frequency.

\(f_{\max} < f_s/2\)
Nyquist is a spacing rule for spectrum copies.15 / 18
This is the key sampling-theory slide. Ask students to look for overlap, not for individual waves. Sampling creates periodic copies of the spectrum, and the sample rate controls the spacing between those copies. Once overlap occurs, the mixture is built into the stored samples. Low-pass filtering before sampling narrows the spectrum so the copies interfere less.
Filter response

Good Sampling And Reconstruction Filters Shape Frequency Content

Sampling filter job

Narrow the spectrum before samples are written, so shifted copies overlap less.

Reconstruction filter job

Preserve the base spectrum while attenuating alias copies introduced by the sampled representation.

Ideal versus practical

The sinc idea is ideal in frequency, but infinite support and ringing make practical windowed or smoother filters preferable in many graphics settings.

Filter response is the spectrum-side contract.16 / 18
Compare the response curves qualitatively. The box filter has broad side lobes, so it leaves visible reconstruction artifacts. The tent response rolls off more strongly because it is a box convolved with itself. Gaussian response falls smoothly, which is why it is attractive for prefiltering. Then make the practical point: ideal frequency behavior is not the same as ideal image appearance or runtime.

Recap

Signal Processing Gives Graphics A Sampling Discipline

What to remember
  • Samples are measurements, not the original function.
  • Aliasing is information from different frequencies mixed into one sample set.
  • Convolution is the shared language for filtering, reconstruction, and image effects.
  • Filter shape predicts blur, ripple, continuity, and ringing.
Practical rule

When a signal changes sample rate, ask what frequency content the new lattice can represent, then choose a normalized filter whose support and response match that task.
