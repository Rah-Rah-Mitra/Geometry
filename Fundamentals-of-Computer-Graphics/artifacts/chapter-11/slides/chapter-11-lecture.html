Chapter 11Texture Mapping
Open the lecture by separating the informal phrase put the image on the object from the computation that actually happens. The renderer starts at a visible surface point, evaluates a texture coordinate function, filters the texture signal, and only then uses that value in shading or geometry.
Chapter map

The Two Problems Behind Every Texture

Problem 1

Choose a coordinate function

A surface point must map to a location in texture space. The map should control distortion, avoid accidental many-to-one behavior, and place seams where they are least visible.

Problem 2

Sample without aliasing

A pixel covers an area in texture space, not an infinitesimal point. The lookup must interpolate when magnifying and average when minifying.

Everything else in the chapter is an application of these two mechanisms: texture coordinates plus filtered texture lookup.

Looking up texture values

The Map Points Backward from the Rendered Point

Shader routine
p = visible_surface_point uv = surface.texture_coordinates(p) sample = filter(texture, uv, footprint) shade(material, sample, normal, light)

Common mistake

Thinking in the forward direction, as if texels push themselves onto the object, hides the real dependency. The shader pulls texture data from the location requested by the surface point.

φ: S → T,   p = (x,y,z) ↦ (u,v)
Texture lookupSurface point to texture coordinate
Emphasize the direction of dependency in a shader. The visible point is already known from ray tracing or rasterization, so texture mapping asks the surface where to sample. This mental model helps students debug wrong wrap modes, wrong transforms, and inverted coordinate conventions.
Texture coordinate functions

Parameterization Is a Geometric Compromise

Bijectivity

Avoid accidental reuse of one texture point on unrelated surface points.

Area scale

Keep texel density roughly constant where the material should look uniform.

Shape scale

Keep small circles from becoming extreme ellipses in texture space.

Continuity

Minimize seams, then place unavoidable seams where artifacts are least costly.

Coordinate families in the chapter

  • Planar maps are cheap and natural for mostly flat patches, but they collapse front and back on closed surfaces.
  • Spherical and cylindrical maps use angles, making seams and pole behavior visible.
  • Cubemaps trade six seams for lower angular distortion and cheaper directional lookup.
  • Mesh UVs store the coordinate function at vertices and interpolate it across triangles.
Coordinate designDistortion, scale, shape, continuity
Use this slide to connect texture mapping to cartography. The renderer needs a practical coordinate function, but the map is never free: injectivity, area scale, local shape, and continuity compete. The examples are deliberately schematic so students focus on tradeoffs rather than on a specific asset.
Interpolated texture coordinates

Triangles Carry UV Values at Their Vertices

What the atlas view reveals

  • Each triangle in the surface has a partner triangle in texture space.
  • Shared mesh vertices force neighboring triangles to agree along their common edge.
  • Overlapping atlas triangles intentionally or accidentally reuse the same texture region.
  • Large differences between surface area and atlas area become visible texture density changes.

A UV atlas is not just a storage format. It is a visualization of the function that the shader will evaluate for every fragment.

Barycentric interpolationMesh UVs define the surface-to-texture map
Explain that mesh UVs turn a global mapping problem into many small affine maps. Each triangle can be simple, while the atlas layout decides where seams, overlaps, and density changes occur. Barycentric interpolation is familiar from rasterization, but here it interpolates coordinates used to fetch another signal.
Seams, wrapping modes, and transforms

Continuity in 3D Can Be Discontinuity in UV Space

Three extension tools

Repeat

Use the fractional part of u and v so the texture tiles.

Clamp

Extend boundary values when the lookup leaves the unit square.

Constant

Return a background value outside the intended patch.

[u', v', 1]T = MT [u, v, 1]T

A texture transform moves, scales, or rotates the lookup domain without changing the mesh.

Seam handlingRepeat, clamp, constant, and UV transforms
Walk through the seam example slowly. The two end points are close on the model but far apart in the atlas if the coordinates are interpreted literally. Duplicating vertices lets neighboring triangles choose equivalent coordinates, while wrapping modes define what texture values mean outside the unit square.
Projected triangles

Screen-Linear UVs Are Wrong When Depth Varies

The rasterizer interpolates after projection

Attributes that are linear on a 3D triangle are not generally linear in screen coordinates after the homogeneous divide. Correct texture interpolation carries reciprocal depth along with the UVs.

u = (sum λi ui / wi) / (sum λi / wi)
v = (sum λi vi / wi) / (sum λi / wi)

Naive affine interpolation makes checkerboards swim or bend on receding surfaces because it ignores projective depth.

Perspective-correct interpolationInterpolate u/w, v/w, and 1/w
Tie this formula back to the graphics pipeline chapter. Barycentric weights are computed in screen space, but the original triangle lived in homogeneous coordinates. Interpolating u over w, v over w, and one over w is the repair that makes texture features track the projected 3D surface.
Antialiasing texture lookups

A Pixel Becomes a Footprint in Texture Space

x = ∂(u,v)/∂x Jy = ∂(u,v)/∂y

Filtering regimes

Magnification: footprint is smaller than a texel. Reconstruct between texels, usually with bilinear interpolation.

Minification: footprint covers many texels. Average over an area before sampling to avoid aliasing.

Shape: the Jacobian columns set footprint size and anisotropy. Oblique views produce long, thin regions.

Pixel footprintψ = φ composed with inverse projection
Make the key shift from point samples to areas. The local derivative of the image-to-texture mapping tells the renderer what texture region contributes to one image sample. Small footprints call for reconstruction; large footprints call for prefiltered averages; elongated footprints explain why simple mipmaps blur at grazing angles.
Mipmapping

Precompute Averages, Then Choose a Level

x||, ||Jy||),   k = log2(D)

What mipmaps buy

  • A pyramid stores successively larger square averages.
  • Trilinear sampling blends between two adjacent levels to reduce popping.
  • The level choice is usually driven by a footprint width estimate.
  • Square averages are fast and stable, but not ideal for long, thin footprints.

The chapter notebook checks that the pyramid preserves mean color and reduces high-frequency energy in far-field views.

Mipmap pyramidFast minification by prefiltering
Present mipmaps as a memory-for-time tradeoff. Instead of averaging thousands of texels during a lookup, the renderer stores averages at powers of two and samples an appropriate level. The approximation is excellent for roughly square footprints and visibly over-blurry for strongly elongated ones.
Beyond square footprints

Anisotropic Filtering Samples Along the Long Axis

Why the floor still matters

A receding floor often maps one pixel to a long strip in texture space. A square mip lookup must cover the strip's longest width, so it includes many texels that do not belong to the footprint.

Anisotropic filtering chooses a level based on the short axis, then averages multiple samples placed along the long axis.

The cost is more texture lookups; the gain is less directional blur and fewer shimmering artifacts during motion.

AnisotropyLong footprints need directional averaging
Use the diagram to contrast what the mipmap approximation does with what the footprint actually asks for. The dashed square is easy to sample but includes too much irrelevant texture. Multiple samples along the long axis approximate the same area with better directional selectivity.
Applications of texture mapping

A Texture Can Store Any Shading Parameter

Color is only the first use case

Once a filtered lookup is available, the sample can feed almost any material input. The important design question becomes what the numbers mean, not whether the storage is an image.

Diffuse

Base color or albedo used by the shading model.

Roughness

Surface microstructure controls highlight width.

Opacity

Alpha masks cut or blend surface visibility.

material(p) = decode(filter(texture, φ(p)))
Material mapsTextures as parameter fields
After students understand lookup and filtering, broaden the meaning of texture. A texture may store colors, scalar coefficients, masks, or packed channels. The shader must decode the sampled values consistently, and the same filtering issues still apply because the texture is still a sampled signal.
Shape detail

Normals Change Lighting; Displacement Changes Geometry

Normal map

Stores normals directly, often relative to the local tangent frame. Great for high-frequency shading detail.

Bump map

Stores scalar height. The shader differentiates the height field to perturb the shading normal.

Displacement map

Moves tessellated geometry. Silhouettes, parallax, and intersections can change because the surface itself changes.

The same height signal can support a shading trick or a geometric operation; the renderer must choose which consequence is intended.

Normal, bump, displacementShading detail versus actual surface detail
Separate the three techniques by their observable consequences. Normal and bump maps alter lighting normals while the underlying surface remains smooth, so silhouettes stay unchanged. Displacement maps move geometry after tessellation, which is more expensive but changes shape, parallax, and intersections in a way shading tricks cannot.
Depth textures from the light

A Shadow Map Stores the Closest Light-Space Depth

map < bias; PCF averages binary tests

Shadow-map pipeline

  • Render from the light and record nearest depth in a texture.
  • Project the camera fragment into the light's texture coordinates.
  • Compare its light-space depth with the stored depth.
  • Use bias to avoid self-shadowing and PCF to soften jagged boundaries.

Bias is a tolerance, not a lighting model. Too little creates acne; too much detaches contact shadows.

Shadow mapsTexture lookup returns visibility information
Describe the shadow map as a visibility cache from the light. The main render projects each fragment into that cache and compares depths. Bias addresses numerical disagreement between the two passes, while percentage-closer filtering averages nearby pass-fail comparisons rather than interpolating the stored depths themselves.
Direction-domain textures

Environment Maps Lookup Radiance by Direction

Different input, same machinery

The lookup coordinate comes from a unit direction rather than a surface location. A ray that misses the scene, or a mirror reflection ray from a shiny surface, asks the environment for radiance.

direction r → environment coordinates → filtered radiance
Lat-long

Simple formula, pole compression.

Cubemap

Six charts, lower distortion.

Lighting

Samples become illumination, not surface color.

Environment mapsTexture domain is the sphere of directions
Make clear that environment maps are still texture maps, but the domain is directions. The reflected ray direction determines the lookup. Latitude-longitude storage is easy to derive and easy to visualize, while cubemaps reduce pole distortion and are common in interactive rendering systems.
Procedural 3D textures

Some Materials Are Better as Functions Than Images

Why procedural solid textures matter

  • They avoid a 2D parameterization when the material is naturally volumetric.
  • They define values everywhere in space, so cuts through the object still look coherent.
  • Noise and turbulence add natural variation across several feature scales.
  • They trade storage and UV labor for computation and careful function design.
Procedural texturesSolid functions, stripe fields, noise, turbulence
Use procedural textures as the endpoint of the chapter: the machinery is still about sampling a signal, but the signal need not be a stored 2D image. Solid textures are useful when the material should behave like it fills a volume, and turbulence provides multiscale irregularity without hand-painted data.
Texture-mapping debugger

Read Artifacts by Asking What Changed

Inspection checklist

  • Do colored corners land on the intended surface corners?
  • Do grid cells keep stable size away from intentional distortion?
  • Does a seam triangle interpolate locally or across the atlas?
  • Does the far field shimmer, alias, or blur directionally?
  • Does changing bias fix acne without separating shadows?
  • Do procedural changes alter both numeric checks and visible variation?

Applied lab: change one parameter, predict which failure signature should move, then compare the visual artifact with the recorded numeric check.

Debugging workflowVisual artifact plus numeric invariant
Turn the notebook into a practical debugging routine. Diagnostic textures should reveal orientation, scale, and seam errors immediately. The strongest lab answers pair a visual observation with a numeric invariant, because texture bugs often look plausible until a check exposes the wrong mapping or filtering assumption.
