Distal Scene, Proximal Pattern, Perceived World

vision science vocabulary for rendering

Rendering Limit

A renderer controls emitted light and samples. It does not directly control lightness, object identity, or depth experience.

Perceptual Rule

Vision estimates stable world causes from changing image evidence, so context is part of the signal.

Graphics Warning

Numerically accurate pixels can still be perceptually misleading when the wrong cues dominate the interpretation.

Distal and proximal stimulus language is useful because it separates the world from the retinal or display pattern. A renderer controls only the proximal side. The viewer uses priors, context, and task demands to infer the distal scene, so identical pixels can support different perceptual conclusions.

Sensitivity Is Pattern-Dependent

contrast, frequency, and adaptation

Local Contrast

\( C \approx \Delta L / L \)

A 1 percent luminance change can matter at an edge, but visibility depends on the pattern around it.

Display Consequence

Small high-frequency texture may vanish at one viewing distance and become obvious at another.

Design Move

Audit fine detail with viewing geometry, not just with framebuffer differences.

Treat contrast sensitivity as a design constraint rather than as trivia about the eye. Fine detail, low contrast, and viewing distance interact. The notebook grating check makes this operational by marking which frequency and contrast cells cross a visibility threshold under a simple synthetic model.

Adaptation Moves The Useful Window

dynamic range is perceptual as well as physical

Graphics Implications

  • HDR tone mapping should protect relative differences the viewer can use after adaptation.
  • Dark-to-bright transitions need temporal consideration, not only a static exposure choice.
  • Color-rich detail can become weak when the operating window shifts toward low light.
local what changed near an edge
state what the eye is adapted to
Lecture Cue

Ask whether the viewer is detecting energy, ratios, or a contrast pattern after adaptation.

Adaptation should be linked to tone mapping and display range. The visual system covers enormous physical luminance changes by moving a smaller useful discrimination window. A graphics system that ignores the adaptation state can preserve numeric luminance while still losing what the viewer needs to compare.

Equal Pixels Can Support Unequal Percepts

lightness and color are contextual

Invariant Sample

The center values are held fixed; the perceptual report changes because the surround changes the inferred surface.

Common Failure

A color picker validates a local value, while the user judges the color inside a full scene.

Graphics Test

Preview colors, labels, and medical or design cues in their actual contrast context.

Spend time on the equal centers because this is the chapter's practical warning for debugging appearance. The center samples can be numerically identical, yet the surrounds change the inferred lightness or color. Pixel equality is a local fact, while perceived appearance is a contextual inference.

Color Is A Perceptual Coordinate, Not A Spectrum

spectral compression makes displays possible

Cone Response Model

\( r_i = \int E(\lambda)s_i(\lambda)\,d\lambda \)

The viewer receives a small response vector, not the original spectrum.

Why RGB Works

Three display primaries can be mixed to match many cone-response triples.

Why RGB Is Limited

Device primaries, adaptation, and context still affect the perceived color.

Use the cone diagram to explain why color management works at all and why it remains limited. Three broad cone responses collapse spectra into a smaller coordinate. Metamers are not mistakes in a display pipeline; they are consequences of compressive biological measurement.

Spatial Vision Turns Cues Into Constraints

depth is inferred from aligned assumptions

Oculomotor

Focus and eye rotation carry near-field information when the geometry is known.

Binocular

Disparity and vergence triangulate layout, but conflict with flat display surfaces.

Pictorial

Occlusion, perspective, texture, shading, and shadows are strong but assumption-heavy.

Motion

Parallax and optic flow reveal layout over time, while independent objects complicate the field.

Frame spatial vision as constraint fusion. No single image cue simply writes depth into the picture. Vergence, disparity, motion, texture, shadows, and occlusion each carry assumptions. Reliable graphics aligns several cues so the visual system can settle on one coherent layout.

Depth Cues Have Geometry And Assumptions

metric when calibrated, ordinal when not

Vergence triangulates near depth

\( d = \frac{b}{2\tan(\alpha/2)} \)

Ground declination uses eye height

\( d = h / \tan\theta \)

Notebook validation: vergence residual is zero in the synthetic check; ground declination distance decreases as the visual angle increases.
Make the formulas concrete by tying each one to a picture operation. Vergence gives a metric constraint when eye geometry is known, while ground declination gives a distance relation when eye height and ground contact are plausible. Both are fragile when their assumptions are violated.

Pictorial Cues Are Strong Because They Are Structured

order, contact, slant, and shape

Consistent Cues Feel Effortless

  • Occlusion establishes which surface is in front, but not exact metric distance.
  • Cast shadows can attach floating objects to a support plane.
  • Texture gradients assume regular surface markings.
  • Shading assumes a light direction and material model.
Cue Conflict

A realistic renderer can still look wrong when cues disagree about contact, scale, or surface orientation.

The pictorial cue slide is a catalog of assumptions, not a list of tricks. Occlusion gives order, shadows attach objects to support surfaces, texture gradients imply slant, and shading constrains normals under a light model. Rendering errors often come from mixing these cues inconsistently.

Motion Reveals Layout, But Sampling Can Lie

optic flow and temporal aliasing

Heading Cue

Forward motion creates a flow field centered on the direction of travel when the scene is static.

Scale Ambiguity

Doubling scene scale and speed can leave translational flow unchanged in the notebook check.

Animation Risk

Periodic detail near the frame rate can generate a stable but wrong apparent motion.

Motion needs both signal and sampling vocabulary. Optic flow can reveal heading and relative scene layout, but animation samples can also manufacture apparent motion that contradicts physical motion. The wagon-wheel style aliasing example connects perception directly to frame rate and temporal filtering.

Object Perception: Group First, Then Identify

segmentation and recognition are different jobs

Why It Matters For Interfaces

  • Grouping can make several marks feel like one object before the viewer names it.
  • Similarity can hide a target even when its pixels are sharp and high contrast.
  • Recognition may depend on a canonical view, visible parts, or invariant features.

Separate grouping from identification. Proximity, similarity, continuity, and common fate organize primitives before a recognition strategy names the object. This distinction matters for graphics interfaces because a well-rendered target can still be hard to find if grouping fights the intended task.

Attention And Events Decide What Gets Used

pop-out, search, and time-to-contact

Fast Route

Single-feature differences in color, orientation, or shape are easy to notice.

Slow Route

Targets defined by combinations can require serial search even when each primitive is clear.

Event Route

Image expansion and optic flow can warn about contact before an object is semantically recognized.

Attention and event perception give the chapter an interaction design edge. A single strong feature can pop out quickly, while conjunction search costs more. Expanding image motion can signal impending contact, so visual emphasis should respect how users discover events under time pressure.

Picture Perception: The Display Has Its Own Geometry

projection station point versus actual viewer

Correct Station Point

A perspective image matches one frustum and one intended viewing geometry.

Mismatch

Moving closer widens the actual border angle; moving farther narrows it.

Application

Large screens, VR walls, simulators, and design review displays should make viewing distance explicit.

Picture perception returns to projection geometry. A perspective image is correct for a particular station point, but the actual viewer may sit closer or farther away. Human perception often tolerates the mismatch, yet large screens, simulators, and immersive displays must account for it explicitly.

Takeaways: Render For Human Inference

what to remember from Chapter 19

Appearance is contextual. Local RGB values do not determine perceived lightness or color by themselves.
Depth is cue alignment. Good pictures coordinate perspective, occlusion, shadows, texture, stereo, and motion.
Motion is sampled. Flow can reveal layout, while frame sampling can create convincing aliases.
Pictures are geometry. The flat display, viewer position, and rendered frustum all participate in perception.
