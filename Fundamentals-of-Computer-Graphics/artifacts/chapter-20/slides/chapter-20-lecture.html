Chapter 20Tone Reproduction
Open by defining tone reproduction as a deliberate mapping from radiance-like scene data to display values. The important phrase is controlled loss. The operator cannot preserve everything, so the lecture will ask what signal each family protects: ratios, local contrast, gradients, color relationships, or visual appearance.
Range before appearance

The Display Has Fewer Useful Stops Than The Scene

Definition

Dynamic range is a ratio, so the useful measurement lives naturally in log units.

$$R_{\mathrm{stops}}=\log_2\left({L_{\mathrm{hi}}\over L_{\mathrm{lo}}}\right)$$
  • The notebook synthetic HDR scene measures about 16.7 stops.
  • Robust percentiles avoid treating isolated outliers as the whole scene.
  • A darker night scene may have lower values but still have high range.
Dynamic rangeprinted pages 569-574
Use the ladder to make dynamic range numerical instead of vague. The notebook scene spans about 16.7 stops, while the target display interval is bounded and quantized. Point out that lowering every value does not lower dynamic range; only the ratio between useful dark and bright values changes it.
Classification

Operators Choose A Domain For The Sacrifice

Curve families

Use one compressive mapping for every pixel. Fast, controllable, and often good enough for preview.

Image-formation families

Use log luminance, frequency, base-detail, or gradient fields to protect texture-like structure.

Spatial and appearance families

Use neighborhoods or vision models when global compression cannot preserve the intended perceptual signal.

Operator taxonomyWhat information moves?
This slide is the map for the chapter. Tone reproduction operators differ less by taste than by the domain in which they decide to spend contrast. A one-dimensional curve, a base-detail split, an edited gradient field, a local adaptation map, and a night appearance model answer different questions.
Transfer functions

Tone Curves Spend Display Codes Nonlinearly

Global curve idea

Every pixel uses the same input-output rule. The curve controls where contrast is dense and where it is compressed.

$$L_d={L\over 1+L}\qquad L_d={\log(1+L)\over\log(1+L_{\max})}$$
Tone curvesOne rule for all pixels
Present tone curves as transfer functions with explicit failure modes. Linear normalization keeps ratios but wastes most display codes when highlights set the maximum. Clamping chooses a white point and discards brighter structure. Log, Reinhard, and sigmoid maps bend the curve so midtones receive usable display resolution.
Exposure is diagnosis

Compression Tries To Keep Evidence From Several Exposures

Underexpose

Interior evidence is buried in shadows.

Overexpose

Bright regions saturate to the same code.

Compress

The operator compresses range rather than choosing one exposure.

Exposure and clippingSynthetic HDR scene from the notebook
The exposure panels should feel like a diagnostic sweep through the same HDR array. A dark exposure protects the bright window and loses the room. A bright exposure reveals interior detail and clips the window. Compression is attractive because it tries to keep evidence from both exposure regimes.
Image formation in density

Log Luminance Turns A Product Into A Sum

Diffuse model
$$L_v(x,y)=r(x,y)E_v(x,y)$$
$$D(x,y)=\log L_v=\log r+\log E_v$$
  • Reflectance is surface structure and tends to be lower range.
  • Illuminance can span many stops and dominates HDR variation.
  • Highlights violate the clean diffuse interpretation but still need handling.
Image formationBase/detail intuition
Move from photography language to image formation. In diffuse regions, luminance can be treated as reflectance multiplied by illuminance. Taking logs turns that product into a sum, which explains why density, base layers, and detail layers are natural computational objects for tone reproduction.
Frequency and base-detail operators

Compress The Low-Frequency Base, Then Restore Detail

Operator pattern

Filter log luminance into a smooth base and a detail layer. Compress the base, then add detail back before exponentiating to display values.

The frequency view says that illumination often changes slowly while texture and object boundaries change quickly. The operator compresses the smooth base and adds detail back afterward. Gradient-domain operators

Pipeline

Compute log-luminance gradients, attenuate extreme jumps, then solve for the density image whose gradients best match the edited field.

$$\nabla D \rightarrow g' \qquad \Delta D'=\nabla\cdot g'$$

For gradient-domain operators, the editable object is not luminance but the slope field. Large density jumps from highlights are attenuated, then a Poisson reconstruction finds an image whose gradients best match the edited field. The notebook residual reminds students that edited gradients are not always perfectly integrable.
Spatial operators

Global Operators Use One Anchor; Local Operators Use Neighborhoods

Global

Analyze the whole image once. Geometric mean luminance often steers a bounded curve.

Local

Compute a neighborhood statistic for each pixel. This increases local detail but makes halos possible near strong edges.

$$\bar L_v=\exp\left({1\over N}\sum_{x,y}\log(\delta+L_v(x,y))\right)$$
Spatial operatorsGlobal statistics versus adaptation fields
Contrast global and local spatial operators by their source of the adaptation value. A global method anchors every pixel to one image statistic, often the geometric mean. A local method uses neighborhood evidence, which can reveal shadow detail but also makes the operator sensitive to neighborhood size and edges.
Division operators

Blurred Divisors Create A Halo Tradeoff At Edges

Division form
$$L_d(x,y)={L_v(x,y)\over f(x,y)}$$

When \(f\) is a blurred image, it may describe neither side of a strong edge.

Notebook halo scoreValue
division small blur0.532

Large blur hides local mismatch but also moves the operator back toward global behavior.

Division operatorsLocal contrast versus halo risk
Division by a blurred adaptation image is intuitive but fragile. Near a strong edge, the blur mixes two neighborhoods that should not share one divisor, producing bands or halos. Increasing the blur can hide the artifact, but in the limit the operator gives up its local advantage.
Sigmoid operators

Semi-Saturation Selects The Most Legible Luminance Band

Compressive form
$$L_d={L_v\over L_v+f^n}$$

For \(n=1\), \(L_v=f\) maps to \(L_d=0.5\).

  • Small luminance values are approximately linear.
  • The output is bounded above by one.
  • A local \(f(x,y)\) acts like an adaptation image.
SigmoidsPhotoreceptor-inspired bounded response
The sigmoid slide should make the semi-saturation constant feel like a pivot rather than a magic parameter. For exponent one, an input equal to f maps to one half. Moving f decides which luminance band is most legible, and making f local turns the curve into an adaptation operator.
Color after luminance compression

Preserving Ratios Is A Numerical Choice, Not Always An Appearance Choice

Ratio reconstruction
$$I_{r,d}=\left({I_r\over L_v}\right)^s L_d$$

Color appearance can be adjusted after luminance compression, but the adjustment should be named and measured.

Color preservationLuminance mapping plus chromaticity choice
Separate luminance compression from color reconstruction. Preserving RGB-to-luminance ratios is a clean invariant before clipping, but it can look too saturated when the display surround differs from the scene. The parameter s is a controlled way to trade chromatic constancy against plausible appearance.

Applied operator selection

Choose The Operator Whose Loss Matches The Task

Fast preview

Use a global log, Reinhard, or sigmoid curve. Accept less local detail for predictable speed.

Offline still with extreme range

Use bilateral base compression or local sigmoid adaptation when halos can be inspected.

Diagnostic or color-critical review

Inspect clipping, gradient energy, chromaticity drift, and saturation decisions separately.

RecapMake the compromise inspectable
Close by giving students a repeatable checklist. First measure the range, then choose the signal to protect, then name the artifact the operator can create, and finally verify with a numeric invariant. Tone reproduction is successful when its compromises are visible to the designer before the viewer notices them.
