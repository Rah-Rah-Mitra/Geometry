How Shape Becomes Brightness

A local shading model turns surface orientation, light geometry, viewer direction, and material parameters into inspectable RGB values.

Lecture Goal

Separate the light calculation from the material calculation so errors can be diagnosed before a renderer grows shadows, textures, and global illumination.

Open by framing surface shading as a diagnostic contract rather than a bag of visual tricks. The learner should leave knowing which variables belong to light transport, which belong to material response, and why separating those calculations makes both ray tracing and rasterization easier to debug.

Use the route slide to establish the chapter architecture before any formulas appear. The sequence is intentionally implementation shaped: compute what reaches the surface, decide what the material sends toward the camera, then add checks that catch the most common numerical and visual failures.
Model Boundary

Local Shading Answers A Narrow Question

not global illumination

Question

At one surface point, what RGB radiance should travel toward this camera sample?

Inputs

  • surface position and unit normal
  • light position or light direction
  • viewer direction
  • material color and highlight parameters
Emphasize that the chapter is not promising a complete physical renderer. It isolates a single surface point and asks what happens locally. That boundary is useful because many later effects, including shadows, reflections, texture maps, and global transport, still need this local calculation as a reliable component.
Point Source Irradiance

Distance Spreads Power Over Area

inverse square
Epoint = I / r2 before tilt
Double distance gives one quarter irradiance.

Implementation Meaning

The intensity belongs to the source. The distance term belongs to the relationship between the source and the shaded point.

Present inverse-square falloff as conservation of emitted power rather than as a magic graphics constant. The same source power crosses a larger spherical area as distance grows, so a small receiver gets less power per unit area. This is why the distance calculation belongs before material color.
Lambert Cosine Law

Tilt Reduces The Effective Receiving Area

max(0, n dot l)
cos(theta) = n dot l
E = source strength x max(0, n dot l)

Clamp Before Shading

A back-facing patch should not contribute negative light. The clamp is part of the model, not an afterthought.

Teach the cosine factor as projected area. A tilted patch intercepts less of the same beam, so its irradiance decreases even though the material has not changed. The clamp at zero is equally important because local shading should not create negative radiance from a back-facing configuration.
Two Light Models

Point Lights Carry Position; Directional Lights Carry Direction

same cosine gate

Point Source

r = ||p - x||
l = (p - x) / r
E = I max(0, n dot l) / r2

Nearby surfaces can receive much stronger irradiance than distant ones.

Directional Source

l is already the source direction
E = H max(0, n dot l)

The source is idealized as far enough away that distance variation is ignored.

Compare the two source types by what an implementation must store and compute. A point light needs its position, then every shaded point derives distance and direction. A directional light supplies the direction and a normal irradiance, so the same cosine gate remains but the inverse-square term disappears.
Diffuse Reflection

Lambertian Materials Forget The Viewer Direction

view-independent
Lr,diffuse = (R / pi) E
R is an RGB reflectance, applied componentwise.

Shape Cue

Diffuse shading depends on surface normal and light direction, so it reveals shape even when the camera moves.

Material Cue

Changing R changes the base color without changing where the highlight would appear.

Stress the phrase view-independent because it is the easiest way to separate diffuse reflection from glossy reflection. Lambertian response can still create a strong form cue, but the cue comes from the light-normal relationship. The camera direction is not part of the diffuse material factor.
Specular Reflection

Blinn-Phong Uses The Half Vector As A Mirror Test

view-dependent
h = normalize(l + v)
specular factor = max(0, n dot h)p

What Moves?

The highlight can shift when the viewer moves because v changes the half vector.

Introduce the half vector as a practical mirror-alignment test. When light and view are symmetric around the normal, the half vector points along the normal and the specular term peaks. Unlike diffuse shading, moving the camera changes the calculation because the view vector is one of the inputs.
Highlight Width

The Exponent Turns Alignment Into Gloss

p controls falloff

Small p

Broad highlight, useful for dull or satin materials.

Medium p

Clear glossy spot that still covers a visible neighborhood.

Large p

Narrow, sensitive highlight that needs careful sampling and filtering.

Read the exponent plot as a parameter diagnostic. Raising a number between zero and one to a higher power makes it collapse faster, so a large exponent keeps only very close half-vector alignment. This explains both the visual sharpness and the numerical sensitivity of glossy highlights.
Basic Reflection Model

The Material Factor Mixes Diffuse Color With Specular Shine

BRDF-like factor
Lr = [ R / pi + ks max(0, n dot h)p ] E
The bracket describes material response; E describes available irradiance.

R

Diffuse reflectance. Use separate RGB values for colored base materials.

ks

Specular scale. Neutral values often keep plastic highlights white.

p

Phong exponent. Higher values concentrate the specular contribution.

The calculation is componentwise in red, green, and blue. The geometry terms still use normalized directions, not color values.

Separate the bracketed material factor from the irradiance factor. That separation is the heart of the chapter. It lets students see that changing light distance changes E, while changing reflectance or the exponent changes the material response after light has already arrived at the surface.
Ambient Illumination

A Constant Fill Term Keeps The Simple Model Usable

approximation
Lambient = ka Ia
Ltotal = local terms + ambient fill

What It Does

It prevents unlit regions from collapsing to pure black and gives an easy scene-level contrast control.

What It Is Not

It is not a full model of extended light, color bleeding, occlusion, or global illumination.

Describe ambient light honestly as a useful approximation. It has no direction, so it only contributes a diffuse-like constant term. That simplicity is precisely why it helps early renderers, but it also explains why ambient cannot model darker corners, indirect color transfer, or true environment lighting.

Link this slide to the notebook dashboard idea. Students should not debug a final shaded image as one undifferentiated output. Splitting diffuse, specular, ambient, and combined terms turns visual artifacts into clues about which input vector, material coefficient, or clamp is probably wrong.
Vector Hygiene

Normalize Before Dot Products; Clamp Before Powers

implementation invariant

Unit Vectors

The dot products mean cosines only when n, l, v, and h have unit length.

Clamped Factors

Use max(0, n dot l) for irradiance and max(0, n dot h) for specular response.

Power After Clamp

A specular exponent should sharpen a valid alignment value, not amplify a broken one.

Make the numerical invariant explicit: dot products are cosines only under unit-length assumptions. A scaled light vector can create values above one, while an unclamped dot product can create negative irradiance. These errors are not small style differences; they change the meaning of the shading model.
Implementation Skeleton

Keep Irradiance And Reflection As Separate Functions

ray tracing or rasterization

Irradiance Function

d = light.position - x
r = length(d)
l = normalize(d)
E = light.I * max(0, dot(n, l)) / (r * r)

Reflection Function

h = normalize(l + v)
diff = material.R / pi
spec = material.ks * pow(max(0, dot(n, h)), p)
color = (diff + spec) * E + material.ka * ambient.I

The two functions can be reused in different renderers. What changes is how x, n, l, and v are obtained.

Use pseudocode to show the software design lesson hiding inside the math. Ray tracing and rasterization may produce the inputs differently, but the local shading calculation can be shared. Keeping irradiance separate from reflection also gives tests natural boundaries and makes visual debugging much faster.
Failure Modes

Most Shading Bugs Have A Geometric Signature

diagnose by pattern
Negative Light

Back faces go dark in the wrong direction or subtract from color.

Scaled Dot Products

Highlights explode because a vector was not normalized.

Wrong Direction

The lit side rotates opposite to the light source.

Ambient Washout

The object becomes flat because the constant term is too large.

Turn the failure list into a practical review protocol. Ask students to name which component image would expose each bug. A bad normal usually appears in diffuse shading, a bad view vector appears in specular shading, and excessive ambient light hides geometry rather than distorting the light direction.
Applied Lab

Sweep Incidence Angle And Exponent

inspect the ridge

Inspection Target

As p increases, the response collapses into a thin ridge near perfect half-vector alignment.

Design Question

Which exponent still gives a readable highlight under your camera distance, mesh resolution, and antialiasing setup?

Connect the lab to renderer design rather than treating it as a separate plot. The response surface explains why glossy materials can alias or flicker: large exponents concentrate energy into very small angular neighborhoods. Students should inspect how sampling and filtering interact with the chosen exponent.
Classroom Design Task

Build A Three-Material Shading Test

original experiment

Matte Clay

High diffuse reflectance, no specular term, low ambient fill.

Gloss Paint

Colored diffuse base, neutral specular coefficient, medium exponent.

Polished Plastic

Lower diffuse base, stronger neutral specular term, large exponent.

Required Evidence

For each material, save the diffuse, specular, ambient, and combined panels. Then write one sentence explaining which term changed the visual impression most.

Hold geometry fixed; vary R, ks, p, and ka.
Assign this as an experiment in controlled variation. The geometry, light, and camera should stay fixed while material parameters change. Requiring component panels prevents students from merely picking attractive numbers; they must say whether the visual difference came from diffuse color, specular width, or ambient floor.
Recap

Surface Shading Is A Small Pipeline With Strong Invariants

carry forward
Irradiance

Light geometry determines how much energy reaches the surface.

Diffuse

Lambertian response is view-independent and color-bearing.

Specular

Blinn-Phong response uses h and p to make glossy highlights.

Ambient

A constant fill term keeps simple scenes readable.

Final Test

Every term should be explainable from a direction, a distance, a material parameter, or a deliberate approximation. If not, split the render and inspect the invariant that failed.

Close by returning to the chapter question. A shaded image should be inspectable: each bright or dark region should trace back to light geometry, diffuse reflectance, specular alignment, or ambient fill. This habit prepares students for later physically based rendering, where the terms become richer but the discipline remains.
