Local Differential Topology

The Morse Lemma: Local Coordinate Models

Definition 1.1
Nondegenerate Critical Points

A critical point \(p\) of a smooth function \(f: M \to \mathbb{R}\) is **nondegenerate** if the Hessian matrix of second-order partial derivatives is invertible: $$\det\left( \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}(p) \right) \neq 0$$

Morse Lemma

Near a nondegenerate critical point \(p\), there exists a local coordinate system \((u_1, \dots, u_m)\) centered at \(p\) such that the function is strictly quadratic: $$f(u) = f(p) - \sum_{i=1}^\lambda u_i^2 + \sum_{j=\lambda+1}^m u_j^2$$ where \(\lambda\) is the **Morse index** of \(f\) at \(p\).

Index \(\lambda\) = # negative eigenvalues

Local Geometric Meaning

The index \(\lambda\) counts the maximum dimension of a subspace on which the Hessian is negative definite. Geometrically, it measures the number of independent directions in which the function values decrease as we move away from \(p\).

Section 2: Morse Local Models Hessian quadratic forms
The Morse Lemma is a local classification result. It states that near any nondegenerate critical point of a smooth function, we can choose coordinates such that the function is strictly quadratic. The number of negative signs in this coordinate representation is the index lambda of the critical point, representing the number of independent directions of downward curvature.
Homotopy & Cobordism

Global Morse Theory: Building Spaces via Handles

Filtration by Sublevel Sets

We analyze the topology of a manifold \(M\) by studying the family of sublevel sets: $$M^c = \{x \in M \mid f(x) \le c\}$$ As \(c\) varies, the topology of \(M^c\) remains unchanged as long as \(c\) does not cross a critical value.

Homotopy Equivalence

Attaching a \(\lambda\)-handle is homotopically equivalent to attaching a cell \(e^\lambda\) of dimension \(\lambda\). Homologically, this means: $$H_r(M^{c+\epsilon}) \cong H_r(M^{c-\epsilon}) \quad \text{for } r < \lambda$$

Theorem 1.2
Handle Attachment Step

If \(f\) has a single nondegenerate critical point \(p\) of index \(\lambda\) in the interval \([c-\epsilon, c+\epsilon]\), then the sublevel set \(M^{c+\epsilon}\) is diffeomorphic to \(M^{c-\epsilon}\) with a \(\lambda\)-handle attached: $$M^{c+\epsilon} \cong M^{c-\epsilon} \cup_{S^{\lambda-1} \times D^{m-\lambda}} \left( D^\lambda \times D^{m-\lambda} \right)$$

\(\lambda\)-handle \(\implies\) cell attachment of dimension \(\lambda\)
Section 3: Global Morse Theory homological cell attachment
In global Morse theory, as our height parameter crosses a critical value, the topology of the sublevel set changes by the attachment of a cell. This cell, or handle, has a dimension exactly equal to the Morse index lambda. This cellular decomposition means that homology in degrees strictly less than lambda remains stable and unchanged as we pass the critical level.
Complex Structure Restrictions

Complex Geometry Restricts the Morse Index

Smooth Affine Variety

Let \(U \subset \mathbb{C}^N\) be a smooth affine algebraic variety of complex dimension \(n\) (real dimension \(2n\)). Let \(f_p(z) = \|z - p\|^2\) be the Euclidean distance function to a generic point \(p \in \mathbb{C}^N\).

Theorem 1.3
Morse Index Upper Bound

For a generic point \(p\), the distance function \(f_p\) restricted to \(U\) is a Morse function, and **every** critical point has index: $$\lambda \le n$$ where \(n\) is the complex dimension of \(U\).

The Core Mechanism

On a complex manifold, the complex Hessian of a plurisubharmonic function (like the distance function) has at least \(n\) positive eigenvalues at any point. Because the index counts negative eigenvalues, the number of negative directions cannot exceed the remaining \(n\) directions. Hence, \(\lambda \le n\).

Homotopy Type of Affine Pieces

This bound implies that any smooth affine variety \(U\) of complex dimension \(n\) is homotopically equivalent to a cell complex of dimension **at most** \(n\).

Section 4: Affine Index Restrictions complex Hessian invariants
This is the pivotal transition to algebraic geometry. When we examine a smooth affine complex variety of complex dimension n, its real dimension is two n. In the real setting, a Morse function could have critical points of index up to two n. However, due to the complex structure and the properties of the complex Hessian, the index of an algebraic distance function is strictly bounded by n.
The Hyperplane Comparison

The Lefschetz Hyperplane Section Theorem

cohomology of the pair

Let \(U = X \setminus Y\) be the open complement. Since \(Y\) is a hyperplane section, \(U\) is a smooth affine variety of dimension \(n\). Homotopically, \(U\) is a cell complex of dimension \(\le n\). By duality: $$H^r(X, Y; \mathbb{Z}) \cong H_{2n-r}(U; \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \quad \text{for } r < n$$

The Restriction Mapping

The long exact sequence of the pair \((X, Y)\) translates this vanishing immediately into isomorphism and injection ranges for the restriction map.

Theorem 1.4
Lefschetz Hyperplane Section Theorem

Let \(X\) be a smooth complex projective variety of dimension \(n\), and \(Y \subset X\) a smooth hyperplane section. Then: $$H^r(X, Y; \mathbb{Z}) = 0 \quad \text{for } r < n$$ The restriction map \(i^*: H^r(X; \mathbb{Z}) \to H^r(Y; \mathbb{Z})\) is: - an **isomorphism** for \(r < n - 1\) - a **monomorphism (injection)** for \(r = n - 1\)

\(H^r(X) \xrightarrow{\cong} H^r(Y)\) for \(r \le n-2\)
Section 5: The Hyperplane Theorem homology comparison exact sequence
We now state the Lefschetz Hyperplane Section Theorem. Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension n, and Y a smooth hyperplane section. The theorem asserts that the cohomology of the pair vanishes below degree n. Consequently, the restriction map is an isomorphism in all degrees below n minus one, and remains injective at the middle dimension. This allows us to understand X using its lower-dimensional slice.
Geometric Intuition

Visualizing the Slicing Hyperplane Section

Deformation of Cycles

For any homological cycle \(\gamma\) of dimension \(r < n\), we can continuously deform it within \(X\) until its support lies entirely inside the hyperplane section \(Y\).

The Role of the Complement

The open variety \(U = X \setminus Y\) is affine, meaning it is topologically "too thin" to support independent homology classes in degrees less than \(n\). This is why the intersection \(Y\) acts as a complete homological representation of the parent variety \(X\).

Only when we reach the middle dimension do we find cycles that cannot be retracted, as they are blocked by the topology of the affine complement.

Review & Retrospective

Summary & Pedagogical Pitfalls

Key Lecture Takeaways

  • Morse theory enables the decomposition of complex varieties using the level sets of algebraic distance functions.
  • A complex affine variety \(U\) of dimension \(n\) homotopically behaves like a cell complex of dimension \(\le n\).
  • This cellular dimension bound forces the vanishing of relative homology below the middle dimension.
  • Thus, the hyperplane section \(Y\) completely captures the lower-degree topological invariants of \(X\).

Pedagogical Pitfalls & Traps

  • **Pitfall 1:** Thinking every hyperplane slice is valid. The slice must be smooth and transverse. Bertini's Theorem provides generic smoothness, but bad slices can collapse homology.
  • **Pitfall 2:** Forgetting the affine complement. The Morse height function is run on the open affine variety \(U = X \setminus Y\), not on the compact projective variety \(X\).
  • **Pitfall 3:** Confusing the bounds. Isomorphism holds only up to degree \(n-2\). The boundary degree \(n-1\) is only guaranteed to be injective.
Chapter Retrospective Lefschetz hyperplane theorem
We conclude with the major takeaways and warning flags. Never forget that the hyperplane section must be smooth and transverse, which is guaranteed generically by Bertini's theorem. The Morse function is analyzed on the open affine complement, not the projective variety directly. By understanding these steps, we see how complex structures cleanly restrict topology.
