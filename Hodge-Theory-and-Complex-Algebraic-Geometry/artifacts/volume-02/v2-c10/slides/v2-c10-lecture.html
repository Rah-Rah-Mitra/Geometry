Representability of Zero-Cycles

01
Representable Chow Groups

A Chow group \(CH_0(X)_0\) of degree-zero cycles is representable if it can be parameterized by a smooth projective variety (the Albanese variety \(Alb(X)\)):

\(Alb : CH_0(X)_0 \cong Alb(X)(\mathbb{C})\)

Roitman's theorem proves that the torsion subgroup of \(CH_0(X)_0\) maps isomorphism-cleanly onto the torsion of its Albanese variety.

Common Pitfall

Do not assume representability holds for all varieties. While curves always have representable Chow groups (equal to their Jacobian tori), surfaces and higher-dimensional varieties can exhibit massive, infinite-dimensional Chow groups that cannot be parameterized by any algebraic variety.

Representability asks if zero-cycles can be parameterized by an algebraic variety. For curves, the Abel-Jacobi map always establishes representability via Jacobians. However, for higher-dimensional varieties, this represents a major obstruction, which Roitman tames only at the torsion level.

Mumford's Theorem on Infinite Dimensionality

02
Mumford's Theorem

Mumford proved that for a smooth projective surface \(X\) over \(\mathbb{C}\), if the geometric genus is non-zero, then the group of zero-cycles is infinite-dimensional:

\(p_g = h^{2,0}(X) > 0 \Longrightarrow \dim CH_0(X)_0 = \infty\)

This means \(CH_0(X)_0\) cannot be parameterized by the Albanese variety or any other finite-dimensional algebraic variety, revealing a deep complex-analytic obstruction.

The Proof Move

The proof translates this infinite-dimensionality into a statement about holomorphic two-forms. If \(CH_0(X)_0\) were representable, we could construct a family of cycles whose integration against a holomorphic two-form \(\omega\) vanishes, contradicting \(h^{2,0} > 0\).

Mumford's theorem is a landmark result. It states that if a smooth projective surface carries any holomorphic two-forms, then its zero-cycles are infinite-dimensional. This prevents representability, showing that analytic two-forms act as obstructions to parameterizing cycles algebraically.

The Bloch-Srinivas Diagonal Method

03
Correspondence Limits

Bloch and Srinivas revolutionized cycle theory by showing how representability questions can be translated into identities of correspondences on the product space \(X \times X\).

If \(CH_0(X)_0\) is representable, we can find a codimension-one divisor \(D \subset X\) such that the diagonal correspondence deforms into supported components.

Proof Action

By decomposing the diagonal as a correspondence operator, we can read support restrictions directly as cohomological annihilation: the supported parts act as zero operators on specific Hodge pieces, forcing cohomological vanishing.

Bloch and Srinivas introduced a beautiful proof move: decomposing the diagonal correspondence. Instead of working with individual cycles, they decomposed the identity operator on cohomology. Under representability, this diagonal splits into pieces supported on subvarieties, restricting how cohomology classes can move.

Visualizing the Decomposition of the Diagonal

04
Operator Splitting

The diagonal correspondence \(\Delta \subset X \times X\) decomposes into components supported on divisors and points:

\(\Delta \equiv Z_1 + Z_2 \pmod{\text{rat}}\)

where \(\text{Supp}(Z_1) \subset D \times X\) for a divisor \(D \subset X\), and \(Z_2\) is supported on \(X \times \{p\}\).

We visualize this diagonal decomposition. The diagonal correspondence Delta represents the identity operator on cohomology. Under representability, this Delta decomposes algebraically into two pieces: Z-1, which is supported along a codimension-one divisor D times X, and Z-2, which is supported on X times a point p.

Supported Pieces Restrict Cohomology Actions

05
Topological Annihilation

Because \(Z_1\) is supported on \(D \times X\), its action on cohomology factors through the restriction map of the divisor \(D\). In particular, it acts as the zero operator on forms that vanish along all divisors.

This forces the identity operator to act as zero on these forms, yielding Mumford's vanishing condition:

\(H^{2,0}(X) = 0\)

Generalised Vanishing

For higher dimensions, representability of \(CH_0(X)_0\) similarly forces all holomorphic forms of degree \(p \ge 2\) to vanish: \(H^{p,0}(X) = 0\).

This diagonal decomposition restricts cohomology actions. Because Z-1 is supported on D times X, its action on forms must factor through the restriction map of D. This forces the identity operator to act as zero on forms that vanish along divisors, yielding Mumford's cohomological vanishing condition.

Generalised Decompositions and Chow Groups

06
Higher Codimension Decompositions

If we allow the diagonal to be decomposed with support on higher codimension subvarieties, we obtain generalized decompositions of the diagonal:

\(\Delta \equiv Z_1 + Z_2 \pmod{\text{rat}}\)

where \(\text{Supp}(Z_1) \subset Y \times X\) for some closed subvariety \(Y \subset X\) of codimension \(c\).

Bloch-Srinivas Theorem

The Bloch-Srinivas theorem proves that if \(CH_0(X)_0\) is representable, the diagonal has a generalised decomposition. This has massive consequences, showing that the Hodge filtration steps are determined by topological bounds on codimension supports.

Generalised decompositions extend Mumford's theorem to higher dimensions and codimensions. If the diagonal has support on a subvariety Y of codimension c, the Bloch-Srinivas theorem proves that the Hodge filtration steps are strictly bounded, showing that codimension supports dictate the shape of Hodge cohomology.
