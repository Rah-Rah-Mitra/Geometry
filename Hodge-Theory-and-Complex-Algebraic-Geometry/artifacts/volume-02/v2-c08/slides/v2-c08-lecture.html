Nori's Connectivity Theorem

02
Hypotheses and Setup

Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety of dimension $n \ge 3$, and let $Y \subset X$ be a general smooth projective hypersurface of sufficiently large degree $d \gg 0$.

Classical Lefschetz theory bounds topological relative groups. Nori's work refines this relative bounding to the level of Hodge structures and filtrations.

Theorem Formulation

The relative cohomology satisfies the vanishing condition:

\(H^i(X, Y; \mathbb{C}) = 0 \quad \text{for all} \quad i < n\)

More importantly, Nori's filtration bound asserts that relative Hodge filtration steps split and vanish in a wide triangular region:

\(F^p H^i(X, Y; \mathbb{C}) = 0 \quad \text{for} \quad p + i < n + \text{bound}(d)\)

Cohomological Consequence

The restriction map in complex cohomology \(r^*: H^i(X, \mathbb{C}) \to H^i(Y, \mathbb{C})\) is an isomorphism for all degrees \(i < n - 1\) and is injectively exact for \(i = n - 1\). This map preserves the entire Hodge decomposition structure on those levels, forcing the hypersurface to carry identical Hodge-theoretic data in low dimensions.

Nori's Connectivity Theorem is a profound extension of the classical Lefschetz Hyperplane Theorem. While Lefschetz tells us that the relative cohomology groups vanish for degrees below the dimension, Nori provides a far more refined bound that governs the Hodge filtration of the relative cohomology. Under connectivity conditions where the degree is sufficiently large, the relative Hodge pieces vanish in a broad triangular region, forcing the cohomology of the hypersurface to mirror the ambient space.

Propagation of Cycle Relations

04
Incidence Correspondences

An **incidence correspondence** is a algebraic subvariety representing families of cycles and hypersurfaces:

\(\Sigma \subset S \times X\)

This correspondence links the parameter space $S$ (which parameterizes hypersurfaces or subvarieties $Y_s$) with the target variety $X$.

The Push-Pull Action

Let $p_1: \Sigma \to S$ and $p_2: \Sigma \to X$ be the natural projections. The cycle relation propagates via the push-pull of cohomology classes:

\((p_2)_* \circ p_1^* : H^*(S) \longrightarrow H^*(X)\)

When $S$ is connected, the topological invariants of moving cycles must remain constant, causing cycle differences to merge into equivalence classes.

Main Mechanics

Moving a family of cycles through connected channels collapses cycle distinctions in the Chow groups. If the incidence graph is connected, any cycle difference class is algebraically equivalent to zero. Thus, Nori uses topological connectivity of $S$ to force algebraic collapse on $X$.

To turn geometric connectivity into algebraic results, we use the language of incidence correspondences. An incidence correspondence behaves like a bridge, connecting the parameter space S with our variety X. By taking a class in S and pulling it back to the correspondence, then pushing it forward to X, we can propagate cycle relations. If S is connected, this push-pull action forces cycle classes to merge, collapsing differences in the Chow groups.

Comparing Equivalence Relations

05
Rational Equivalence (\(Z \sim_{\text{rat}} 0\))
  • Cycles are parameterized strictly by the projective line \(\mathbb{P}^1\).
  • Extremely rigid condition: \(Z = \text{div}(f)\) for a rational function \(f\) on a subvariety.
  • Forms the basis of the Chow groups \(CH^c(X)\).
Algebraic Equivalence (\(Z \sim_{\text{alg}} 0\))
  • Cycles are parameterized by any smooth connected algebraic curve \(T\).
  • Much more flexible: \(Z = Z_{t_1} - Z_{t_2}\) for a family of cycles parameterized by \(T\).
  • Allows continuous movement of cycles across connected parameter spaces.
Common Trap: Mixing Equivalence Relations

A frequent error is assuming algebraic equivalence implies rational equivalence. In fact, their difference is massive:

\(CH^c(X)_{\text{alg}} / CH^c(X)_{\text{rat}} \quad \text{is often infinitely generated.}\)

This quotient is modeled by intermediate Jacobians, and Nori's work shows precisely how connectivity limits the size of this quotient under high degrees.

It is essential to distinguish between rational and algebraic equivalence of cycles. Rational equivalence is extremely rigid, requiring cycles to be parameterized by the projective line P1. Algebraic equivalence is far more relaxed, permitting any smooth connected algebraic curve as a parameter space. A major pitfall is assuming these relations coincide. In fact, their difference is a rich, often infinitely generated group that Hodge theory measures.

Hodge Class of a Normal Function

06
Normal Functions

A **normal function** \(\nu\) is a holomorphic horizontal section of the intermediate Jacobian bundle:

\(\mathcal{J}^c(\mathcal{X}/S) \longrightarrow S\)

Normal functions record the period integrals of a moving family of homologically trivial algebraic cycles \(\{Z_s\}_{s \in S}\).

The Hodge Class Obstruction

The normal function \(\nu\) possesses a topological invariant, its **Hodge class**:

\([\nu] \in H^{c,c}(S \times X, \mathbb{Z})\)

This class represents the primary cohomological obstruction to the normal function \(\nu\) being algebraic, i.e., arising from a global cycle.

Theorem (Griffiths / Nori)

The Hodge class \([\nu]\) vanishes if and only if the normal function is algebraic (up to torsion). By studying the connectivity of the parameter space $S$, Nori places severe restrictions on the possible Hodge classes, limiting the possible normal functions.

Normal functions represent the analytic shadow of moving algebraic cycles. A normal function is a holomorphic horizontal section of the intermediate Jacobian bundle. The key topological invariant is the Hodge class of the normal function. If this class vanishes, it indicates that the normal function is algebraic, meaning it comes from a real algebraic cycle. This provides a direct bridge between Hodge-theoretic invariants and algebraic geometry.

Griffiths' Theorem & Nori Equivalence

07
Griffiths' Classical Theorem

Griffiths proved that algebraic equivalence and homological equivalence do not coincide:

\(Z \sim_{\text{hom}} 0 \quad \not\Rightarrow \quad Z \sim_{\text{alg}} 0\)

There exist homologically trivial algebraic cycles on smooth projective varieties that are not algebraically equivalent to zero. These are detected by normal functions.

Nori's Equivalence and Refinement

Nori introduced **Nori equivalence**, which sits between algebraic and homological equivalence:

A cycle is Nori equivalent to zero if its class vanishes in the relative cohomology of a parameter space underconnectivity criteria.

Nori shows that for hypersurfaces of high degree, Nori equivalence collapses the kernel of the Abel-Jacobi map, restricting the pathology found by Griffiths.

The Upshot

Topological connectivity constraints on the parameter spaces of hypersurfaces prevent cycles from wandering independently. Thus, the vastness of Griffiths' kernel is tamed by the connectivity of parameter families.

Griffiths' classical theorem showed that homological and algebraic equivalence do not coincide, which was a major surprise in algebraic cycle theory. Nori refined this result by introducing Nori equivalence, which sits between algebraic and homological equivalence. By exploiting connectivity of parameter spaces, Nori proved that the kernel of the Abel-Jacobi map is highly constrained for hypersurfaces of high degree, showing that topological connectivity prevents cycle pathological behavior.
