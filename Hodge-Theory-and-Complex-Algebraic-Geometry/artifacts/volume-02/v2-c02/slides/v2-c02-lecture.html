Section 1: Linear Systems & Lefschetz Pencils

Linear Systems and Lefschetz Pencils

Slicing a variety \(X \subset \mathbb{P}^N\) with a pencil of hyperplanes \(L_t\) passing through axis \(A\).

Definition
Lefschetz Pencil

A pencil of hyperplanes \(\{L_t\}_{t \in \mathbb{P}^1}\) in \(\mathbb{P}^N\) is a Lefschetz pencil for a smooth projective variety \(X \subset \mathbb{P}^N\) if:
1. The base locus \(B = X \cap A\) is a smooth codimension-2 submanifold of \(X\).
2. The rational map \(X \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^1\) has only isolated critical points, each having an ordinary quadratic singularity.

Theorem (Lefschetz)
Generic Existence

A generic pencil of hyperplanes is a Lefschetz pencil for \(X\). Thus, Lefschetz pencils always exist on any smooth projective variety.

Section 1: Existence of PencilsLefschetz Pencils
Lefschetz's key insight was that we can study the topology of a variety X by cutting it with a family of hyperplanes. A generic pencil of hyperplanes intersects X in a family of hypersurfaces that are smooth for almost all parameters, except for a finite number of critical values where a single ordinary double point appears. The existence theorem guarantees that such pencils always exist. Near these isolated singularities, the variety looks like a complex quadratic cone, which can be analyzed using local coordinates from the holomorphic Morse lemma.
Section 2: Blowing up the Base Locus

Resolving the Rational Map via Blowup

Resolving the rational map \(X \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^1\) by blowing up the base locus \(B\). The exceptional divisor \(E = \pi^{-1}(B)\) acts as a global section of the fibration \(\widetilde{X} \to \mathbb{P}^1\).

Section 3: Blowup of the Base LocusLefschetz Pencils
A pencil of hyperplanes in projective space all intersect along a codimension-two subspace called the axis of the pencil. Consequently, the rational map from X to the projective line is not defined on the base locus B, which is the intersection of X with the axis. To resolve this rational map, we blow up X along B. The blown-up variety, which we denote as X-tilde, maps holomorphically to the projective line. The hyperplanes, which were intersecting along the axis, now become disjoint fibers in the blowup, and the exceptional divisor E maps as a smooth projective bundle over the base locus, providing a natural section.
Section 4: Holomorphic Morse Lemma & Singularities

Lefschetz Degeneration and Holomorphic Morse Lemma

Deforming a smooth complex hypersurface onto a singular fiber with an isolated ordinary double point. The vanishing cycle \(\delta \cong S^{n-1}\) shrinks to a zero-dimensional node.

Theorem
Holomorphic Morse Lemma

Let \(f: X \to D\) be a family of complex manifolds of dimension \(n\) over a disk, smooth except for a single isolated critical point \(p\) in the fiber over \(0\).
Then there exist local holomorphic coordinates \((z_1, \dots, z_n)\) on \(X\) centered at \(p\) such that the map is given by: $$f(z_1, \dots, z_n) = z_1^2 + z_2^2 + \cdots + z_n^2$$

Topological Neck

For \(t > 0\) real, the intersection of the smooth fiber \(\sum z_i^2 = t\) with the real subspace \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a sphere \(\sum x_i^2 = t\) of dimension \(n-1\). This is the vanishing cycle \(\delta\).

Section 4: Holomorphic Morse Lemma & DegenerationsLefschetz Pencils
The holomorphic Morse lemma gives us a powerful local model for the degenerations. Near an isolated critical point, the family of hypersurfaces can be written in a simple quadratic form. This is the direct complex analogue of the complex Morse lemma. By restricting our variables to the real subspace, we see a sphere of dimension n-minus-one whose radius is the square root of t. As t approaches zero, the radius shrinks, and the sphere collapses to a point at the critical value, which is why we call it the vanishing sphere.
Section 5: Monodromy & Picard-Lefschetz Formula

Vanishing Spheres and Monodromy Action

Notebook artifact: winding around a critical value \(s_j\) induces a monodromy action on the middle cohomology of the smooth fiber.

Monodromy Loop

Let \(U = \mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{s_1, \dots, s_k\}\) be the base of smooth fibers. A loop \(\gamma\) in \(U\) winding around a critical value \(s_j\) induces a homomorphism on middle cohomology: $$T : H^d(X_{t_0}, \mathbb{Z}) \to H^d(X_{t_0}, \mathbb{Z})$$

Theorem
Picard-Lefschetz Formula

For a middle-dimensional class \(\alpha \in H^d(X_{t_0}, \mathbb{Z})\) (with \(d = n-1\)), winding around a critical value with vanishing cycle \(\delta\) yields: $$T(\alpha) = \alpha + (-1)^{(d+1)(d+2)/2} (\alpha \cdot \delta) \delta$$ where \(\cdot\) is the intersection pairing on the fiber \(X_{t_0}\).

Section 5: Picard-Lefschetz FormulaLefschetz Pencils
When we wind around a critical value in the base space, the fibers undergo a topological deformation. Although the fibers are homeomorphically identified after a full loop, the identification is not the identity on homology. Instead, it is given by the Picard-Lefschetz formula. If a cycle alpha does not intersect the vanishing cycle delta, it remains completely unchanged. If it does intersect, it is shifted by a multiple of the vanishing cycle. The coefficient depends on the topological intersection number of alpha and delta. This gives us a highly explicit matrix action for the monodromy.
Section 6: Decomposition of Cohomology

Vanishing and Primitive Cohomology

Primitive Cohomology

The primitive cohomology \(H^d(X_t)_0\) consists of cohomology classes in the fiber \(X_t\) that are orthogonal to all vanishing cycles: $$H^d(X_t, \mathbb{Q})_0 = \{ \alpha \in H^d(X_t, \mathbb{Q}) \mid \alpha \cdot \delta_j = 0 \;\; \forall j \}$$ Equivalently, it is the image of the global variety cohomology \(H^d(X, \mathbb{Q}) \to H^d(X_t, \mathbb{Q})\).

Vanishing Cohomology

The vanishing cohomology \(H^d(X_t)_{\text{van}}\) is the subspace generated by the homological classes of all vanishing cycles \(\delta_j\) associated with the critical values of the pencil.

Theorem
Orthogonal Splitting

Under the intersection pairing, the middle cohomology admits an orthogonal decomposition compatible with the monodromy action: $$H^d(X_t, \mathbb{Q}) = H^d(X_t, \mathbb{Q})_0 \oplus H^d(X_t, \mathbb{Q})_{\text{van}}$$

Global Invariant

The primitive cohomology is exactly the subspace of cycles in \(H^d(X_t, \mathbb{Q})\) that are invariant under the action of the global monodromy group.

Section 6: Cohomology SplittingLefschetz Pencils
The Picard-Lefschetz formula allows us to decompose the middle cohomology of the smooth fiber into two orthogonal subspaces under the intersection pairing. The first is the primitive cohomology, which consists of classes that are invariant under the monodromy action. These classes actually come from global cohomology classes on the entire variety X. The second is the vanishing cohomology, which is the subspace spanned by the vanishing cycles. The global monodromy group acts irreducibly on the vanishing part, giving us a beautiful algebraic splitting that is compatible with the Hodge decomposition.

Common Misconceptions

Pitfalls in Lefschetz Pencil Theory

Pitfall 1 — Base Locus vs. Critical Locus
The base locus \(B = X \cap A\) is NOT where the singular fibers of the pencil occur. Generically, \(B\) is a completely smooth manifold. The critical points of the rational map occur in the complement of \(B\) and correspond to hyperplanes becoming tangent to \(X\).
Pitfall 2 — Monodromy of Simply Connected Spaces
One might expect monodromy to be trivial if the total space \(X\) is simply connected. This is false! Winding around singular fibers in the base \(\mathbb{P}^1 \setminus \{s_1, \dots, s_k\}\) produces non-trivial fundamental group actions even when \(\pi_1(X) = 0\).
Pitfall 3 — Believing Monodromy Preserves All Cycles
Monodromy does NOT act as the identity on all cycles. As the Picard-Lefschetz formula shows, only the primitive cohomology classes orthogonal to the vanishing cycle are preserved. The vanishing cycle homology is actively transformed and permuted.
Correct Diagnostic Strategy

Always split the variety into a static regime (global invariants like \(H^d(X)\)) and a dynamic regime (fiber-wise cohomology \(H^d(X_t)\)). The blowup map \(\pi\) precisely controls the transition between these two spaces.

Section 7: Misconceptions & Rigorous TrapsLefschetz Pencils
As you study Lefschetz pencils, there are a few common pitfalls to avoid. First, do not confuse the base locus of the pencil with its critical locus. The base locus is where all hyperplanes in the pencil intersect, and generically it is completely smooth. The critical points, on the other hand, are the isolated points where hyperplanes become tangent to the variety. Second, do not assume monodromy is trivial just because the total space X is simply connected. The base space of the fibration is a punctured projective line, which has a very rich fundamental group, leading to complex and non-trivial monodromy. Finally, remember that monodromy only preserves the primitive cohomology classes, while the vanishing classes are actively transformed.
