The Group Axioms

Four conditions

Closure under a product, associativity, an identity $e$, and an inverse for each element.

Topology link

Associativity lets us drop parentheses when concatenating paths or composing transformations.

Definition

$(ab)c = a(bc),\quad ea = a = ae,\quad a a^{-1} = e$

Checked by hand

On the six triangle symmetries all four axioms verify by direct enumeration.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424The Group Axioms
A group is a set with an associative product, an identity, and inverses for every element. Associativity is what lets us drop parentheses when concatenating loops or composing covering transformations. We verify all four axioms by enumeration on the six triangle symmetries, the running finite model.

Abelian Groups and Cayley Graphs

Abelian

The product commutes: $ab = ba$ for all elements. $\mathbb{Z}$ is abelian; $S_3$ is not.

Cayley graph

One arrow color per generator. Reaching every node confirms the generators generate.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Abelian Groups and Cayley Graphs
Abelian means the product commutes. The Cayley graph draws one colored arrow per generator, so generation and commutativity become visible. If the generator arrows fail to close into squares at every node, the group is nonabelian, exactly the case for the triangle symmetries where rs differs from sr.

Subgroups, Cosets, and Lagrange

Subgroup

A subset $H \le G$ closed under product and inverse, containing $e$.

Cosets

Left cosets $gH$ partition $G$ into blocks, each the same size as $H$.

Lagrange's theorem

$|G| = [G:H]\,\cdot\,|H|$

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Subgroups, Cosets, Lagrange
A subgroup is a subset closed under product and inverse. Left cosets partition the whole group into blocks all the same size as the subgroup. Counting blocks gives Lagrange's theorem: the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group, a constant background fact in covering theory.

Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms

Homomorphism

$f(ab) = f(a)f(b)$. It need not remember everything.

Isomorphism

A bijective homomorphism: the two groups are algebraically the same.

Kernel and image

$\ker f = f^{-1}(e),\quad \operatorname{im} f = f(G)$

Topology link

Induced maps $f_*$ on fundamental groups are homomorphisms; $\ker f_*$ records collapsed loops.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms
A homomorphism preserves the product; an isomorphism is a bijective one. The kernel collects everything sent to the identity, recording what the map forgets, while the image is what it reaches. Induced maps on fundamental groups are homomorphisms, so kernel and image are the invariants we will track.

Normal Subgroups and Quotient Groups

Normal

$gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g$: conjugation fixes $N$ set-wise.

Quotient

Cosets multiply by $(gN)(hN) = (gh)N$, well-defined exactly when $N$ is normal.

Kernels

Every kernel is normal, so every homomorphism builds a quotient.

Quotient group

$G/N = \{\, gN : g \in G \,\},\quad (gN)(hN) = (gh)N$

Warning

If $N$ is not normal, the coset product depends on representatives and fails.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Normal Subgroups and Quotients
A subgroup is normal when conjugation keeps it set-wise fixed. Normality is exactly the condition making coset multiplication independent of representatives, so the quotient becomes a genuine group. Kernels are always normal, which is why every homomorphism manufactures a quotient on its domain side.

The First Isomorphism Theorem

Theorem

$G/\ker f \;\cong\; \operatorname{im} f$

Two steps

Collapse the kernel to a point, then identify the quotient with the image.

Finite model

Sign map $S_3 \to \{\pm 1\}$ with $\ker = A_3$, so $S_3/A_3 \cong C_2$.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424First Isomorphism Theorem
Every homomorphism factors as a surjection onto a quotient by its kernel, followed by an isomorphism onto its image. The sign map on triangle symmetries collapses six elements to two cosets, then identifies that quotient with the two signs, a clean finite instance of the theorem.

Cyclic Groups: $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n$

One generator

A cyclic group is $\langle g \rangle = \{ g^k \}$. Infinite gives $\mathbb{Z}$; order $n$ gives $\mathbb{Z}/n$.

In topology

$\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$; lens spaces realize $\mathbb{Z}/n$. Every subgroup is again cyclic.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Cyclic Groups Z and Z mod n
A cyclic group is generated by one element: the integers under addition or the residues modulo n. They are the simplest infinite and finite groups and appear as fundamental groups of the circle and lens spaces. Every subgroup of a cyclic group is again cyclic.

Direct Products

Coordinatewise

$(a,b)(a',b') = (aa', bb')$. The order is the product of factor orders.

Coprime orders

If $\gcd(m,n)=1$ then $C_m \times C_n \cong C_{mn}$ by the Chinese remainder map.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Direct Products
The direct product multiplies coordinates independently, so its order is the product of factor orders. When the factor orders are coprime the product is itself cyclic; here the two and three element cyclic groups combine into the six element cyclic group by the Chinese remainder correspondence.

Group Actions: Orbits and Stabilizers

Action

$e \cdot x = x$ and $(gh)\cdot x = g\cdot(h\cdot x)$: group elements move points.

Orbit

$G\cdot x = \{ g\cdot x \}$ — everything reachable from $x$.

Stabilizer

$G_x = \{ g : g\cdot x = x \}$ — the subgroup fixing $x$.

Quotient spaces

$X/G:\ \text{orbits become points}$

Topology link

Free actions (trivial stabilizers) drive covering transformations and quotient manifolds.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Group Actions
An action lets group elements move points of a set: the identity fixes everything and products act in sequence. The orbit of a point is what it reaches; the stabilizer is the subgroup fixing it. Actions are the bridge from algebra to quotient spaces and covering transformations.

The Orbit-Stabilizer Relation

Counting law

$|G| = |G\cdot x|\,\cdot\,|G_x|$

Why

The cosets of the stabilizer $G_x$ correspond bijectively to points of the orbit.

Triangle action

$|S_3| = 6 = 3 \cdot 2$: orbit of vertex $1$ has size $3$, its stabilizer size $2$.

Printed pp. 401-406 · PDF 419-424Orbit-Stabilizer
The orbit-stabilizer relation says the group size equals orbit size times stabilizer size, because the stabilizer cosets index the orbit. For the triangle acting on its three vertices, six symmetries split into three fibers of two, the finite preview of degree counts for covering maps.
