What Is a Topology

The three axioms

$\varnothing,\, X \in \mathcal{T}$

$\textstyle\bigcup_{\alpha} U_\alpha \in \mathcal{T}$

$U_1 \cap \dots \cap U_n \in \mathcal{T}$

Watch the asymmetry

Unions are unrestricted; intersections must be finite.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48What Is a Topology
A topology is a chosen collection of open subsets closed under three operations. The empty set and the whole space are open, arbitrary unions stay open, and finite intersections stay open. On a finite set every union is enumerable, so these axioms become an exact closure checklist we can run by hand.

Three Standard Topologies

Discrete

Every subset is open; all points separate.

Trivial

Only $\varnothing$ and $X$ are open.

Metric / Euclidean

Open balls $B_r(x)$ generate the topology.

Metric-generated opens

$U \text{ open} \iff \forall x\in U,\ \exists r>0:\ B_r(x)\subseteq U$

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Three Standard Topologies
Every set carries at least two topologies. The discrete topology makes every subset open and separates all points. The trivial topology offers only the empty set and the whole space. The metric topology, generated by open balls, is the bridge from Euclidean geometry into the abstract open-set language used throughout the book.

Bases and Subbases

Basis conditions

$X = \bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}} B$

$x\in B_1\cap B_2 \Rightarrow \exists B_3:\ x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$

Subbasis

Any family; finite intersections form a basis, then unions generate $\mathcal{T}$.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Bases and Subbases
A basis compresses a topology into basic open sets whose unions recover every open set. Validity needs two conditions: the basic sets cover the space, and each overlap is locally refined by a smaller basic set through the point. A subbasis is even looser, generating a basis through finite intersections first.

Closed Sets

Definition

$C$ is closed when its complement $X\setminus C$ is open.

Dual axioms

$\varnothing,\, X \text{ closed}$

$\textstyle\bigcap_\alpha C_\alpha \text{ closed},\quad C_1\cup\dots\cup C_n \text{ closed}$

Where it can fail

In a non-Hausdorff space a single point need not be closed.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Closed Sets
Closed sets are exactly the complements of open sets, so the axioms dualize. The whole space and empty set are closed, arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed, and finite unions are closed. In a non-Hausdorff space a single point need not be closed, which already signals trouble for limits.

Interior, Closure, Boundary

Three derived sets

$\operatorname{int} A = \bigcup \{U \text{ open}: U\subseteq A\}$

$\overline{A} = \bigcap \{C \text{ closed}: A\subseteq C\}$

$\partial A = \overline{A}\setminus \operatorname{int} A$

Reading

Interior is largest open inside; closure is smallest closed outside.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Interior, Closure, Boundary
For a subset, the interior is the largest open set inside it, the closure is the smallest closed set containing it, and the boundary is the closure minus the interior. These three nested regions are computed by asking which neighborhoods fit inside and which neighborhoods are forced to meet the subset.

Neighborhoods

Definition

A neighborhood of $x$ is any open set containing $x$.

Specialization preorder

Set $p\le q$ when every open set around $p$ also contains $q$.

Minimal neighborhood (finite)

$U_x = \bigcap \{U \text{ open}: x\in U\}$

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Neighborhoods
A neighborhood of a point is any open set containing it. In a finite topology the intersection of all neighborhoods of a point gives a minimal open neighborhood. The specialization preorder records when every open set around one point also drags a second point along, the finite shadow of failed separation.

Continuity via Open Sets

Definition

$f:X\to Y \text{ continuous} \iff f^{-1}(V)\text{ open for every open } V\subseteq Y$

Basis shortcut

It suffices to test preimages of basis elements of $Y$.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Continuity via Open Sets
A map is continuous exactly when the preimage of every open set is open. This definition needs no metric. When the target has a basis it suffices to test preimages of basis elements, which is the workflow used throughout manifold theory where we check coordinate balls rather than every open subset.

Homeomorphisms

Definition

$f:X\to Y$ bijective, $f$ and $f^{-1}$ both continuous

Topological equivalence

Homeomorphic spaces share every open-set property.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Homeomorphisms
A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse, the isomorphism of topology. Homeomorphic spaces share every property phrased in open sets, so they are indistinguishable as topological spaces. This is the equivalence under which a coffee mug and a doughnut are genuinely the same one-holed surface.

The Hausdorff Condition

Separation axiom

$x\ne y \Rightarrow \exists\, U\ni x,\ V\ni y \text{ open},\ U\cap V=\varnothing$

Why it matters

Without it, sequences may have several limits and points may not be closed.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48The Hausdorff Condition
Hausdorffness promises that distinct points are separable by disjoint neighborhoods. Metric spaces satisfy it automatically using balls of radius below half the distance. Without it, sequences can converge to several limits and finite sets need not be closed. The Sierpinski space is the smallest finite witness of this failure.

Second Countability

Definition

$\exists\, \text{countable basis } \mathcal{B} \text{ generating } \mathcal{T}$

Consequences

Implies first countability, separability, and the Lindelof property.

Euclidean case

Balls with rational center and radius form a countable basis.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Second Countability
A space is second countable when one countable basis generates the whole topology. This single size condition implies first countability, separability, and the Lindelof property, so countable subcovers always exist. Euclidean space qualifies through balls with rational centers and rational radii, a countable family that still generates everything.

The Manifold Definition

Topological manifold

$M^n:\ \text{locally Euclidean of dim } n$

$+\ \text{Hausdorff} \ +\ \text{second countable}$

Each lock is needed

The doubled-origin line is locally line-like but not Hausdorff.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48The Manifold Definition
A topological manifold of dimension n is locally Euclidean of that fixed dimension, Hausdorff, and second countable. The three locks are independent. The doubled-origin line is locally line-like yet not Hausdorff, while finite non-discrete spaces fail the local chart test, so no single condition can be dropped.

Recap and Outlook

Open sets first

Closure, interior, continuity, and separation are all derived from them.

Bases compress

Cover plus refinement validate a generating family.

Safeguards

Hausdorff blocks nonunique limits; second countable bounds the size.

The gate

Locally Euclidean of one dimension plus both safeguards; half-spaces give boundary.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 19-48Recap and Outlook
Open sets are the primitive data from which closure, interior, continuity, and separation are derived. A basis compresses a topology; Hausdorff and second countable are structural safeguards. The manifold gate combines locally Euclidean of one dimension with both safeguards, and half-space charts extend the idea to manifolds with boundary.
