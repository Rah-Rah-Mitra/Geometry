What Is a Manifold?

A globally curved space that is Euclidean up close

Global freedom

Space may be curved, wrapped, glued, or high dimensional.

Local Euclidean

Every small neighborhood looks like flat $\mathbb{R}^n$ of one fixed $n$.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18What Is a Manifold?
This opening chapter is the runway for the course. It does not give the formal definition yet; instead it builds the mental model of a manifold as a space that may be curved, wrapped, or glued globally while every small neighborhood still looks like ordinary Euclidean space of one fixed dimension.

Locally Euclidean: The Provisional Idea

Working definition

An $n$-manifold looks, near each point, like $n$ real parameters.

Near each point

The angle on a circle is local: $0$ and $2\pi$ name the same point.

Local model

$U \subset M \;\xrightarrow{\;\varphi\;}\; \widetilde U \subset \mathbb{R}^n$

One fixed dimension

The same $n$ must work at every point of the space.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Locally Euclidean
The provisional idea is that an n-manifold looks near each point like n real parameters. The phrase near each point carries the weight. A circle is traced by an angle, but that angle is not a global coordinate because zero and two pi name the same point, so locality is essential.

A Gallery of Model Manifolds

Every entry is locally Euclidean; the global shapes differ sharply

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18A Gallery of Model Spaces
The gallery compares a circle, a sphere, a torus, and a genus-two surface. Every example carries local coordinates of a fixed dimension, yet only some admit a single global chart. Dimension Is Local Parameter Count

Curves

One parameter: the circle $S^1$ is a $1$-manifold.

Surfaces

Two parameters: sphere and torus are $2$-manifolds.

Higher

The $3$-sphere $S^3$ needs three local parameters.

Intrinsic dimension

$\dim M = n \iff \varphi(U) \subset \mathbb{R}^n$

Ambient is irrelevant

Dimension counts local parameters, not the space we drew it in.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Dimension as Parameter Count
Dimension is the number of independent parameters a local chart records. A circle needs one, a surface needs two, and the three-sphere can be described locally by three. Dimension is intrinsic; it does not depend on the dimension of the ambient space in which we happened to draw the object.

A Chart Is a Reversible Measurement

Assign coordinates

A chart $\varphi$ sends a neighborhood $U$ to Euclidean coordinates.

And invert them

The inverse $\varphi^{-1}$ rebuilds points from their coordinates.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Coordinate Charts
A chart is a reversible measurement device. It assigns Euclidean coordinates to points in a neighborhood and reconstructs those points from the coordinates. The goal of the whole subject is to do calculus and topology locally, exactly as we already know how to do them on flat Euclidean space.

Stereographic Projection: A Concrete Chart

Projection from the north pole

$\varphi(x,y,z) = \dfrac{(x,y)}{1-z}$

Reversible

Defined and invertible on the whole sphere except $N$.

Flat grid to patch

A two-parameter plane grid maps onto a curved surface patch.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Stereographic Projection
Stereographic projection makes a chart concrete. Remove one pole, draw a line from it through a point of the sphere, and record where that line meets a flat plane. The map is reversible away from the pole, so a flat two-parameter grid becomes a curved patch covering the sphere minus a single point.

Atlases: One Chart Is Not Enough

The gap

Stereographic projection always misses its pole.

The fix

Use two charts, one from each pole; their domains cover $S^2$.

Definition by atlas

A manifold is given by enough compatible charts, not one global system.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Atlases: Why One Chart Fails
No single chart covers the whole sphere, since stereographic projection always misses its pole. A manifold is therefore defined not by one universal coordinate system but by an atlas, a collection of enough compatible local charts whose domains together cover the entire space without gaps.

Homeomorphism: Provisional Sameness

Definition

$f:X \to Y$ continuous, bijective, $f^{-1}$ continuous

Ignores geometry

Length, angle, and curvature are not preserved.

Preserves topology

Connectedness, holes, and compactness survive.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Homeomorphism
Homeomorphism is the chapter's provisional notion of sameness: a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. It ignores metric accidents such as length, angle, and curvature. That is why a cube boundary and a round sphere are the same topological surface even though one has flat faces and sharp edges.

Invariants Certify Non-Sameness

Proving sameness

Exhibit one explicit homeomorphism; pictures can suffice.

Proving difference

Pictures fail; you need data no homeomorphism changes.

Invariant logic

$I(X) \ne I(Y) \;\Rightarrow\; X \not\cong Y$

Checkable

A negative claim becomes a finite comparison of signatures.

Examples

Euler characteristic, first Betti number, orientability.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Topological Invariants
To prove two spaces are not homeomorphic, pictures fail and invariants succeed. An invariant is data no homeomorphism can change, turning the negative claim into a checkable comparison. If two spaces disagree on an invariant, no homeomorphism between them can exist, which is exactly what we want to certify.

Euler Characteristic Separates Surfaces

Orientable surfaces

$\chi = 2 - 2g, \qquad b_1 = 2g$

Reads the handles

$g$ counts handles; $b_1$ counts independent loop directions.

Geometry-blind

Sphere and cube boundary share $\chi = 2$.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Euler Characteristic
Euler characteristic is a coarse invariant for compact surfaces. For orientable surfaces it satisfies chi equals two minus twice the genus, linking it to the number of handles. The sphere scores two, the torus zero, and the genus-two surface minus two, immediately separating these surfaces by an invariant.

Classifying Compact Surfaces

Orientable family

Sphere with $g$ handles; $\chi = 2 - 2g$.

Nonorientable family

Sphere with $k$ crosscaps; $\chi = 2 - k$.

Signature

Orientability plus $\chi$ pins down the surface.

Classification pattern

standard list $+$ invariant formula $+$ sanity checks

Examples

$\chi=1$ is the projective plane; $\chi=0$ nonorientable is the Klein bottle.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Classifying Surfaces
A classification pairs a standard list with a checkable signature. Orientable compact surfaces are spheres with g handles; nonorientable ones are spheres with k crosscaps. Knowing orientability and Euler characteristic pins down the surface, demonstrating the pattern of standard family plus invariant formula that the course will later prove rigorously.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds · printed pp. 1-18Roadmap and Takeaways
Chapter one names the recurring pattern the book makes precise. Local Euclidean structure leads to homeomorphism questions, invariants certify non-homeomorphism, and these feed classification. Ahead lie topological spaces, compactness, connectedness, fundamental groups, covering spaces, cell complexes, and the full classification of surfaces, each replacing a node of today's scaffold.
