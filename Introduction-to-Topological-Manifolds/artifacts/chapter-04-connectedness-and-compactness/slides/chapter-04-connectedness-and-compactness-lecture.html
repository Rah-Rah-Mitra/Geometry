Connectedness and Compactness

Two global tests that make manifold topology work

Connectedness

No nontrivial clopen yes/no split of the space.

Compactness

Every open cover hides a finite subcover.

Printed pp. 85-126Connectedness and Compactness
This chapter spans printed pages 85 through 126 and turns two global questions into working tests. Connectedness asks whether a space admits a nontrivial clopen yes/no split, and compactness asks whether every open cover hides a finite subcover. Both ideas become practical tools once we specialize them to manifolds.

What Connectedness Forbids

Separation

No split $X = U \cup V$ into disjoint nonempty open sets.

Clopen test

Only $\varnothing$ and $X$ are open and closed.

Discrete probe

$f:X \to \{0,1\}$ continuous $\Rightarrow$ $f$ constant

Why

The preimage $f^{-1}(1)$ would be a nontrivial clopen set.

Printed pp. 85-126What Connectedness Forbids
A space is connected when it cannot be cut into two disjoint nonempty open pieces. The working version is the clopen test: only the empty set and the whole space are simultaneously open and closed. Equivalently, every continuous map into a discrete two-point space must be constant on a connected domain.

Components and Discrete Probes

Components

Maximal connected subsets; they partition $X$.

Label count

Locally constant binary labels number $2^{k}$ for $k$ components.

Printed pp. 85-126Components and Discrete Probes
Components are the maximal connected subsets, and they partition the space. A locally constant binary label corresponds to a continuous map into the two-point discrete space, so the number of such labels equals two raised to the component count. On a connected space only the two constant labels survive.

Continuous Images Stay Connected

Preservation

If $X$ is connected and $f$ continuous, $f(X)$ is connected.

Consequence

The image of an interval is an interval.

Intermediate value theorem

$f(a) < c < f(b) \;\Rightarrow\; \exists\, x,\ f(x)=c$

Reading

No disconnection can be created by a continuous map.

Printed pp. 85-126Continuous Images Stay Connected
Continuous maps cannot manufacture a disconnection, so the image of a connected space is connected. Applied to a real-valued map on an interval this is exactly the intermediate value theorem: the image is an interval, so every intermediate value is attained. This single preservation theorem powers many later arguments.

Path Connectedness

Definition

A path is continuous $\gamma:[0,1] \to X$ joining two points.

Easy cases

Convex sets use straight segments; punctured planes detour.

Implication

path-connected $\;\Rightarrow\;$ connected

Caution

The converse fails in general spaces.

Printed pp. 85-126Path Connectedness
A path is a continuous map from the unit interval, so path connectedness is constructive: exhibit a path and two points share a path component. Convex Euclidean sets use straight segments, and punctured planes detour around the hole. Path connectedness always implies connectedness, but the converse can fail.

The Topologist's Sine Curve

Connected

Closure of a connected graph stays connected.

Not path-connected

No path leaves the segment for the oscillating graph.

Printed pp. 85-126The Topologist's Sine Curve
The closure of the oscillating graph together with the vertical segment is connected because closure preserves connectedness. Yet no path can leave the segment and reach the graph, since the oscillations compress without bound near the axis. This is the canonical example separating connectedness from path connectedness.

Local Connectedness on Manifolds

Local test

Every point has small connected and path-connected neighborhoods.

Charts

Balls and half-balls are path-connected.

On manifolds

components $\;=\;$ path components

Payoff

Prove connectedness by building paths.

Printed pp. 85-126Local Connectedness on Manifolds
Local connectedness and local path connectedness require small connected or path-connected neighborhoods at every point. Euclidean balls and half-balls satisfy both, so manifolds inherit them. In a locally path-connected space path components are open and coincide with components, which is why path construction proves connectedness inside the manifold category.

Compactness via Finite Subcovers

Definition

Every open cover has a finite subcover.

Failure

$(0,1)$ covered by $(1/n,1)$: no finite piece reaches $0$.

Printed pp. 85-126Compactness via Finite Subcovers
A space is compact when every open cover admits a finite subcover, converting infinite local data into a finite certificate. The closed interval passes because any cover can be reduced to finitely many sets, while the open interval cover by intervals one over n through one fails: every finite piece misses a point near zero.

Heine-Borel and Products

Heine-Borel

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, compact means closed and bounded.

Finite products

A product of finitely many compact spaces is compact.

Building block

$K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ compact $\iff$ $K$ closed and bounded

Tychonoff (finite)

Closed boxes are products of closed intervals.

Printed pp. 85-126Heine-Borel and Products
In Euclidean space compactness is exactly closed and bounded, the Heine-Borel theorem. Finite products of compact spaces are compact, the finite Tychonoff case, so closed boxes are compact and serve as the building blocks for the Heine-Borel characterization of compact subsets of n-dimensional Euclidean space.

Compact Images and Closed Maps

Preservation

Continuous image of a compact space is compact.

Extreme values

A continuous real function on a compact space attains its bounds.

Closed-map lemma

$X$ compact, $Y$ Hausdorff $\Rightarrow$ $f$ closed

Use

Recognize quotient maps, embeddings, homeomorphisms.

Printed pp. 85-126Compact Images and Closed Maps
Continuous images of compact spaces are compact, giving the extreme value theorem: a continuous real function on a compact space attains its maximum and minimum. Compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed, so a continuous map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is closed, the lemma behind quotient and embedding recognition.

Local Compactness and Proper Maps

Local compactness

A basis of precompact neighborhoods; charts supply it.

Half-balls

Boundary charts have compact closures too.

Proper map

$K$ compact $\Rightarrow$ $f^{-1}(K)$ compact

Contrast

$x \mapsto x^2$ proper; $x \mapsto \sin x$ not proper.

Printed pp. 85-126Local Compactness and Proper Maps
Local compactness asks for a basis of precompact neighborhoods, which manifold charts supply through balls and boundary half-balls whose closures are compact. A proper map pulls compact sets back to compact sets; the squaring map is proper while the sine map is not, since one fiber has an unbounded preimage escaping to infinity.

Recap and Invariants

Connectedness

No nontrivial clopen split; preserved by continuous images.

Compactness

Finite subcovers; preserved by images and closed subsets.

On manifolds

Connected equals path-connected; charts give local compactness.

Engines

Heine-Borel, closed-map lemma, proper maps.

Printed pp. 85-126Recap and Invariants
Connectedness is the absence of a nontrivial clopen decision, and path connectedness strengthens it but agrees with it on manifolds. Compactness turns infinite covers into finite certificates, survives continuous images and closed subsets, and through Heine-Borel, the closed-map lemma, and proper maps it drives the structural theorems of later chapters.
