Polygonal Presentations

A disk with named edges

Take a polygon, label and orient each boundary edge.

Pair and glue

Each label appears twice; identify mates by affine maps.

Locally Euclidean

Corner wedges fan into a disk, so the quotient is a surface.

Edge word

$W = a\,b\,a^{-1}\,b^{-1}$

Printed pp. 159-182Polygonal Presentations
A polygonal presentation is a disk whose boundary edges are named and paired, then glued by affine maps. The cyclic edge word records the gluing recipe. Each label appears exactly twice, and the quotient is locally Euclidean everywhere, even where many polygon corners fuse into one point.

Reading a Schema

Faces

One polygon gives one $2$-cell: $F = 1$.

Edges

Each label pair is one $1$-cell, so $E$ counts the labels.

Vertices

Union-find on corners gives the quotient vertex classes $V$.

Torus count

$V=1,\; E=2,\; F=1 \;\Rightarrow\; \chi = 0$

Printed pp. 159-182Reading a Schema
From an edge word we extract finite data: one two-cell, one one-cell per label pair, and vertex classes found by union-find on the corners. Counting these gives the Euler characteristic directly. The torus word a b a-inverse b-inverse identifies all four corners to a single vertex.

Orientability From Edge Pairs

Complementary pair

$a \dots a^{-1}$: one forward, one reversed. Orientation survives.

Twisted pair

$a \dots a$: both same direction. Orientation flips.

The flag

Orientable $\iff$ no pair is twisted.

Printed pp. 159-182Orientability
Orientability is a combinatorial flag in this model. A pair is complementary when its label appears once forward and once reversed, and twisted when both occurrences run the same way. A presentation is orientable exactly when no pair is twisted, so a consistent face orientation survives all gluings.

The Standard List

Sphere

$aa^{-1}, \quad \chi = 2$

Orientable genus $g$

$\prod_{i=1}^{g} a_i b_i a_i^{-1} b_i^{-1}$

Nonorientable, $k$ crosscaps

$c_1 c_1 \cdots c_k c_k$

Printed pp. 159-182The Standard Surfaces
The standard list has three columns: the sphere with word a a-inverse, the orientable genus-g surface built from g commutator handle blocks, and the nonorientable surface built from k twisted crosscap pairs. Every compact connected surface, the theorem promises, is homeomorphic to exactly one of these models.

The Connected Sum

Cut

Remove an open disk from each of $M$ and $N$.

Glue

Identify the two new boundary circles.

Words

For one-face schemas: concatenate over disjoint alphabets.

Euler rule

$\chi(M \# N) = \chi(M) + \chi(N) - 2$

Printed pp. 159-182Connected Sum
Connected sum removes an open disk from each surface and glues the resulting boundary circles. For one-face presentations this is just concatenating the two edge words over disjoint alphabets. Geometrically it splices two surfaces into one, and it is the operation that manufactures every higher-genus member of the list.

Two Tori Make Genus Two

Cut each torus

Excise a small disk from each one-handled torus.

Splice

Glue the boundary circles into one two-handled surface.

Resulting word

$a_1 b_1 a_1^{-1} b_1^{-1}\, a_2 b_2 a_2^{-1} b_2^{-1}$

Printed pp. 159-182Genus-2 by Connected Sum
Two tori, each cut along a small circle, glue along those circles to produce the genus-two surface with two handles. The edge word becomes the two commutator blocks written in sequence. This picture is the prototype: adding a handle is exactly adding one more commutator block to the presentation word.

Euler Characteristic

Definition

$\chi = V - E + F$

Invariant under moves

Elementary transformations cancel: $dV - dE + dF = 0$.

Sum lowers it

Each connected sum subtracts two from $\chi$.

Printed pp. 159-182Euler Characteristic
The Euler characteristic V minus E plus F is the chapter's first fast invariant. Elementary transformations on a presentation change vertex, edge, and face counts in amounts that always cancel, so chi is preserved. Connected sum subtracts two each time, which is exactly how each handle or crosscap lowers chi.

Genus From Euler Characteristic

Orientable

$\chi = 2 - 2g$

Nonorientable

$\chi = 2 - k$

Recover the count

Genus $g$ or crosscap number $k$ reads off directly from $\chi$.

Printed pp. 159-182The Two Formulas
For the standard families the Euler characteristic collapses to two clean formulas: chi equals two minus two g for the orientable genus-g surface, and chi equals two minus k for the connected sum of k projective planes. Genus or crosscap count is therefore recoverable directly from chi.

The Klein Bottle

Word

$abab^{-1}, \quad \chi = 0, \text{ nonorientable}$

Decomposes

$K \cong \mathbb{RP}^2 \,\#\, \mathbb{RP}^2$ by a cutting argument.

No mixed family

$T^2 \# \mathbb{RP}^2 \cong \mathbb{RP}^2 \# \mathbb{RP}^2 \# \mathbb{RP}^2$.

Printed pp. 159-182Klein Bottle and Crosscaps
The Klein bottle, word a b a b-inverse, is nonorientable with Euler characteristic zero, and a cutting argument shows it equals the connected sum of two projective planes. Likewise a torus summed with a projective plane equals three projective planes, so no mixed orientable-nonorientable family is ever needed.

The Classification Theorem

Theorem

Every compact connected surface is homeomorphic to the sphere, a connected sum of tori, or a connected sum of projective planes.

Reduction algorithm

Triangulate, paste to one face, cancel, gather, consolidate, isolate blocks.

Printed pp. 159-182The Classification Theorem
The proof is a reduction algorithm. Triangulate, paste into one face, cancel adjacent complementary pairs, gather twisted pairs, consolidate the vertices to one class, and isolate commutator handle blocks. The result is always a sphere, a sum of tori, or a sum of projective planes, exhausting the standard list.

Invariants and Outlook

Two invariants suffice

$\chi$ and orientability label each presentation.

Separating $\chi = 0$

Orientable gives the torus; nonorientable gives the Klein bottle.

Deferred proof

Chapter 10's homology makes the invariants genuinely topological.

Printed pp. 159-182Invariants and Outlook
Euler characteristic plus orientability together separate the standard surfaces at the presentation level: chi zero with orientability is the torus, chi zero without it is the Klein bottle. Chapter ten supplies the homology that proves these invariants are genuinely topological, completing the classification's distinctness claim.
