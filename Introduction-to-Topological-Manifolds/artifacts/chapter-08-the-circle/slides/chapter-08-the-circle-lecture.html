The Circle

Computing the first nontrivial fundamental group: $\pi_1(S^1,1)\cong\mathbb{Z}$

The obstruction

No continuous global angle exists on $S^1$; angle is only a local coordinate.

The method

Unwrap with a covering map, then count turns of a lifted loop.

The payoff

Winding number gives degree, fixed-point theorems, and the algebra of loops.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232The Circle
This chapter delivers the first nontrivial fundamental group of the course. The angle on the circle is only a local coordinate, so we unwrap it with the exponential covering map and convert loop homotopy into ordinary endpoint arithmetic on the real line, producing the isomorphism with the integers.

The Exponential Covering Map

Definition

$\varepsilon:\mathbb{R}\to S^1,\qquad \varepsilon(r)=e^{2\pi i r}$

Periodicity

$\varepsilon(r+n)=\varepsilon(r)$ for every integer $n$: one unit of $r$ is one full turn.

Fiber

$\varepsilon^{-1}(1)=\mathbb{Z}$. Each circle point sits below a whole integer-spaced fiber.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232The Exponential Covering Map
The exponential map sends a real turn count to a point on the unit circle, and a unit shift in the source returns the same point. This periodicity is exactly why no continuous global angle exists, and it makes the real line an unwrapped model carrying infinitely many copies of every short arc.

Evenly Covered Arcs and Local Sections

Evenly covered

$\varepsilon^{-1}(U)=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} I_n$

Each $I_n$ maps homeomorphically onto the arc $U$.

Local section

Invert one sheet to get a continuous branch of angle $\sigma:U\to\mathbb{R}$ with $\varepsilon\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}$.

Caution

Branches exist only on arcs that do not wrap all the way around.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Evenly Covered Arcs
A short open arc pulls back to a disjoint union of intervals, and on each interval the exponential map restricts to a homeomorphism onto the arc. Inverting one such restriction gives a continuous local section, a single branch of angle valid on the arc but never on the whole circle at once.

Path Lifting

Path lifting property

$f:I\to S^1,\ \ \tilde f:I\to\mathbb{R},\ \ \varepsilon\circ\tilde f=f$

Initial data

Choose $\tilde f(0)$ in the fiber over $f(0)$; the rest of $\tilde f$ is then forced.

Meaning

The lift is a continuous running angle in turns, recording rotation the circle forgets.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Path Lifting
Fix a real point above the starting circle point, and the path lifting property produces a unique continuous lift of the whole path. The lift is a running angle record measured in turns. Because the exponential map cannot see integer shifts, the lift remembers accumulated rotation that the circle itself discards.

Unique Lifting via Connectedness

Uniqueness lemma

If $\tilde f_1,\tilde f_2$ lift $f$ on a connected domain and agree at one point, then $\tilde f_1=\tilde f_2$.

Agreement is open

Near a point of agreement both lifts share a sheet; local injectivity keeps them equal.

Agreement is closed

Near a disagreement the lifts sit in distinct sheets and stay apart.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Unique Lifting
Two lifts of one map that agree at a single point must agree everywhere on a connected domain. The agreement set is open because nearby points share a sheet, and closed because disagreement also persists locally. Connectedness forces an all-or-nothing verdict, which is the engine behind every uniqueness claim in the chapter.

Homotopy Lifting

Homotopy lifting property

$H:I\times I\to S^1\ \Rightarrow\ \tilde H:I\times I\to\mathbb{R}$

Compactness

Finitely many evenly covered arcs cover each time track; lift strip by strip.

Gluing

Uniqueness makes overlapping local lifts agree, so the pieces glue continuously.

Stationary endpoints

Fixed endpoints below stay fixed above: the integer is homotopy invariant.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Homotopy Lifting
Homotopies lift too, because the compact time interval is covered by finitely many evenly covered arcs that we lift strip by strip and glue using uniqueness. Endpoints held fixed downstairs stay fixed upstairs, so the lifted endpoint difference is a genuine invariant of the path homotopy class, not of one drawing.

The Winding Number

Definition

$N(f)=\tilde f(1)-\tilde f(0)\in\mathbb{Z}$

Lift-independent

Another lift shifts both endpoints by the same constant; the difference is unchanged.

Homotopy-invariant

Path homotopy with fixed endpoints lifts to one with fixed endpoints, so $N$ is constant on classes.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Winding Number
For a loop both lift endpoints lie in one fiber, so their difference is an integer called the winding number. Choosing a different lift only shifts both endpoints by a constant, leaving the difference unchanged, and homotopy lifting shows the integer is stable across the entire path homotopy class of the loop.

$\pi_1(S^1,1)\cong\mathbb{Z}$

Theorem

$N:\pi_1(S^1,1)\to\mathbb{Z}$ is a group isomorphism.

Generator

$\omega(s)=e^{2\pi i s}$ lifts to $s$, so $N(\omega)=1$ and $\alpha_n(s)=e^{2\pi i n s}$ gives $N=n$.

Homomorphism

Concatenation adds endpoint differences: $N(f\cdot g)=N(f)+N(g)$.

Both directions

$n\mapsto[\omega]^n,\qquad [f]\mapsto N(f)$

These maps invert each other, so the fundamental group is infinite cyclic.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232The Isomorphism
The standard once-around loop has lift the identity, so its winding number is one, and the n-fold loop lifts to n times the parameter. Concatenation adds endpoint differences, so winding number is a homomorphism that pairs with raising the generator to powers, giving the isomorphism between the fundamental group and the integers.

Degree of Maps $S^1\to S^1$

Definition

$\deg\varphi=N(\varphi\circ\omega)\in\mathbb{Z}$

Examples

Power map $z^n$ has degree $n$; conjugation $\bar z$ has degree $-1$; rotation has degree $1$.

Multiplicative

$\deg(\psi\circ\varphi)=\deg\psi\cdot\deg\varphi$, and degree is a homotopy invariant.

Consequences

Nonzero degree forces surjectivity; degree $\ne 1$ forces a fixed point.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Degree of Circle Maps
Every self-map of the circle carries one integer, the winding number of its composite with the standard loop, called its degree. Power maps have degree equal to their exponent, conjugation has degree minus one, rotations have degree one, and degree multiplies under composition while staying invariant under homotopy of the map.

No Retraction and Brouwer

No-retraction theorem

There is no retraction $r:\overline{\mathbb{D}^2}\to S^1$ fixing the boundary circle.

Proof sketch

A retraction would induce an injection $\mathbb{Z}=\pi_1(S^1)\hookrightarrow\pi_1(\mathbb{D}^2)=0$, which is impossible.

Brouwer in dim 2

Any continuous $g:\overline{\mathbb{D}^2}\to\overline{\mathbb{D}^2}$ has a fixed point.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232No Retraction and Brouwer
If the disk retracted onto its boundary circle, the boundary inclusion would induce an injection on fundamental groups, sending the integers into the trivial group, which is impossible. This no-retraction theorem immediately yields the two-dimensional Brouwer fixed point theorem by the standard ray-construction contradiction argument.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Theorem

Every nonconstant $p(z)=z^n+\cdots\in\mathbb{C}[z]$ has a complex root.

Large circle

On $|z|=R$ large, the leading term dominates, so $p$ winds $n$ times about $0$.

Small circle

On $|z|=\varepsilon$ small, $p$ stays near $p(0)$, so it winds $0$ times.

Contradiction

If $p$ never vanished, winding would be constant; $n\ne 0$ forces a root.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
A complex polynomial of positive degree restricted to a large circle winds around the origin as many times as its degree, while on a tiny circle it winds like its constant term. If the polynomial never vanished, the winding number would be homotopy invariant and constant, contradicting these two counts and forcing a root.

Recap and Outlook

The machine

Covering map $\Rightarrow$ evenly covered arcs $\Rightarrow$ lifts $\Rightarrow$ uniqueness $\Rightarrow$ homotopy lifting.

The invariant

Winding number gives $\pi_1(S^1,1)\cong\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the once-around loop.

Applications

Degree theory, the no-retraction theorem, Brouwer, and the fundamental theorem of algebra.

Looking ahead

The same integer reappears in punctured planes, tori, and higher degree theory.

Lee, Introduction to Topological Manifolds — printed pp. 217-232Recap and Outlook
The circle teaches the covering-space method and a single integer invariant that resurfaces everywhere. Evenly covered arcs supply lifts, connectedness supplies uniqueness, compactness supplies homotopy lifting, and the winding number supplies the isomorphism. The same integer becomes degree, drives fixed-point theorems, and previews higher-dimensional topology to come.
