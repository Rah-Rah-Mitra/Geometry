Singular Simplices and Chains

Singular simplex

A continuous map of the standard simplex; folding allowed.

Chain

A finite integer combination of such maps.

Chain group

$C_p(X) = \bigoplus_{\sigma} \mathbb{Z}\,\sigma,\quad \sigma:\Delta^p \to X$

Why permissive

Algebra needs ordered vertices, not embeddings.

Printed pp. 339-380Singular Simplices and Chains
A singular p-simplex is any continuous map from the standard simplex, allowed to fold or collapse. Free integer combinations of these maps form the chain group C_p. The geometry need not embed cleanly; the algebra only needs ordered vertices so each face carries a definite sign.

The Boundary Operator

Definition

$\partial_p\sigma = \sum_{i=0}^{p}(-1)^i\,\sigma|_{[v_0,\dots,\hat{v_i},\dots,v_p]}$

Sign rule

Drop one vertex at a time; alternate the sign.

Printed pp. 339-380The Boundary Operator
The boundary operator takes the alternating sum of a simplex restricted to its faces, dropping one vertex at a time. The triangle here shows oriented edges whose signed sum cancels at shared vertices. This single alternating-sign rule drives every later cancellation argument in the chapter.

Boundary of a Boundary is Zero

Key identity

Every codimension-two face appears twice with opposite signs.

Consequence

$\operatorname{im}\partial_{p+1}\subseteq \ker\partial_p$.

Theorem

$\partial_{p-1}\circ\partial_p = 0$

Why it matters

This is the algebraic permission to form the quotient.

Printed pp. 339-380Boundary of a Boundary is Zero
Applying the boundary twice gives zero because every codimension-two face appears exactly twice with opposite signs. This identity makes image of one boundary sit inside the kernel of the next, which is the algebraic permission to form the homology quotient. The matrix product is exactly zero.

Cycles, Boundaries, and Homology

Cycle

$z\in\ker\partial_p$: no remaining boundary.

Boundary

$b\in\operatorname{im}\partial_{p+1}$: produced by a higher chain.

Printed pp. 339-380Cycles, Boundaries, Homology
Cycles are chains with zero boundary; boundaries are chains produced by a higher chain. Since boundaries lie inside cycles, the quotient kernel modulo image is well defined. The n-th homology group records cycles that survive after every higher-dimensional filling has been subtracted away as trivial.

Functoriality

Induced map

$f:X\to Y$ sends $\sigma$ to $f\circ\sigma$.

Commutes

Composition commutes with face restriction.

Chain map

$f_\# \,\partial = \partial\, f_\# \;\Rightarrow\; f_*:H_p(X)\to H_p(Y)$

Invariance

Homeomorphisms give inverse isomorphisms.

Printed pp. 339-380Functoriality
A continuous map sends each singular simplex to its composite, and composing commutes with face restriction. So the induced chain map carries cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries, descending to homology. Homeomorphisms thus yield inverse isomorphisms, which is precisely why homology is a topological invariant.

Homotopy Invariance

Chain homotopy

$\partial h + h\,\partial = G_\# - F_\#$

On a cycle

$\partial z = 0$ kills the side term: $G,F$ agree in $H_p$.

Printed pp. 339-380Homotopy Invariance
Homotopic maps induce equal homology maps. The proof triangulates the prism swept out by a homotopy; its side faces are exactly the correction term in the chain-homotopy identity. On a cycle the side correction vanishes, so the two images differ only by a boundary and agree in homology.

$H_0$ Counts Path Components

Points are cycles

Every 0-chain has zero boundary.

Paths glue

A 1-chain identifies its two endpoints.

First computation

$H_0(X)\cong \mathbb{Z}^{\,\#\{\text{path components}\}}$

Reduced version

$\tilde H_0$ drops one $\mathbb{Z}$ for connected spaces.

Printed pp. 339-380H0 Counts Components
Zero-dimensional homology is free abelian on the path components of the space. Every point is a cycle, and a path between two points is a one-chain whose boundary identifies them. Thus H_0 has rank equal to the number of path components, the first concrete homology computation.

Homology of Spheres

Sharp answer

$\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0$ and $n$, else $0$.

Consequences

Invariance of dimension; Brouwer; hairy ball.

Theorem

$\tilde H_k(S^n)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z} & k=n\\ 0 & k\neq n\end{cases}$

Benchmark

Every method in this chapter must reproduce it.

Printed pp. 339-380Homology of Spheres
The n-sphere has homology Z in degrees zero and n and zero otherwise. This sharp result distinguishes spheres of different dimensions, proves invariance of dimension, and yields Brouwer fixed point and the hairy ball theorem. It is the benchmark every computational technique in the chapter must reproduce.

Mayer-Vietoris

Long exact sequence

$\cdots\to H_p(U\cap V)\to H_p(U)\oplus H_p(V)\to H_p(X)\xrightarrow{\partial_*} H_{p-1}(U\cap V)\to\cdots$

On the circle

The two overlap components birth $H_1(S^1)=\mathbb{Z}$.

Printed pp. 339-380Mayer-Vietoris
Mayer-Vietoris glues two open pieces into a long exact sequence linking their homology, the overlap, and the union. On the circle the overlap of two arcs has two components; their difference lies in a kernel that the connecting map fills, creating the one-dimensional loop class out of local data.

Hurewicz: $H_1$ and $\pi_1$

The bridge

$H_1$ is the abelianization of $\pi_1$.

Trade-off

Forget loop order; gain a computable group.

Hurewicz (degree 1)

$H_1(X)\cong \pi_1(X)\,/\,[\pi_1(X),\pi_1(X)]$

Example

Figure-eight: $\pi_1$ is free on two; $H_1=\mathbb{Z}^2$.

Printed pp. 339-380Hurewicz: H1 and pi1
For a path-connected space, first homology is the abelianization of the fundamental group. Homology forgets the noncommutative order in which loops are traversed but gains an abelian group that linear algebra can compute. This is the precise bridge between Chapter twelve's pi_1 and the homology of this chapter.

Euler Characteristic

Two formulas

Alternating cell count equals alternating Betti sum.

Torsion

Rank zero, so it never contributes.

Identity

$\chi(X)=\sum_{n}(-1)^n c_n = \sum_{n}(-1)^n b_n$

Why stable

Boundaries shuffle rank; the alternating total cancels.

Printed pp. 339-380Euler Characteristic
The Euler characteristic is the alternating sum of cell counts and equally the alternating sum of Betti numbers. Boundary maps shuffle rank between adjacent degrees, but exact cancellation keeps the alternating total invariant. Torsion has rank zero, so it never contributes, which is why surfaces are classified by this single integer.

Recap and Outlook

Core mechanism

  • $\partial\circ\partial=0$ defines $H_n=\ker\partial_n/\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}$.
  • Functorial and homotopy invariant.

Computations

  • $H_0$ counts components; $H_1$ abelianizes $\pi_1$.
  • $\tilde H_*(S^n)=\mathbb{Z}$ in degree $n$.

Assembly tools

  • Mayer-Vietoris glues local pieces.
  • $\chi$ as an alternating Betti sum.

Next

Cohomology reverses every arrow via $\operatorname{Hom}(-,G)$.

Printed pp. 339-380Recap and Outlook
Homology begins with the boundary-of-a-boundary identity and ends with a computable functor: H_0 counts components, H_1 abelianizes pi_1, spheres give Z in their own degree, Mayer-Vietoris assembles global classes, and the Euler characteristic survives as an alternating Betti sum. Cohomology will later reverse every arrow.
