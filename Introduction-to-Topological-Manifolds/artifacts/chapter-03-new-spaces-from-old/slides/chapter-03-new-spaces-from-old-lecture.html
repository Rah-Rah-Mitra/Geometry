The Four Constructions at a Glance

Subspace $S\subset X$

Open in $S$ means $S\cap V$ for $V$ open in $X$.

Product $X_1\times\cdots\times X_n$

Continuous in iff every coordinate map is continuous.

Disjoint union $\coprod X_i$

Continuous out iff every component restriction is continuous.

Quotient $X/{\sim}$

$V$ open iff $q^{-1}(V)$ open; only saturated sets are seen.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84The Four Constructions
Subspaces clip ambient opens through a subset, products test continuity coordinatewise, disjoint unions tag points by their source piece, and quotients see only saturated sets. Reading the four constructions side by side highlights a shared theme: continuity questions reduce to canonical maps, so each construction is a continuity gate, not a list of opens.

Subspaces: Openness Is Relative

Subspace topology

$U \subseteq S$ open $\iff U = S \cap V,\ V$ open in $X$

Relative, not absolute

$[0,1]$ is open inside $S=[0,1]\cup(2,3)$: the ambient $(-1,2)$ cuts it out.

Always name the ambient

In $\{1/n\}$ each point is isolated, yet $0$ is still a limit point in $\mathbb{R}$.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Subspaces: Openness Is Relative
In the subspace topology a set is open exactly when it equals the intersection of the subset with an ambient open set. Relative openness is genuinely different: the closed interval becomes open inside a disconnected ambient set, and isolated points appear that were not isolated before, so closures and neighborhoods must always name their ambient space.

Topological Embeddings: Injective Is Not Enough

Embedding

$f:X\to Y$ continuous, injective, and a homeomorphism onto $f(X)$

Yes: the parabola

$s\mapsto(s,s^2)$. First projection recovers $s$ continuously on the image.

No: wrapping the circle

$s\mapsto e^{2\pi i s}$ on $[0,1)$ is continuous and injective, but the inverse tears.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Embeddings: Injective Is Not Enough
A topological embedding is a continuous injection that is a homeomorphism onto its image. The parabola map qualifies because the first projection inverts it continuously. The half-open interval wrapping around the circle is continuous and injective yet fails, because parameters near one map near the image of zero, so the inverse tears nearby image points apart.

Characteristic Properties: Continuity Gates

Initial: maps in

Subspace reduces $Y\to S$ to $Y\to X$. Product reduces $Y\to\prod X_i$ to coordinate maps.

Final: maps out

Disjoint union reduces to restrictions; quotient reduces $X/{\sim}\to Z$ to a class-constant map $X\to Z$.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Characteristic Properties
Each construction carries a characteristic property that recognizes it by mapping behavior. Subspaces and products are initial: maps into them test after composing with inclusion or projections. Disjoint unions and quotients are final: maps out of them test by restricting to summands or composing with the quotient map. These gates organize every continuity proof in the chapter.

Products: Coordinates Are Tests

Characteristic property

$f:Y\to\prod X_i$ continuous $\iff \pi_i\circ f$ continuous for all $i$

Weakest such topology

The product topology is the coarsest making every projection $\pi_i$ continuous — no extra tests.

Familiar shadow

A vector-valued map is continuous iff all its scalar components are.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Products: Coordinates Are Tests
For a finite product, a map in is continuous exactly when every coordinate function is continuous, because basic opens are product rectangles. The product topology is the weakest making all projections continuous, so it adds no test beyond the coordinate tests. This is the familiar componentwise continuity of vector-valued functions, made topological.

Infinite Products: Product vs. Box

Product topology

A basic open restricts only finitely many coordinates; the rest run free.

Box topology

Every coordinate may be restricted at once — far too many opens.

The diagonal test

$d(t)=(t,t,t,\dots)$, $\ d^{-1}\!\big(\textstyle\prod_n(-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n)\big)=\{0\}$

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Infinite Products Need Care
In the product topology a basic open restricts only finitely many coordinates; the box topology restricts all at once. The diagonal map into countably many copies of the line is continuous for the product topology but not the box topology, where the shrinking box around zero has preimage a single point. Componentwise continuity characterizes the product, not the box.

Disjoint Unions: Keep the Tags

Open test

$U\subseteq\coprod_i X_i$ open $\iff U\cap X_i$ open in $X_i$ for every $i$

Tags are data

Same-named points from different summands stay distinct until a quotient forgets them.

Each component is open

Its slice fills one summand and is empty in all the others.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Disjoint Unions: Keep the Tags
A disjoint union tags every point by its source component, so a set is open exactly when its slice in each summand is open there. The canonical injections are embeddings, and a map out is continuous iff every restriction to a summand is continuous. Each whole component is open, since its slice fills one summand and empties the rest.

Quotients: Only Saturated Sets Are Seen

Quotient topology

$q:X\to Y$ surjective; $\ V\subseteq Y$ open $\iff q^{-1}(V)$ open in $X$

Saturated subsets

Preimages of $Y$-sets: once they hold a point they hold its whole class.

What it builds

Circle from an interval, torus from a square, $\mathbb{RP}^n$ from $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}$, and $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Quotients: Only Saturated Sets
A quotient starts from a surjection: a set downstairs is open exactly when its preimage is open upstairs. The only upstairs sets that matter are saturated sets, which contain whole equivalence classes. This single rule builds circles from intervals, tori from squares, projective space from the sphere, and the circle as the real line modulo integer translates.

The Torus from the Square — and Its Cousins

Torus

Glue both edge pairs with matching arrows: $S^1\times S^1$.

Klein bottle

Reverse one pair of arrows — a non-orientable closed surface.

Mobius band

Identify just one pair with a flip — one edge, one side.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84The Torus from the Square
Gluing opposite edges of the unit square with matching arrow directions yields the torus, equally the product of two circles. Reversing one pair of arrows gives the Klein bottle, and identifying just one pair with a flip gives the Mobius band. The arrow pattern on the fundamental polygon encodes which quotient, and therefore which surface, results.

Adjunction Spaces and Quotient Pathologies

Adjunction $X\cup_f Y$

$A\subseteq X,\ f:A\to Y;\ $ take $X\sqcup Y$, identify $a\sim f(a)$

Not a new primitive

Disjoint union followed by a quotient: cones, doubles, attached cells.

Pathology warning

The line with two origins is locally Euclidean and second countable, yet not Hausdorff.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Adjunction Spaces and Pathologies
An adjunction space glues a subspace of one space onto another by first forming a disjoint union, then quotienting by the attaching map; it models cones, doubles, and attached cells. Quotients also breed pathologies: the line with two origins is locally Euclidean and second countable yet not Hausdorff, since saturated neighborhoods of the two origins always overlap.

Audit Workflow and Takeaways

1. Name the canonical map

Inclusion, projection, injection, or the surjective quotient map.

2. State the open-set test

Clip, coordinatewise, slicewise, or saturated-preimage.

3. Apply the characteristic property

Reduce the continuity question to maps you already control.

4. Record what survives

Inherited vs. needs proof — check saturation and separation before claiming a manifold.

Lee, Ch. 3 · printed pp. 49–84Audit Workflow and Takeaways
A topology on a constructed set is best understood through its canonical maps. The standing audit is short but strict: name the canonical map, state the open-set test, apply the characteristic property, then record which properties are inherited and which require proof. For quotients especially, verify saturation and separation before ever calling the result a manifold.
