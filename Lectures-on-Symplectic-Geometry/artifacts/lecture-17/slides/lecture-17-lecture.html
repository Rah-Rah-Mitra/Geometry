Hodge Theory: Harmonic Representatives

Codifferential

The star operator gives an adjoint $\delta$ to $d$.

Laplacian

Elliptic and self-adjoint on a compact manifold.

Hodge Laplacian

$\Delta = d\,\delta + \delta\, d, \qquad \delta = \pm * d *$

Hodge theorem

$H^k_{dR}(M) \;\cong\; \mathcal{H}^k = \ker \Delta|_{\Omega^k}$

Upshot

One harmonic form per cohomology class.

Printed pp. 96-102Hodge Theory Recap
On a compact oriented Riemannian manifold the star operator builds a codifferential adjoint to d, and the Laplacian is their anticommutator. Hodge proved every de Rham class has exactly one harmonic representative, so cohomology becomes a finite dimensional space of solutions of an elliptic equation. This is the engine everything else rides on.

The Kahler Refinement: Three Laplacians

Splitting $d$

On a complex manifold $d = \partial + \bar\partial$.

Kahler identities

Hold precisely when $g$ is Kahler.

Coincidence

$\Delta_d = 2\,\Delta_{\partial} = 2\,\Delta_{\bar\partial}$

Printed pp. 96-102The Kahler Refinement
Walk through the three Laplacians. On a complex manifold d splits into dolbeault operators, each with its own Laplacian. The Kahler identities, which hold exactly when the metric is Kahler, force these three Laplacians to coincide up to a factor of two. That single coincidence is what makes harmonic forms respect complex bidegree.

The Hodge Decomposition

Bidegree preserved

$\Delta$ keeps the $(p,q)$ type of harmonic forms.

Dolbeault

Each piece is a $\bar\partial$-cohomology group.

Decomposition

$$H^k(M;\mathbb{C}) \;=\; \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M)$$

Conjugation symmetry

$$H^{p,q} \;=\; \overline{H^{q,p}}$$

Caution

This split fails on a generic complex manifold.

Printed pp. 96-102Hodge Decomposition
State the theorem carefully. Because the Laplacian preserves bidegree, the harmonic k-forms split into pieces of pure type p plus q equals k. Translating back through Hodge and Dolbeault isomorphisms gives the decomposition of de Rham cohomology into Dolbeault groups. Emphasize that this fails on a general complex or symplectic manifold.

The Hodge Diamond

Bigraded array

Each node is $h^{p,q} = \dim H^{p,q}$.

Rows are Betti

$b_k = \sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}$.

Example $n=2$

A complex surface gives a five-row diamond.

Printed pp. 96-102The Hodge Diamond
Draw the diamond live. Each lattice point is a Hodge number, arranged by bidegree, with degree increasing downward. Conjugation reflects it left to right, and Serre duality through the Hodge star rotates it by a half turn. Point out that summing each horizontal row recovers the ordinary Betti number for that degree.

Symmetries of the Hodge Numbers

Conjugation

Real forms force $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$.

Serre / Hodge star

Gives $h^{p,q} = h^{n-p,\,n-q}$.

Two reflections

$$h^{p,q}=h^{q,p}=h^{n-p,n-q}=h^{n-q,n-p}$$

Consequence

The full diamond is determined by a small corner.

Numerical

All the topological tests flow from here.

Printed pp. 96-102Symmetries of Hodge Numbers
Make the two symmetries precise. Complex conjugation sends a harmonic form of type p,q to one of type q,p, giving equality of the two reflected Hodge numbers. The real Hodge star supplies the antipodal symmetry. Together they pin down the diamond from less than half its entries and drive every numerical consequence below.

The Hard Lefschetz Theorem

Lefschetz operator

$L = \omega \wedge (\cdot)$ raises degree by two.

Theorem

$L^k$ is an isomorphism across the middle.

Hard Lefschetz

$$L^k : H^{n-k}(M) \;\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}\; H^{n+k}(M)$$

Printed pp. 96-102Hard Lefschetz
Introduce the Lefschetz operator as wedging with the symplectic form. Its powers map cohomology up in degree, and the hard Lefschetz theorem says the k-fold power is an isomorphism from degree n minus k onto degree n plus k. This forces Betti numbers to increase up to the middle, the strongest constraint in the package.

The Kahler Package

$\partial\bar\partial$-lemma

$d$-exact and $d^c$-closed forms are $\partial\bar\partial$-exact.

Primitive decomposition

Every class splits into Lefschetz-primitive pieces.

$dd^c$ lemma

$$\alpha = d\beta = d^c\gamma \;\Longrightarrow\; \alpha = \partial\bar\partial\,\eta$$

Primitive form

$$H^k = \bigoplus_{j} L^j\, P^{k-2j}$$

Rigidity

No formal manifold can fake the whole package.

Printed pp. 96-102The Kahler Package
Collect the toolkit. The dd-complement lemma, often written with the conjugate differential, says forms that are closed and exact in the right senses differ by a doubly exact term. Together with hard Lefschetz and the primitive Lefschetz decomposition this is the package that no formal manifold can fake without genuine Kahler geometry.

Not Every Symplectic Manifold Is Kahler

Kodaira-Thurston

Compact, symplectic, and complex at once.

The failure

$b_1 = 3$ is odd, so it cannot be Kahler.

Conclusion

Symplectic strictly contains Kahler.

Printed pp. 96-102Not Every Symplectic Manifold
Deliver the punchline with the Kodaira-Thurston manifold. It is compact, symplectic, and complex, yet its first Betti number is three, an odd number. The Hodge parity test it fails proves it admits no Kahler metric. So symplectic strictly contains Kahler, and these tests are how we tell them apart.

Synthesis

Compactness

Harmonic representatives of de Rham classes.

Kahler

Splits them by $(p,q)$ type: the Hodge diamond.

Hard Lefschetz

Amplifies symmetry into Betti inequalities.

Necessary conditions

$$b_{\text{odd}}\in 2\mathbb{Z}, \quad b_2\ge 1, \quad b_{k-2}\le b_k \;(k\le n)$$

Separation

Kahler, projective, complex, symplectic all distinct.

Next

Use these tests to certify non-Kahler examples.

Printed pp. 96-102Synthesis
Tie the thread together. Compactness gives harmonic representatives, the Kahler condition splits them by type, the diamond encodes the symmetries, and hard Lefschetz amplifies them into Betti inequalities. The upshot is a battery of necessary conditions that separate Kahler, projective, complex, and symplectic geometry cleanly from one another.
