Appendix Atechnical tools for variation
Open the appendix as a toolkit rather than a detour. The chapter supplies the smoothness and support conventions that make the variational arguments legal on domains with edges, collars, and compactly supported perturbations. Tell learners that these tools are small, but they decide whether a formal variation is admissible.
Smoothness Convention

At a boundary point, smooth means extendable to a neighborhood

\(f: D \to \mathbb{R}^n\) smooth if \(f=\tilde f|_D\)

Why it matters

Differentiation near the boundary uses ordinary smooth functions nearby.

Lecture habit

Draw the closed domain and the invisible open collar where the extension lives.

Smoothnessrestriction of a nearby smooth map
Stress the local extension test. A map on a closed domain is treated as smooth when it is the restriction of a smooth map nearby. That convention lets calculus operate at boundary points without inventing one-sided differential rules.
Support

Support is the closure of the active region

Active set

Where the variation field is nonzero.

Support

The closure of that active set, including limiting edge points.

Compact support

The support fits inside the domain away from boundary trouble.

Supportwhere the variation is allowed to act
Use support as a bookkeeping device for variations. If the support stays away from the boundary, integration by parts loses its boundary term. That is exactly why compact support appears so often in variational derivations.
Smooth Switches

A bump function is a differentiable on-off switch

\(0 \le \rho \le 1\)
\(\rho=1\) on the test region
\(\rho=0\) outside a larger region
Bump functionlocal experiment, smooth transition
A bump function is a smooth switch. It is zero outside a chosen region, one where the experiment should happen, and smooth through the transition. The point is not the formula, but the ability to localize a variation without tearing differentiability.
Admissible Variations

Multiply by a cutoff to keep the field legal

\(V_{\mathrm{legal}}=\rho V\)

Inside

\(\rho=1\), so the intended variation is unchanged.

Outside

\(\rho=0\), so the field cannot disturb the boundary condition.

Cutofflocal equation from admissible tests
Explain the workflow: start with the field one wants, multiply by a cutoff, and obtain an admissible compactly supported field. Then shrink the cutoff region to test the local equation. This is how a global variation argument turns into a pointwise Euler-Lagrange condition without abusing the boundary.
Boundary Coordinates

A collar turns an edge into tangential and normal directions

Tangential variable

Moves along the boundary curve or face.

Normal variable

Measures distance into the domain.

Use

Separate boundary restrictions from interior variation arguments.

Collardraw the boundary as a product strip
Collar coordinates turn a boundary into a product direction. This makes it clear which variable is tangential and which is normal to the edge, so support and extension statements become drawable rather than abstract. Use this as the visual bridge between domain language and the boundary terms seen in integration by parts.
Common Misreads

Three technical words that should not be blurred

Smooth versus continuous

A corner can be continuous and still fail the differentiability needed for variation.

Support versus size

Support tracks nonzero location, not how large the field appears in a plot.

Cutoff versus hard mask

A hard mask may be easy to draw, but it inserts nonsmooth edges.

Legal local argument = smooth extension + controlled support + smooth cutoff
Pitfallstechnical words carry proof obligations
Name the common mistakes explicitly. Continuity is not smoothness, support is not the same as the region where a formula is visually large, and a cutoff that is sharp in a plot may fail to be smooth.
