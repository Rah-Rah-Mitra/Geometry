Open by moving the class from a circular sample space to the unit sphere. The essential change is not merely one more coordinate; it is the loss of a preferred chart and the arrival of axial observations, where a measured line identifies opposite unit vectors.
Spherical model building

Four Decisions Carry The Chapter

Working question

Which summaries and model families still make geometric sense when observations live on \(S^{p-1}\), or when \(x\) and \(-x\) represent the same measurement?

\(S^{p-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:x^{T}x=1\}\)

The sample space is constrained before any probability model is chosen.

1. Constraint

Every observation is normalized onto the sphere.

2. Symmetry

Directions preserve sign; axes do not.

3. Statistic

Use \(\bar x\) or \(T\) according to the object.

4. Approximation

Read Gaussian limits in the right coordinates.

Use the route as the contract for the lecture. We first decide what the observation is, then choose summaries that survive rotation, then place model families on top of those summaries, and finally read the two asymptotic stories without mixing them.
Data object

A Direction Is Not The Same As An Axis

Translation guide

  • Point on \(S^{p-1}\): a unit row vector with norm one.
  • Directional sample: signs carry information, so \(\bar x\) can identify a mean pole.
  • Axial sample: signs are irrelevant, so summaries must be invariant under \(x\mapsto -x\).
  • First check: any statistic should behave coherently under rotations of the entire data set.
direction: \(x\neq -x\)    axis: \(x\sim -x\)
Emphasize that a point on the sphere and an unoriented axis live in different statistical spaces. The same recorded vector can be meaningful as a signed direction or only as a line through the origin, and the summary must respect that scientific choice.
Descriptive measures

The Mean Resultant Detects Poles; Inertia Detects Axes

Two summaries

$$\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i,\qquad R=\|\bar x\|$$
$$T=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_ix_i^{T}=\frac{X^{T}X}{n}$$

For axes, \(x_ix_i^T=(-x_i)(-x_i)^T\), so the quadratic summary survives antipodal sign changes.

The mean resultant is excellent when the cloud has one oriented pole, but it deliberately cancels an antipodal cloud. The inertia matrix keeps quadratic information, so the equality between x x transpose and minus x minus x transpose becomes the bridge to axial statistics.
Geometry checks

Summaries Must Survive Rotation And Unit-Norm Constraints

Invariant identities

$$\operatorname{tr}(T)=1,\qquad T\succeq 0$$
$$T=\frac{n-1}{n}S+\bar x\bar x^T$$
$$\frac{n-1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(S)+R^2=1$$

These fail quickly if normalization or coordinate handling is wrong.

A rotation should rotate vectors and eigenvectors, not alter \(R\), trace, or inertia eigenvalues.

This is the algebraic safety rail for the chapter. Trace one, positive semidefiniteness, the variance decomposition, and rotation-equivariance are not optional decorations; they are quick tests that the computation still belongs to a spherical sample space.

One-pole models

Fisher/vMF Tilts The Sphere Toward A Mean Direction

Density on \(S^2\)

$$f(x;\mu,\kappa)=\frac{\kappa}{\sinh \kappa}\exp(\kappa \mu^T x)$$
  • \(\mu\) is a unit mean direction.
  • \(\kappa=0\) gives the uniform sphere.
  • Larger \(\kappa\) concentrates mass near one pole.
  • The sufficient statistic is the resultant direction.
Present the Fisher model as the spherical analogue of a one-pole concentration model. The sufficient statistic is the projection onto the mean direction, so increasing kappa steepens density along one axis without making the antipodal point equally likely.
Distribution theory

On \(S^2\), Normalization Is A One-Dimensional Check

Why the scalar \(t\) matters

Under the uniform distribution on \(S^2\), \(t=\mu^T X\) is uniform on \([-1,1]\). A Fisher density with respect to uniform sphere measure therefore checks through

$$\int_{-1}^{1}\frac12\frac{\kappa}{\sinh\kappa}e^{\kappa t}\,dt=1.$$

The notebook records this integral as \(1.0\) to numerical precision and checks the corresponding mean resultant function.

For S two, reduce the normalization check to the scalar t equal mu dot x. The uniform sphere contributes a flat density in t on minus one to one, so the exponential tilt can be integrated directly and checked numerically.
Sign-invariant models

Axial Families Put Probability On Lines, Girdles, And Quadratic Shapes

Watson, pole pair

\(f(x)\propto e^{\kappa(\mu^Tx)^2}\)

Watson, girdle

axis known, spread is circular

Bingham

\(f(x)\propto e^{x^TAx}\)

Axial modeling begins by honoring the symmetry \(f(x)=f(-x)\). The model should not reintroduce an orientation that the measurement process never observed.

Watson and Bingham models answer the question that Fisher does not: what if signs are irrelevant or the spread is better described by a quadratic form? Ask students to inspect whether mass lives at two poles, around an equator, or in an oval axial lobe.

Resultant length

\(R=\|\bar X\|\)

Useful for departures from uniformity and one-pole alternatives.

Projected coordinate

\(T_{\mu}=\mu^TX\)

Turns rotationally symmetric densities into scalar distributions.

Inertia spectrum

\(\lambda(T)\)

Separates axial, girdle, and diffuse alternatives.

The later tests in spherical statistics ask how these objects behave under uniformity, under concentration, or under axial symmetry.

Before tests and confidence cones, the class needs the random objects whose distributions will be approximated. Resultant length, projected coordinates, and inertia eigenstructure each answer a different geometric question, and each has a different null behavior.
Local asymptotics

Large \(\kappa\) Turns The Sphere Into A Tangent Gaussian Problem

Local coordinates near \(\mu\)

$$2\kappa(1-\mu^TX)\;\approx\;\chi^2_{p-1}$$
$$\sqrt{\kappa}\,u(X)\;\approx\;N_{p-1}(0,I)$$

For the notebook's \(S^2\) simulation, the scaled tangent covariance is close to the \(2\times2\) identity and the radial statistic has the two-degree chi-square shape.

Keep the limit local. When kappa is large, observations near the pole can be represented in a tangent plane, and the scaled tangent coordinates look Gaussian. The radial statistic two kappa times one minus the projection behaves like a chi-square variable.
Dimension asymptotics

Uniform High-Dimensional Spheres Have Gaussian Coordinates

Different from high concentration

$$\sqrt{p}\,X_1\Rightarrow N(0,1)$$

A uniform point on \(S^{p-1}\) is not near a fixed pole. Instead, each fixed coordinate is usually small, and the \(\sqrt p\) scaling reveals a normal limit.

The notebook checks this by simulating dimensions \(p=6,50,200\) and recording scaled-coordinate variances close to one.

Separate high dimension from high concentration. A uniform point on a large sphere is not near a chosen pole, but any fixed coordinate is small with Gaussian scaling. The square root p factor is the natural magnifying glass for that coordinate.
Lab workflow

Choose The Statistic That Matches The Scientific Object

Workflow

  1. Normalize observations and audit unit norms.
  2. Decide whether a sign flip changes the phenomenon.
  3. Inspect \(R\), mean direction, and inertia spectrum together.
  4. Use Fisher/vMF for oriented poles; use Watson or Bingham for axes and girdles.
  5. Use tangent or high-dimensional limits only after checking the regime.
Turn the theory into a workflow. First decide whether signs matter, then verify unit norms, then inspect resultant and inertia evidence, then choose a one-pole, axial, girdle, or quadratic model family before moving to inference.
Recap

What Carries Forward To Inference On Spheres

Takeaways

  • Unit-norm constraints define the sample space, not a preprocessing preference.
  • The resultant vector is a directional statistic; inertia is the axial bridge.
  • Fisher/vMF models one pole; Watson and Bingham handle sign-invariant structure.
  • High concentration and high dimension are different Gaussian stories.

A model is successful only when its symmetry, statistic, and approximation regime all match the geometry of the measurement.

Close by tying the chapter to later inference on spheres. A reliable spherical analysis starts with the sample space, uses invariant summaries, chooses a model whose symmetry matches the data, and treats tangent or high-dimensional approximations as checks rather than slogans.
