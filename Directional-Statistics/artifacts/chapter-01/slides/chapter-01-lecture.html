Circular Data First Decisions

A circular observation is not just a number in degrees. It is a point, direction, wrapped time, or unoriented axis on a closed sample space.

Coordinate Labels
Angles are measurements of a chosen circular coordinate system

The Observation Is Not The Label

The geometric object is a point on \(S^1\), an axis in \(S^1/\{\theta \sim \theta+\pi\}\), or a residue class of a line modulo a period.

Coordinate Map

angle label \(\theta\) depends on zero, orientation, and units

Misread

A number such as 10 degrees is incomplete until the measurement story is known.

Emphasize that the point on the circle is the observation and the numerical angle is a coordinate label. Moving the zero direction or changing orientation relabels the same geometry, while confusing the observation type changes the problem itself.
Conventions
Two angle systems that often get mixed

Clock And Compass Conventions

Compare the two convention panels carefully. Clock data usually wrap a line with a chosen period, while compass bearings often use north as zero and measure clockwise. Both can be stored as angles, but their signs, cut points, and scientific questions differ.
Displays
The same circular sample can support several honest summaries

Four Honest Displays

Raw Circular PlotShows observations as points without binning or a linear seam.
Circular HistogramGroups adjacent arcs while keeping zero and a full turn adjacent.
Rose DiagramUses sectors whose areas should be proportional to frequencies.
Unrolled HistogramUseful after declaring a cut point and checking sensitivity.
Frame the display slide as a design problem. A raw circular plot avoids bins, a circular histogram groups counts around the circle, a rose diagram must encode counts by area, and an unrolled histogram is only honest when the cut point is declared.
Binning
Boundary bins are neighbors, not endpoints

Circular Bins Preserve The Sample

Count Preservation

\(\sum_{j=1}^{m} n_j = n\)

A circular histogram groups arcs around \(S^1\). The first and last bins touch each other because \(0\) and \(2\pi\) are the same boundary point.

Inspection Target

Check whether a cluster near the cut remains visually adjacent to itself.

The key invariant for a binned circular display is count preservation. Bins are arranged around the boundary so that a cluster crossing zero degrees is still adjacent to itself. Ask students to inspect whether the boundary is treated as a seam or as a neighbor relation.
Rose Diagrams
Frequency should be represented by area

Rose Area Carries Frequency

Area Rule

sector area \(\propto r_j^2 \Delta\theta_j\)

For equal angular widths, scaling radius by \(\sqrt{n_j}\) makes visible area proportional to the bin count.

Common Error

Scaling radius directly by count can make high-frequency sectors dominate more than the data justify.

Use this slide to prevent the most common rose diagram mistake. If the radius is proportional to frequency, area grows quadratically and large bins are exaggerated. To make sector area proportional to count, the radius should scale like the square root of the count.
Unrolling
Linear views require a declared seam

The Cut Point Can Invent A Story

The same circular cluster can look bimodal or unimodal after it is flattened to a line.

Cut-Sensitivity Test

Move the seam. If the visual conclusion changes, the conclusion belongs to the display choice.

The unrolled histogram is useful precisely because it reveals how much the conclusion depends on the chosen cut. A cluster near zero degrees becomes split when the cut passes through it, yet the same observations look unified when the cut is moved opposite the cluster.
Axial Data
Unoriented lines are not arrows

Axial Observations Identify Opposites

Equivalence

\(\theta \sim \theta+\pi\)

Crack directions, optical axes, and elongation directions may have no arrowhead. Reversing the line should not create a new observation.

Mistake

Treating an axis as an oriented direction can split one pattern into two artificial modes.

Make the distinction between directions and axes explicit. Directional data distinguish an arrow from its reverse, but axial data record an unoriented line. Treating an axis as a direction doubles the apparent categories and can make summaries point to a false preferred orientation.
Axial Conversion
A modeling map from axes to ordinary directions

Doubling Turns Axes Into Directions

Doubling Invariant

2(\(\theta+\pi\)) \(\equiv\) 2\(\theta\) mod \(2\pi\)

After analyzing the doubled directions, report the result back on the original axial scale.

Explain doubling as a modeling map, not a cosmetic plotting trick. Because theta and theta plus pi represent the same axis, multiplying by two sends both representatives to the same circular direction modulo two pi. Interpretation then has to be halved back to axes.
Generative Story
Clock data are residues of a linear measurement

Wrapping Starts From A Line

Clock Example

23 h and 1 h are 2 h apart periodically

The seam is a coordinate feature. Distances and clusters should be evaluated on the circle, not on the unwrapped line alone.

For clock-like observations, begin with a linear variable and reduce it modulo a period. The cut point is arbitrary but visible, and periodic distance must cross the boundary correctly. The example distance from 23 to 1 hours is two hours, not twenty-two.
Generative Story
Compass data are directions of nonzero planar vectors

Projection Starts From A Vector

Radial Projection

v \in \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}, \quad u = v / \lVert v \rVert \in S^1

A projected direction is unstable when the vector length is close to zero. That is a different failure mode from choosing a bad cut point.

For compass-like observations, the angle comes from projecting a nonzero planar vector to the unit circle. The unstable case is not a bad cut point but a vector near the origin, where the direction is poorly determined even before an angle is assigned.
Before Computing
A small routing chart prevents large interpretive errors

A Pre-Statistics Decision Tree

Present this as the checklist before computing any circular statistic. Decide whether the data wrap a period, project a direction, or identify antipodal axes. Then choose a display whose invariants match that observation type rather than retrofitting a linear plot afterward.
Checks
Small invariants make displays auditable

Invariant Checks

Wrapping Domain

x \mapsto x \bmod T \in [0,T)

Boundary values return to the period, not to a new endpoint.

Projection Norm

u = v / \lVert v \rVert, \quad \lVert u \rVert = 1

The origin is excluded because direction is undefined there.

Axial Doubling

2(\(\theta+\pi\)) \equiv 2\(\theta\) \pmod{2\pi}

Opposite representatives become the same circular direction.

Display Counts

\(\sum_j n_j = n,\quad r_j \propto \sqrt{n_j}\)

The display preserves observations and encodes frequency honestly.

The formulas are small because they are meant to be reused. Wrapping keeps values inside the period, projection returns unit vectors away from the origin, axial doubling identifies antipodes, and binned displays preserve the sample count. These checks make the visual argument auditable.
Recap
The geometry is the first statistical assumption

Carry Forward

Portable Principle

Choose the sample space, then choose the display, then compute the statistic.

  • Clock data wrap a line modulo a period.
  • Compass data project nonzero planar vectors.
  • Axial data identify opposite directions.
  • Rose diagrams and unrolled histograms need explicit display choices.
