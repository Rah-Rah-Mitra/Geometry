Bessel ratios, Kummer derivatives, and normalizing constants are the numerical bridge between curved sample spaces and estimable model parameters.

Draw attention to the map rather than the names. The circular case begins with modified Bessel functions of low order, spherical models shift the order with dimension, axial models call on Kummer functions, and frame models generalize the same averaging idea over a larger curved space.

Modified Bessel Functions Normalize Directional Exponentials

$$c_p(\kappa)=\frac{\kappa^{p/2-1}}{(2\pi)^{p/2} I_{p/2-1}(\kappa)}$$
Reading The Formula

The modified Bessel term is the spherical average hidden inside the exponential tilt \(e^{\kappa \mu^\top x}\).

Numerical Caution

Large \(\kappa\) makes the exponential cap narrow, so log normalizers or scaled Bessel functions are safer than direct exponentials.

Connect the formula to mass on the sphere. The Bessel function is not an arbitrary constant; it is what remains after the exponential tilt has been integrated around every direction. The visual cap also previews why large concentration creates numerical overflow and quadrature sensitivity.

The Bessel Ratio Predicts Mean Resultant Length

$$A_p(\kappa)=\frac{I_{p/2}(\kappa)}{I_{p/2-1}(\kappa)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad E(\bar R)\approx A_p(\kappa)$$
Geometry In The Curves

Higher dimension spreads mass into more side directions, so the same concentration produces a smaller expected resultant.

Use this slide as the main mental model for concentration estimation. The ratio is monotone, so inversion is well posed, but the same kappa does not mean the same visible clustering across dimensions. Students should inspect the ordering of the curves before touching a root finder.

Estimating \(\kappa\) Is A One-Dimensional Inverse Problem

Estimator Shape

Given \(0<\bar R<1\), solve \(A_p(\hat\kappa)=\bar R\). The residual is the audit trail.

Round Trip Check

Compute \(A_p(\hat\kappa)-\bar R\). A successful table replacement reports this number, not only the estimate.

Boundary Behavior

Near \(\bar R=1\), the curve flattens and tiny changes in \(\bar R\) can imply very large concentration.

Frame concentration estimation as a numerical inverse problem with a visible domain. The observed resultant length must lie between zero and one, the monotonic curve gives a unique solution, and the residual after substituting the estimate back into A_p is the simplest reliability certificate.

Approximation Formulas Are Regime Statements

Small Concentration

\(A_p(\kappa)\) grows almost linearly; use this only while the relative error is visibly small.

Middle Concentration

Interpolation and root solving are usually safer than pretending either asymptotic formula dominates.

Large Concentration

The ratio approaches one, but direct evaluation can overflow before the statistical approximation becomes clean.

Make the approximation message practical rather than formal. The appendix gives local formulas, but the notebook turns them into error plots. In the middle regime students should prefer exact evaluation or numerical inversion, because neither the low concentration nor the high concentration story is yet trustworthy.

Stable Arithmetic Keeps The Model From Lying

$$\log c_p(\kappa)=\left(\frac{p}{2}-1\right)\log \kappa-\frac{p}{2}\log(2\pi)-\log I_{p/2-1}(\kappa)$$
Failure Mode

Direct \(I_\nu(\kappa)\) and \(e^\kappa\) may overflow while ratios and log constants are still statistically meaningful.

Spend a minute on computation because this appendix is used by later fitting routines. The safe implementation often works with logarithms, scaled special functions, or direct ratios. Otherwise the code can fail in high concentration exactly where the statistical signal is strongest.

Kummer Functions Compute Axial Moment Ratios

$$D_p(\kappa)=\frac{d}{d\kappa}\log M\!\left(\frac12,\frac p2,\kappa\right) =\frac{1}{p}\frac{M(3/2,p/2+1,\kappa)}{M(1/2,p/2,\kappa)}$$
Interpretation

\(D_p(\kappa)\) predicts a squared projection onto a favored axis, so it starts at \(1/p\), not zero.

Contrast the Kummer derivative ratio with A_p. A directional first moment vanishes under uniformity, but an axial squared projection has baseline one over p. That single distinction explains why the Kummer curve begins above zero and why axial models use second moment language.

The Derivative Identity Is The Audit Hook

$$\frac{d}{d\kappa}M(a,b,\kappa)=\frac{a}{b}M(a+1,b+1,\kappa)$$
Check

Compare the analytic ratio with a finite-difference derivative of \(\log M\). Agreement validates both the formula and implementation.

Teaching Point

The derivative of a log normalizer is a moment. This pattern recurs across exponential families on curved spaces.

Use this slide to connect symbolic identities to numerical trust. The derivative formula supplies an analytic ratio, while a finite difference of log M gives an independent check. When those curves agree, the learner sees the moment calculation and the implementation test at the same time.

A One-Frame Stiefel Integral Is Just A Sphere Average

$$E_{X\sim \mathrm{Unif}(S^2)} e^{\kappa X_1}=\frac{\sinh \kappa}{\kappa}$$
Smallest Case

A one-frame in three dimensions is a point on the sphere, so the matrix-argument story reduces to a visible average.

Inspection Target

As \(\kappa\) grows, most contribution comes from a small cap near the preferred coordinate direction.

Make the Stiefel language less intimidating by starting with one frame. In three dimensions that object is just a unit vector on the sphere. Averaging exp(kappa X_1) over a deterministic sphere grid gives a direct check against sinh kappa over kappa.

Normalizing Constants Are Averages, Not Lookup Magic

Two Equivalent Views

The special-function expression and a direct average over the sample space are two descriptions of the same normalizer.

Executable Check

The notebook records the relative error between quadrature and closed form for a grid of concentration values.

This slide turns normalizing constants into measurable objects. Learners should see two routes to the same number: a special-function formula and a deterministic average over the relevant curved space. Agreement between them is evidence that the implementation respects the geometry.

Executable Table Replacement: Inputs, Solves, Residuals

Input

Choose dimension \(p\) and a feasible resultant length \(\bar R\).

Solve

Find \(\hat\kappa\) from \(A_p(\hat\kappa)=\bar R\) using a bracketed root solve.

Certify

Report \(|A_p(\hat\kappa)-\bar R|\) beside the estimate, so the lookup has an audit trail.

Recap: Which Calculator Does The Model Need?

If The Data Are Directional

Look for a resultant equation and a Bessel ratio \(A_p(\kappa)\).

If The Constant Is Mysterious

Ask what curved space is being averaged and what numerical check would prove the average.
