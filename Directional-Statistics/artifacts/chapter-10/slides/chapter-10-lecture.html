The Basic Data Object

Definition-level summary
$$R=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\right\|,\qquad \bar R=R/n,\qquad \hat\mu=\frac{\sum_i x_i}{R}.$$
Geometric reading
  • \(\hat\mu\) is a pole estimate.
  • \(\bar R\) is concentration evidence.
  • The scatter matrix remembers axial patterns.
Establish notation before discussing tests. The resultant direction is undefined if the resultant length is essentially zero, and that failure is not just numerical noise; it is evidence that the data may be uniform, girdle-like, or antipodal.

Fisher Model: Location And Concentration

Model on \(S^2\)
$$f(x;\mu,\kappa)=c_3(\kappa)\exp\{\kappa\,\mu^T x\},\qquad \|\mu\|=1,\quad \kappa\ge 0.$$
Interpretation

The pole \(\mu\) sets the center; the concentration \(\kappa\) controls how quickly mass decays away from that pole. Uniformity is the boundary case \(\kappa=0\).

Introduce the Fisher density as the spherical analogue of a centered normal family, while avoiding the false impression that the sphere has Euclidean tails. The sufficient summaries are the resultant direction and length because the log likelihood depends on the sample through their dot product with mu.

Estimating Concentration

Likelihood equation
$$A_3(\hat\kappa)=\bar R,\qquad A_3(\kappa)=\coth\kappa-\kappa^{-1}.$$
Reference notebook fit

For the chapter sample, \(\bar R=0.9054\) gives \(\hat\kappa=10.5735\), with a numerical residual near \(10^{-16}\).

Practical warning

When \(\bar R\) is very small, a Fisher concentration estimate is not a meaningful summary of axial or multimodal structure.

Walk through kappa estimation as a one-dimensional inverse problem. The graph helps learners see why larger mean resultant length forces larger concentration, while also showing why the estimate should be interpreted only after the data actually look Fisher-like.

Mean-Direction Confidence Cone

Reported object
$$C_\alpha=\{\mu\in S^2:\ \mu^T\hat\mu \ge \cos \alpha\}.$$
Interpretation

The cone is an angular uncertainty statement. It is not a Euclidean ellipse drawn in a tangent plane, although tangent-plane approximations can motivate its radius for concentrated samples.

Cap Probability: Uniform Baseline Versus Fitted Fisher

Why compute cap mass?

It calibrates how surprising a local cluster is under uniformity and how much the fitted model concentrates near the estimated pole.

Reference comparison

The notebook reports uniform mass \(0.0022\) and fitted Fisher mass \(0.0457\) for the same confidence-cone angle, a roughly twentyfold contrast.

Reading the contrast

A small uniform cap mass supports non-uniformity, but it does not by itself distinguish a Fisher cluster from a mixture or axial distribution.

Use the cap comparison to connect estimation with testing. The same angular region can be evaluated under two models, and the difference in mass explains why concentrated spherical samples quickly become incompatible with a uniform null.

Uniformity Tests Have Different Eyes

Rayleigh direction test
$$W_R=3n\bar R^2 \quad\leadsto\quad \chi^2_3\ \text{under uniformity}.$$
Second-moment tests

Bingham-type statistics read the eigenvalues of \(T=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i x_i^T\). They can detect girdles and bipolar clouds even when the vector resultant is small.

Contrast first-moment and second-moment evidence. Rayleigh is powerful against a single mean shift, while Bingham-style tests are designed for departures that alter the orientation tensor without creating a large resultant vector. Ask students which point in the diagram each alternative would occupy before naming the test.

Three Non-Uniform Patterns

Fisher Cluster

Large resultant; Rayleigh should respond strongly.

Bipolar Axial

Signs cancel in the mean, but the principal axis is stable.

Girdle

Mass concentrates near a great circle; the smallest eigenvalue is diagnostic.

Uniform

Resultant and orientation tensor fluctuate at their null scales.

This taxonomy prevents a common mistake: treating any rejection of uniformity as evidence for a Fisher cluster. Axial and girdle alternatives can be very non-uniform while having nearly zero resultant, so the visualization determines the testing strategy.

Axial Diagnostics: Scatter Matrix And Eigenvalues

Orientation tensor
$$T=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i x_i^T,\qquad \lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=1.$$
Sign invariance

Replacing any observation by its antipode changes \(x_i\) but leaves \(x_i x_i^T\) unchanged. That is why axial tests can ignore arrow orientation.

Explain that the scatter matrix is the right object when the observation is an axis rather than an arrow. Its eigenvalues reveal whether mass piles up near one axis, spreads around a great circle, or resembles uniform spherical noise.

Common Pitfalls In Spherical Inference

Flattening too early. A tangent-plane plot can help, but final direction statements live on \(S^2\).
Confusing vectors and axes. Antipodal data require sign-invariant summaries such as \(x x^T\).
Using Rayleigh for every alternative. A small resultant can hide a strong girdle or bipolar signal.
Reporting p-values without geometry. The alternative pattern must be visible in the explanation.
Use the pitfalls slide as a quality-control checklist for student analyses. The main message is that spherical inference is not ordinary multivariate inference with spherical pictures added; the sample space changes the summaries, uncertainty sets, and diagnostics.

Recap And Instructor Prompt

Takeaways
  • Inference starts with unit-vector geometry and the resultant vector.
  • Fisher estimation turns \(\bar R\) into \(\hat\kappa\) and \(\hat\mu\) into a confidence cone.
  • Uniformity tests are targeted: Rayleigh sees mean shift, while Bingham sees orientation imbalance.
  • Axial data require sign-invariant summaries and tests.
Short exercise

Close by asking learners to identify which geometric feature a statistic is measuring before they compute it. The exercise is intentionally about a girdle-like eigenvalue pattern, where the mean resultant gives little evidence but the orientation tensor gives a clear clue.
