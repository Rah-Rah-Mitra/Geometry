The Von Mises Working Model

A circular analogue of a normal distribution: one center direction, one concentration parameter.

Density
$$f(\theta\mid \mu,\kappa)=\frac{\exp\{\kappa\cos(\theta-\mu)\}}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)}$$

\(\mu\) rotates the center of mass on the circle.

\(\kappa\) controls how sharply observations gather around that center.

\(I_0\) normalizes the wrapped exponential tilt.

Explain the von Mises model as the circular analogue of a normal model with mean direction and concentration. The important point for inference is that all likelihood information flows through C, S, and R, so geometry and statistics are the same object here.
Sufficient Statistics
sample vector sum

The Resultant Is The Sufficient Dashboard

Statistics To Carry Forward
$$C=\sum_i \cos\theta_i,\quad S=\sum_i \sin\theta_i,\quad R=\sqrt{C^2+S^2}$$

The maximum-likelihood mean direction is \(\hat\mu=\operatorname{atan2}(S,C)\).

\(\bar R\)resultant length per observation
\(\hat\mu\)estimated center direction
\(\hat\kappa\)fitted concentration from \(\bar R\)

No later test should be read without asking whether \(R\) indicates coherent direction or only weak circular signal.

Spend time on the resultant diagram. Each observation contributes a unit vector, the vector sum points to the estimated mean direction, and its length reports concentration. Short resultants mean either diffuse data or competing modes, so no mean-direction test should be interpreted without this diagnostic.

One-Sample Null: Rotate To The Proposed Mean

Testing \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) becomes a balance check after subtracting \(\mu_0\) from every angle.

Score Intuition
$$U(\mu_0)=\sum_i \sin(\theta_i-\mu_0)$$

Under the null, perpendicular components should cancel around zero.

Large positive \(U\): observations lean counterclockwise from \(\mu_0\).

Large negative \(U\): observations lean clockwise from \(\mu_0\).

Present the one-sample test by rotating the hypothesized mean direction to zero. Under the null, sine components should balance above and below the axis. A persistent perpendicular imbalance is the visual signal that the proposed mean direction is misaligned.

Confidence Arcs Invert The Test

A direction belongs to the arc when its rotated sine imbalance is statistically ordinary.

High-Concentration Approximation
$$\operatorname{se}(\hat\mu)\approx \{n\hat\kappa A_1(\hat\kappa)\}^{-1/2}$$

The arc widens when the sample is small or the fitted concentration is low.

Likelihood Geometry

Profile Likelihood Couples Mean And Concentration

When \(\kappa\) is unknown, each proposed mean direction gets its own best concentration fit.

Profile View
$$\ell_p(\mu)=\max_{\kappa\ge 0}\left\{\kappa\sum_i\cos(\theta_i-\mu)-n\log I_0(\kappa)\right\}+c$$

This is why the notebook keeps estimated-kappa and known-kappa confidence arcs side by side.

Use the profile likelihood sketch to show why kappa cannot be ignored. For each candidate mean direction, kappa is fitted from the resultant length after rotation. The mean estimate and concentration estimate are coupled, and small samples make that coupling visible.

Two Samples: How Much Length Does Pooling Lose?

Separate mean directions recover two group resultants; a common mean direction must live with one pooled resultant.

Between-Group Signal
$$B=(R_1+R_2)-R_{\mathrm{pooled}}$$

Small \(B\) means pooling preserves length. Large \(B\) means the two group directions pull apart.

Move to two samples by drawing separate resultants and the pooled resultant. If the two group means agree, pooling loses little length. If directions separate, the pooled vector is shorter than the sum of separate lengths; that loss is the between-group signal.

Watson-Williams As A Resultant Budget

Statistic Shape
$$F_{\mathrm{WW}}\propto \frac{(N-q)(\sum_j R_j-R)}{(q-1)(N-\sum_j R_j)}\times C(\hat\kappa)$$

The correction \(C(\hat\kappa)\) keeps the circular finite-concentration approximation from becoming a straight-line ANOVA by accident.

10.65notebook multi-sample \(F\)
4.48e-6decision p-value
1.14correction factor
Explain Watson-Williams as circular ANOVA built from resultant budgets. The numerator measures how much resultant length is recovered by allowing group-specific means. The denominator measures within-group circular scatter, and the correction acknowledges finite concentration rather than pretending the circle is a line.

Multi-Sample Inference Shares One Concentration

The null is focused: several von Mises groups have the same mean direction and a common concentration.

Interpretation

Rejecting the null is evidence that at least one group pulls the shared mean direction away from the others, after accounting for within-group circular scatter.

Check Concentration Homogeneity Before Circular ANOVA

Unequal \(\kappa\) changes the weight each group has in the resultant budget.

Why It Matters

A very concentrated group can dominate the pooled direction even when sample sizes are similar; a diffuse group can hide a real mean shift.

Use the second-harmonic diagnostic to catch two-lobed or axial alternatives. A sample can have a stable first resultant and still violate a von Mises shape. Large second-harmonic structure is a warning that the fitted single mode is summarizing the wrong object.
Calibration

P-Values Are Calibrated Geometry

The statistic is geometric; the p-value asks whether that geometry is surprising under the fitted null.

Two Layers

Observed layer: sine imbalance, confidence arc width, pooling loss, or concentration contrast.

Calibration layer: asymptotic formula, finite-sample correction, or simulation under \(H_0\).

The notebook keeps both layers visible so that students do not mistake a formula for a diagnostic.

Interpret p-values as calibrated geometry. The observed statistic is a distance or budget loss, and the null distribution tells us how surprising that geometry is after accounting for n and fitted kappa. This is why simulation checks sit beside formulas in the notebook.

Instructor Cue

Every assumption should have a visible artifact: a circle plot, a fitted-kappa contrast, a harmonic diagnostic, or a simulation envelope.

Make the failure modes explicit. Wrapped normal-looking data, axial mixtures, unequal concentrations, and sparse samples can all produce confident-looking output. The instructor should connect each failure to a diagnostic rather than treating assumptions as boilerplate.

Operational Checklist

Compute \(C,S,R,\hat\mu,\hat\kappa\) before any test.

Choose the comparison: one mean, two means, or \(q\) group means.

Use the statistic that matches the geometry: sine imbalance, arc width, or resultant budget.

Check shared concentration and von Mises shape before interpreting the decision.
