Definition Layer

Topological Manifold: Three Global Safeguards

Topological Manifold

An \(n\)-dimensional topological manifold is a topological space \(M\) that is Hausdorff + second countable + locally Euclidean.

For each \(p\in M\), some open \(U\ni p\) is homeomorphic to an open set in \(\mathbb{R}^n\).
  • Hausdorff separates distinct points by disjoint neighborhoods.
  • Second countable keeps the topology controlled by countably many basic opens.
  • Locally Euclidean supplies coordinates near each point.
Pause on the order of the definition. Local Euclidean behavior gives charts, but by itself it does not rule out spaces that cannot separate points or have unmanageable topology. The chapter insists that topology be disciplined before smooth coordinate changes are even discussed.

Coordinate Neighborhoods

What a Chart Actually Does

A chart is a local measuring device, not a global flattening of the space.

Chart

A chart on \(M\) is a pair \((U,\phi)\) where \(U\subset M\) is open and \(\phi:U\to \phi(U)\subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is a homeomorphism onto an open set.

What The Chart Lets Us Do

  • Name a point by coordinates \(x^1,\ldots,x^n\).
  • Translate local topology into Euclidean topology.
  • Perform calculus only after passing through coordinates.
A useful metaphor is that a chart is a local instrument panel. It reports coordinates for points in one neighborhood, and the report is trustworthy because phi is a homeomorphism onto an open Euclidean set. It does not claim the entire manifold can be flattened at once.

Smooth Structure

Compatible Charts Compare Coordinates on Overlaps

Compatibility Test

Charts \((U,\phi)\) and \((V,\psi)\) are smoothly compatible when both transition maps are smooth on overlap images:

\(\phi\circ\psi^{-1}\) and \(\psi\circ\phi^{-1}\)

Circle Atlas Check

For the stereographic circle model, the transition is \(u\mapsto 1/u\) on each overlap component, with derivative \(-1/u^2\).

Local check: circle-atlas-transition-check.json

Stress that compatibility is a statement about overlap images in Euclidean space. Students should track the domain of each transition map, since smoothness is only asked where both charts are valid. The circle example is memorable because reciprocal transition maps are smooth away from zero.

From Charts to Smooth Structure

Atlases Cover the Space and Maximal Atlases Close the System

Smooth Atlas

A smooth atlas is a collection of mutually compatible charts whose domains cover \(M\).

Maximal Atlas

The maximal atlas contains every chart compatible with the chosen atlas. It records the full smooth structure rather than one convenient chart list.

Why Maximality Matters

Different starting atlases can determine the same smooth structure when their union is compatible.

Treat an atlas as a contract among charts, not simply a pile of coordinate maps. The maximal atlas language prevents the course from depending on a particular favorite cover. Once compatibility is fixed, every chart that agrees with it belongs to the same smooth structure.

Examples and Near Misses

Manifold Tests Are Local and Global at Once

Open \(U\subset\mathbb{R}^n\)

The identity chart supplies coordinates and the usual topology already passes the three safeguards.

Circles and Spheres

No single chart sees everything, but overlapping stereographic charts give smooth transitions.

Products

Product charts combine coordinates and preserve local Euclidean dimension additively.

Lie Groups

Manifold charts coexist with multiplication and inverse maps that can be tested smoothly.

\(\mathbb{RP}^n\)

A quotient by nonzero scalar multiples becomes a manifold through homogeneous coordinate charts.

Two Origins

Local Euclidean neighborhoods are present, but Hausdorff separation breaks at the doubled origin.

The defining conditions are independent tests: passing the local Euclidean test does not automatically pass Hausdorffness, second countability, or smooth chart compatibility.

Use the examples to keep students from overfitting to smooth embedded surfaces. Euclidean opens, spheres, products, Lie groups, and projective spaces all enter through charts. The line with two origins is the deliberately uncomfortable example because it looks locally correct while failing a global separation condition.

Coordinate Smoothness

Smooth Functions Are Tested Through a Chart

Function Test

A function \(f:M\to\mathbb{R}\) is smooth near \(p\) when \(f\circ\phi^{-1}\) is an ordinary smooth function on the Euclidean chart domain.

$$h(u)=\frac{u^4+4u^2-1}{(u^2+1)^2},\qquad h'(u)=\frac{-4u(u^2-3)}{(u^2+1)^3}$$

Notebook check: chart-smoothness-check.json

The important move is not the particular rational formula, but the habit it represents. A function on a curved or abstract space is called smooth only after it is pulled back into coordinates. The denominator check confirms the expression is smooth on the chart domain.

Maps Between Manifolds

Smooth Maps Use Source and Target Coordinates

Coordinate Test for Smooth Maps

For \(F:M\to N\), choose \((U,\phi)\) around \(p\) and \((V,\psi)\) around \(F(p)\). The local representative must be smooth:

\(\psi\circ F\circ\phi^{-1}\)

Why Compatibility Is Needed

The result must be independent of chart choices. Smooth transition maps make that independence a theorem rather than a wish.

For maps between manifolds, one chart is no longer enough. The source chart turns inputs into Euclidean coordinates, and the target chart turns outputs into Euclidean coordinates. Smoothness of the middle coordinate expression is the definition, not an afterthought.

Functorial Habit

Composition Works Because Coordinate Expressions Compose

$$\sigma\circ(G\circ F)\circ\phi^{-1}=(\sigma\circ G\circ\psi^{-1})\circ(\psi\circ F\circ\phi^{-1})$$
Consequence

If \(F\) and \(G\) are smooth, then \(G\circ F\) is smooth because the coordinate expression factors as a composition of ordinary smooth Euclidean maps.

This identity is the algebraic reason the definition is stable. Each parenthesized factor is a Euclidean smooth map by hypothesis. Their composition is smooth in ordinary calculus, so the manifold-level composition inherits smoothness through the chosen charts.

Sameness in the Smooth Category

Diffeomorphisms Preserve the Smooth Structure

Diffeomorphism

A map \(F:M\to N\) is a diffeomorphism when it is bijective, smooth, and \(F^{-1}\) is smooth.

Local Coordinates Still Matter

Locally, a diffeomorphism is represented by a smooth coordinate change with a smooth inverse. Globally, it is the correct notion of sameness for smooth manifolds.

Clarify the difference between a smooth bijection and a diffeomorphism. The inverse must also be smooth, because otherwise the coordinate structure may be distorted in one direction. The reversible grid picture is a visual cue for smooth equivalence rather than mere set-theoretic matching.

Quotient Spaces

The Quotient Topology Defines Openness by Pullback

Quotient Topology

For the projection \(\pi:S\to S/{\sim}\), the topology on the quotient is defined by

\(W\subset S/{\sim}\) open iff \(\pi^{-1}(W)\) is open in \(S\).

Stress Test

Quotients are useful because they build spaces by identification. They are dangerous because separation and countability may be lost.

The quotient topology is the rule that decides what the new open sets are. Students should picture a set downstairs as open only when its full preimage upstairs is open. This is the mechanism that makes projective space possible and bad quotients visible.

A Bad Quotient

The Line With Two Origins Fails Hausdorff Separation

Diagnostic

Each origin has a locally Euclidean neighborhood, but the two origin classes cannot be separated by disjoint open neighborhoods.

Notebook Warning

locally Euclidean at each origin: true
Hausdorff separation possible: false

Check: quotient-hausdorff-warning-check.json

This example is the antidote to the misconception that locally Euclidean automatically means manifold. Around either origin the local picture resembles a line. The problem appears only when trying to separate the two origins, because their neighborhoods inevitably meet along the punctured line.

Quotient Checklist

When a Quotient Behaves, Charts and Topology Agree

Manifold Quotient Habit

Track four ledgers at the same time: equivalence classes, quotient opens, chart domains, and transition maps.

Useful Conditions to Inspect

  • Does the quotient topology make chart domains open?
  • Do the proposed charts separate equivalence classes?
  • Are the transition maps smooth on overlap images?
  • Do Hausdorffness and second countability survive?
This slide turns the warning into a method. For a proposed quotient manifold, do not start by drawing a pretty picture and declaring success. Track the equivalence classes, verify the quotient open sets, build chart domains from saturated data, and then run the same compatibility tests as before.

Successful Quotient

Real Projective Space Identifies Points on the Same Line

Projective Equivalence

\(\mathbb{RP}^n=(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/{\sim}\), where

\(x\sim y\) iff \(y=t x\), \(t\in\mathbb{R}^{*}\).

Geometric Meaning

A point \([x]\in\mathbb{RP}^n\) is a line through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\). Homogeneous coordinates remember ratios, not scale.

Projective space is the constructive answer to the quotient warning. We identify all nonzero scalar multiples of a vector, so a class is a line through the origin. The quotient is not accepted on faith; the next charts show how ratios become local coordinates.

Low-Dimensional Model

\(\mathbb{RP}^1\): Antipodal Points, Slopes, and Reciprocal Charts

Slope Charts

On \(x\ne 0\), use slope \(s=y/x\). On \(y\ne 0\), use reciprocal slope \(r=x/y\). On the overlap, \(r=1/s\).

representatives \(y/x\) \(x/y\)
\((2,1)\), \((-2,-1)\) 0.5 2.0
\((1,-3)\), \((-1,3)\) -3.0 -0.333...

Samples: projective-chart-samples.csv; check: projective-atlas-check.json

Use RP one as the accessible model before the general formula. Antipodal points on the circle represent the same line through the origin. The slope chart does not care which antipodal representative was chosen, and the overlap map is the reciprocal function away from zero.

Standard Atlas

The Charts on \(\mathbb{RP}^n\) Use Nonzero Homogeneous Coordinates

Projective Charts

For each coordinate index \(i\),

$$U_i=\{[a]:a_i\ne 0\},\qquad \phi_i([a])=(a_0/a_i,\ldots,\widehat{a_i/a_i},\ldots,a_n/a_i).$$
Transition on \(U_0\cap U_1\)
$$(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\mapsto(1/x_1,x_2/x_1,\ldots,x_n/x_1).$$

The denominator is nonzero on the overlap, so the transition is smooth there.

The standard atlas is where projective space earns its manifold status. Each chart chooses a nonzero homogeneous coordinate and normalizes by it. On overlaps, changing the normalizing coordinate produces rational formulas whose denominators are nonzero precisely on the overlap.

Takeaways

  • Topological manifolds need Hausdorff, second countable, and locally Euclidean structure.
  • Smoothness is always tested through charts.
  • Quotients require topology checks before chart formulas can be trusted.
