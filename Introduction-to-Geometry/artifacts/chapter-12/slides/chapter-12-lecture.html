The Betweenness Relation $[ABC]$

Primitive

$[ABC]:\ B \text{ lies between } A \text{ and } C$

Order axioms

$[ABC] \Rightarrow A \neq C$ and $[CBA]$; but never $[ABC]$ and $[BAC]$.

Symmetry

Reversing endpoints fixes the middle point.

Exclusion

At most one of three points is between the others.

Printed pp. 175-190The Betweenness Relation
The single primitive is the relation bracket A B C, read B lies between A and C. The axioms of order govern it: the endpoints are distinct, reversing them fixes the middle point, and bracket A B C excludes bracket B A C. These austere rules are exactly the obvious facts Coxeter insists we name.

Segments, Rays, and Lines

Segment

$AB = \{P : [APB]\}$, the points strictly between.

Ray

From $A$ through $B$: one side of $A$ on the line.

Line

Two opposite rays plus the interval between $A$ and $B$.

Printed pp. 175-190Segments, Rays, and Lines
From betweenness alone we carve a one-dimensional line into structure. The segment AB is the set of points between A and B; adjoining endpoints gives the interval; the two rays point outward in opposite senses; together the rays and interval reconstitute the entire line without any notion of distance.

The Pasch Axiom

Axiom (Pasch)

A line meeting side $AB$ of $\triangle ABC$, missing the vertices, meets $AC$ or $BC$.

What it asserts

Entering through one side forces an exit through exactly one other.

Printed pp. 175-190The Pasch Axiom
Pasch supplies the first major proof engine. A line entering a triangle through one side, missing the vertices, must leave through exactly one of the other two sides. This crossing invariant is a statement about sidedness and order, never about measured lengths, and it cannot be derived from Euclid's earlier postulates.

Coordinate model

A coordinate model keeps the primitive relation visible. The arithmetic confirms the ordered exit that Pasch's axiom guarantees on purely combinatorial grounds.

Triangles, Sides, and Convex Regions

Sides of a line

A line separates the plane into two convex half-planes.

Interior

Inside $\triangle ABC$: the inner side of all three edges.

Convexity

Regions are intersections of half-planes, no area needed.

Printed pp. 175-190Triangles and Convex Regions
Sides and betweenness let order define interior and exterior without area. A point is inside a triangle when it lies on the inner side of all three edges, so convex regions arise as intersections of half-planes. Separation of the plane by a line is the engine driving every such region argument.

Sylvester's Problem of Collinear Points

Theorem (Sylvester-Gallai)

A finite non-collinear set has a line through exactly two points.

Order proof

Argue on order along a chosen line and an empty segment.

Notebook

An honest finite search counts incidences for every joining line.

Printed pp. 175-190Sylvester's Ordinary Line
Order alone proves a striking incidence fact: any finite, non-collinear set of points admits an ordinary line through exactly two of them. The notebook performs an honest finite search, building every joining line and counting incidences, making the phrase ordinary line concrete before a reader writes the order-based proof.

Planes, Hyperplanes, and Regions

Region count

$f(n,m) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{m}{k}$

Recurrence

$f(n,m) = f(n,m-1) + f(n-1,m-1)$.

Examples

$f(2,3)=7$ regions; $f(3,4)=15$ regions.

Printed pp. 175-190Hyperplanes and Region Counts
Order extends upward through planes and hyperplanes, which slice space into convex cells. In general position m hyperplanes in n dimensions cut a number of regions counted by a sum of binomials obeying a simple Pascal-style recurrence, giving a compact computational check on the separation behavior of arrangements.

Parallelism as Ray Separation

No angles

Through $A$ off line $r$, classify rays that meet $r$.

Boundary

Parallel directions separate the meeting fan from the missing fan.

Veblen

Euclid: two parallels; hyperbolic chapters allow more.

Printed pp. 175-190Parallelism as Ray Separation
Parallelism is recast without angle. Through a point off a line, rays split into those meeting the line and those missing it, and the boundary directions separate the two fans. Euclidean order yields two parallel directions; the later hyperbolic chapters return here to allow more, distinguishing the parallel axioms.
