Cartesian and Polar Coordinates

Cartesian

Ordered pair against perpendicular axes; natural for lines.

Polar

Distance and direction from a pole; natural for circles.

Conversion

$x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta$

Printed pp. 107-134Cartesian and Polar
Descartes addresses each point by an ordered pair against perpendicular axes, while polar coordinates name the same point by distance and direction from a pole. Neither language is superior; we choose Cartesian for lines and rectangles, polar for circles and spirals, and translate freely between them as the geometry demands.

The Equation of a Line

Line

$ax + by + c = 0$

Distance

$d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$

Angle between lines

$\tan\phi = \dfrac{m_2 - m_1}{1 + m_1 m_2}$

Printed pp. 107-134The Equation of a Line
A straight line becomes a first-degree equation in two variables, and the distance and angle formulas follow from the inner product. The distance between two points is a square root of squared coordinate differences, while the angle between two lines is fixed by the ratio of their direction numbers.

Conics by Focus and Directrix

Focus-directrix

$\dfrac{\text{dist to focus}}{\text{dist to directrix}} = e$

$e<1$

Ellipse, a closed oval.

$e=1$

Parabola, a single branch.

$e>1$

Hyperbola, two branches.

Printed pp. 107-134Conics by Focus and Directrix
Each conic is the locus of points whose distance to a fixed focus is a constant multiple of the distance to a fixed directrix. That constant is the eccentricity. Below one, the curve closes into an ellipse; equal to one, it is a parabola; above one, it opens into a hyperbola.
Introduction to GeometryChapter 08

Conics as Sections of a Cone

Circle and ellipse

Cuts that miss the slant; closed curves.

Parabola

A cut parallel to a generating line.

Hyperbola

A steep cut meeting both nappes.

Printed pp. 107-134Conics as Plane Sections
Apollonius saw the three conics as sections of one cone cut at increasing tilt. A horizontal cut gives a circle, a gentle tilt an ellipse, a cut parallel to a slant edge a parabola, and a steeper cut both branches of a hyperbola. The eccentricity records the tilt angle of the cutting plane.

Standard Conic Equations

Ellipse

$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$

Hyperbola

$\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$

Parabola

$y^2 = 4px$

Foci and shape

For the ellipse, $b^2 = a^2(1-e^2)$ and the foci sit at $\pm ae$ along the major axis.

Printed pp. 107-134Standard Conic Equations
In axis-aligned coordinates the ellipse and hyperbola become sums and differences of squared ratios equal to one, while the parabola is purely quadratic in a single variable. The foci sit along the major axis, and the semi-axes and the focal distance obey a simple Pythagorean relation that ties eccentricity to shape.

The General Second-Degree Equation

General conic

$Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$

$B^2-4AC<0$

Ellipse (elliptic type).

$B^2-4AC=0$

Parabola (parabolic type).

$B^2-4AC>0$

Hyperbola (hyperbolic type).

Removing the cross term

Rotating the axes by the right angle eliminates $Bxy$, revealing the principal directions and the standard form.

Printed pp. 107-134The General Second Degree
Every conic is captured by one general second-degree equation in two variables. The discriminant of its quadratic part classifies the curve: negative gives an ellipse, zero a parabola, positive a hyperbola. Rotating the axes removes the cross term, exposing the principal directions and the true shape behind any tilted equation.

Barycentric Coordinates

Weighted average

$P = \dfrac{\alpha A + \beta B + \gamma C}{\alpha + \beta + \gamma}$

Homogeneous

Only the ratios $\alpha:\beta:\gamma$ matter.

Interior

$P$ is inside when all weights are positive.

Printed pp. 107-134Barycentric Coordinates
Mobius weights the three vertices of a triangle so that a point is their balance point, or centroid of the loaded masses. Only the ratios of the weights matter, so the coordinates are homogeneous. A point lies inside the triangle exactly when all three weights can be taken positive, giving an intuitive area picture.

Areal Coordinates as Subtriangle Areas

Normalized

$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1,\quad \alpha = \dfrac{[PBC]}{[ABC]}$

Area fractions

Each coordinate is the area subtended from the opposite side.

Centroid

Equal areas give $\left(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13\right)$.

Printed pp. 107-134Areal Coordinates
Normalizing the barycentric weights to sum to one turns them into the three signed areas of the subtriangles formed by the point and the edges, divided by the whole area. Each areal coordinate is literally the fraction of the triangle subtended from the opposite side, so the coordinates read off as proportions.

Trilinear Coordinates and Triangle Centers

Trilinear

$x : y : z = d_a : d_b : d_c$

Incenter

$1 : 1 : 1$, equal distances to all sides.

Centroid

$\tfrac1a : \tfrac1b : \tfrac1c$.

Circumcenter

$\cos A : \cos B : \cos C$.

Printed pp. 107-134Trilinear Coordinates and Centers
Trilinear coordinates measure a point by its signed perpendicular distances to the three sides. Classical centers acquire memorable forms: the incenter is one to one to one, the centroid uses the reciprocals of the sides, and the circumcenter and orthocenter follow from cosines. Symmetry in the labels signals a genuine triangle center.

Circular and Hyperbolic Functions

Circle

$x^2 + y^2 = 1,\ (x,y)=(\cos t,\sin t)$

Hyperbola

$x^2 - y^2 = 1,\ (x,y)=(\cosh t,\sinh t)$

One sign apart

The parameter $t$ measures sector area in both cases.

Printed pp. 107-134Circular and Hyperbolic Functions
The unit circle parameterized by cosine and sine satisfies x squared plus y squared equals one, while the rectangular hyperbola parameterized by hyperbolic cosine and sine satisfies x squared minus y squared equals one. The analogy is exact apart from a sign in the metric, and the sector area plays the role of the parameter.

The Equiangular Spiral

Polar equation

$r = a\,e^{b\theta}$

Self-similar

Rotating by $\delta$ scales the radius by $e^{b\delta}$.

Constant angle

The tangent meets every radius at the same angle.

Printed pp. 107-134The Equiangular Spiral
The logarithmic spiral has polar equation r equals a times e to the b theta, so rotating the angle multiplies the radius by a fixed factor and reproduces the same shape at a new scale. Its tangent meets every radius at one constant angle, a genuine invariant that makes it the curve unchanged by spiral similarity.

Coordinates in Space

Plane

$ax + by + cz + d = 0$

Sphere

$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = R^2$

Ruled quadric

The hyperbolic paraboloid carries two families of straight lines.

Printed pp. 107-134Coordinates in Space
In three dimensions a line is parametric, a plane is one linear equation, a sphere is one quadratic equation, and a tangent plane is read from the gradient. Quadric surfaces include the ruled hyperbolic paraboloid, whose two families of straight lines sweep a curved surface, showing how compact equations encode rich spatial structure.

Synthesis: Coordinates as Computable Invariants

Plane curves

Discriminant $B^2-4AC$ classifies any conic at a glance.

Triangle

Weights and side-distances pin centers with symmetric forms.

Space

One equation fixes a plane, a sphere, or a ruled surface.

Guiding idea

$\text{geometry} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{equation} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{invariant}$

Always check twice

Each representation is chosen to make one invariant easy to inspect; a second representation guards against error.

Printed pp. 107-134Synthesis
Coordinates compress a geometric decision into a computable invariant: a discriminant classifies a conic, a triangle of weights locates a center, and a single equation pins a surface. Each representation is chosen because it makes a different invariant easy to inspect, and a second representation always checks the first against error.
