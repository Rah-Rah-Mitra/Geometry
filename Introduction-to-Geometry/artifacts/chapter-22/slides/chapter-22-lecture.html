Printed pp. 396-412Building the Tesseract
The measure polytope, or hypercube, is the unit cube swept one unit along the fourth axis. Sweeping doubles the old vertices and joins each to its copy, producing sixteen vertices and thirty-two edges. Its boundary is built from eight cubic cells, twenty-four square faces, and these thirty-two edges.

Printed pp. 396-412Slicing the 16-Cell
The sixteen-cell is the four-dimensional cross-polytope, the unit ball of the l-one norm. Fixing the fourth coordinate at level t leaves a three-dimensional octahedron of radius one minus the absolute value of t. As the plane advances toward a vertex the octahedron shrinks, its volume falling by a cubic law.

Printed pp. 396-412Six Regular Polytopes
Four dimensions admit exactly six regular convex polytopes: the five-cell simplex, the tesseract, the sixteen-cell, the twenty-four-cell, the hundred-twenty-cell, and the six-hundred-cell. This count exceeds the five Platonic solids of three dimensions, yet collapses to only three regular polytopes in every dimension five and above.

The Euler-Poincare Relation

Boundary identity

$V - E + F - C = 0$

Why zero

The boundary is a closed $3$-complex, not a sphere shell of even parity.

Printed pp. 396-412The Euler-Poincare Check
The boundary of a four-polytope is a closed three-dimensional complex, so its alternating face count vanishes: vertices minus edges plus faces minus cells equals zero. This identity audits every f-vector in the table; a miscounted edge or cell almost always shows up as a broken Euler sum, catching errors cheaply.

Why Four Dimensions Is Special

Dimension 2

Infinitely many regular polygons.

Dimension 3

Exactly five Platonic solids.

Dimension 4

Dimension $\ge 5$

Printed pp. 396-412Why Four Is Special
Dimension two hosts infinitely many regular polygons, dimension three exactly five Platonic solids, dimension four a surprising six polychora, and every higher dimension only three. The twenty-four-cell, self-dual and with no three-dimensional analogue, is the exceptional figure responsible for four dimensions being uniquely rich.

Close Packing: the $D_4$ Lattice

Kissing number

$24$ neighbors all at distance $\sqrt{2}$.

Density

$\Delta = \dfrac{\pi^2}{16}$

Printed pp. 396-412D4 Close Packing
The densest equal-sphere packing in four dimensions is the D4 lattice. Its root shell consists of the twenty-four vectors with two nonzero entries of plus or minus one, all of norm root two. Hence each sphere touches twenty-four neighbors, and the lattice achieves packing density pi squared over sixteen.

Every four-dimensional representation should declare what it forgets. Projection discards metric scale but keeps edge incidence; slicing hides one coordinate but leaves a concrete octahedron. Schlafli symbols, the Euler relation, and packing invariants give complementary checks on what a flat shadow of a four-dimensional figure may honestly claim.
