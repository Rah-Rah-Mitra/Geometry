Lattices and the Translation Group

Definition

The lattice is the set of integer combinations of two independent vectors.

Group p1

The translations alone form the simplest wallpaper group.

Cell area

The fundamental parallelogram has area $|\det[\,a\;b\,]|$.

Lattice

$\Lambda = \{\, i\,a + j\,b : i,j \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}$

Translation subgroup

$T(\Lambda) \cong \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$

Printed pp. 50-66Lattices and the Translation Group
A plane lattice is the orbit of one point under two independent translations. Every lattice point is an integer combination of the basis vectors, so the translation subgroup is isomorphic to the integers crossed with the integers. The parallelogram spanned by the basis is a fundamental region whose area equals the determinant magnitude.

Fundamental Regions and Dirichlet Cells

Not unique

Any region tiling once per lattice point is fundamental.

Dirichlet region

The Voronoi cell of nearest points is the canonical choice.

Voronoi cell

$V_0 = \{\, x : \lVert x\rVert \le \lVert x-\ell\rVert\ \forall\,\ell \in \Lambda \,\}$

Printed pp. 50-66Fundamental Regions and Dirichlet Cells
Any region that tiles the plane once per lattice point is a fundamental region, so the choice is far from unique. The canonical choice is the Dirichlet region, the Voronoi cell of nearest points. For a generic oblique basis it is a centrally symmetric hexagon; a rectangular basis collapses two sides and leaves a rectangle of equal area.

Motifs, Half-Turns, and Glides

Motif on a lattice

A pattern is a shape carried to every lattice point.

Half-turn

$H_c(z) = 2c - z$; two half-turns compose to a translation.

Glide

Reflect, then shift along the axis by half a tile.

Glide relation

$g^2 = \tau \quad (\text{a translation, not the identity})$

Printed pp. 50-66Motifs, Half-Turns, and Glides
A wallpaper pattern is a motif carried by a lattice. Attaching an asymmetric motif under translations alone gives the group p1, while a glide reflection interleaves flipped copies. The defining relation is computational and exact: a glide performed twice is a pure translation, never the identity, which separates glides from ordinary mirrors.

Point Groups and the Seventeen Wallpaper Groups

Point group

The finite rotations and reflections fixing a point modulo translations.

Generators

Translations, rotations, mirrors, and glide reflections.

Extension

$1 \to T(\Lambda) \to G \to P \to 1$

Classification

Allowed point groups, combined with translations and the glide-versus-mirror distinction, give exactly 17 plane groups.

Range

From p1 (translations only) to p6m (six-fold with mirrors). Conway's orbifold notation labels the same list.

Printed pp. 50-66Point Groups and Wallpaper Groups
Each wallpaper group has a point group, the finite collection of rotations and reflections fixing a point after translations are quotiented out. Combining the allowed point groups with translations, and distinguishing genuine mirrors from glides, yields exactly seventeen plane crystallographic groups, from the trivial p1 to the fully symmetric p6m.

The Crystallographic Restriction

Nearest-center argument

Take two equal-period centers $P,Q$ at minimum separation.

Contradiction

Rotating one about the other and back makes a closer center for periods $5$ or $>6$.

Allowed orders

$n \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6\}$

Printed pp. 50-66The Crystallographic Restriction
A discrete lattice cannot host every rotation order. Place two equal-period centers at minimum separation, rotate one about the other and back; for period five or beyond six this manufactures a closer same-period center, contradicting minimality. Only orders two, three, four, and six survive the nearest-center argument unscathed.

The Integer Trace Test

Integer matrix

A lattice-preserving rotation is an integer matrix in a lattice basis.

Trace is integer

The trace is basis-independent, hence an integer.

Agreement

Geometry and algebra single out the same four orders.

Trace condition

$\operatorname{tr} R = 2\cos\!\frac{2\pi}{n} \in \mathbb{Z}$

Solutions

$2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{n} \in \{2,1,0,-1,-2\} \Rightarrow n \in \{1,2,3,4,6\}$

Printed pp. 50-66The Integer Trace Test
The restriction has a clean algebraic proof. A rotation preserving a lattice acts as an integer matrix in a lattice basis, so its trace is an integer. A planar rotation of order n has trace two cosine of two pi over n, which is an integer only for n equal to one, two, three, four, or six. The geometry and the algebra agree.

Frieze Groups: The One-Dimensional Analogue

Strip patterns

Periodic in a single direction; translation subgroup $\cong \mathbb{Z}$.

Allowed symmetries

Half-turns, vertical and horizontal mirrors, and glides.

Count

$\textbf{7 frieze groups}$

Printed pp. 50-66Frieze Groups: The One-Dimensional Analogue
Friezes are patterns periodic in a single direction, the strip analogue of wallpaper. Their translation subgroup is one copy of the integers, and the compatible point symmetries are half-turns, horizontal and vertical mirrors, and glides. These combine into exactly seven frieze groups, a small rehearsal for the richer seventeen-fold plane classification.

Regular Tessellations and Duality

Schläfli symbol

$\{p,q\}$: regular $p$-gons, $q$ meeting at each vertex.

Angle balance

$(p-2)(q-2) = 4$

Three solutions

$\{6,3\}$ hexagons, $\{4,4\}$ squares, $\{3,6\}$ triangles.

Duality

Swapping faces and vertices pairs $\{6,3\}$ with $\{3,6\}$; the square tiling $\{4,4\}$ is self-dual.

Printed pp. 50-66Regular Tessellations and Duality
A regular tessellation brace p comma q places regular p-gons with q meeting at each vertex. The angle balance forces p minus two times q minus two to equal four, leaving only hexagons, squares, and triangles. Duality swaps faces and vertices, pairing the hexagonal and triangular tilings while the square tiling is self-dual.

Collinearity and Ordinary Lines

Sylvester-Gallai

Can a finite noncollinear set avoid an ordinary line through exactly two points?

Kelly

No. Pick the point-line pair of least positive distance and derive a contradiction.

Collinearity test

$\det\!\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1\\ x_2 & y_2 & 1\\ x_3 & y_3 & 1\end{pmatrix} = 0$

Printed pp. 50-66Collinearity and Ordinary Lines
Lattice fragments raise the Sylvester-Gallai question: can a finite noncollinear set avoid an ordinary line through exactly two points? Kelly's answer is no. Collinearity is tested by a three-by-three determinant, and the proof picks the point-line pair of least positive distance, deriving a contradiction if any third point lay on it.

Synthesis and Takeaways

Lattice plus cells

A lattice is a translation group; the Dirichlet cell is its nearest-neighbor region.

Seventeen groups

Rotations, mirrors, and glides build all plane crystallographic groups.

Restriction

Only orders $1,2,3,4,6$ survive both the nearest-center and integer-trace tests.

Companions

One angle equation fixes the regular tilings; determinants decide collinearity.

Printed pp. 50-66Synthesis and Takeaways
A planar lattice is a translation group plus the geometry of its cells, and the Dirichlet cell is its nearest-neighbor fundamental region. Adding rotations, mirrors, and glides yields seventeen wallpaper groups, constrained by the crystallographic restriction. Tessellations follow from one angle equation, and finite collinearity is decided by determinants.
