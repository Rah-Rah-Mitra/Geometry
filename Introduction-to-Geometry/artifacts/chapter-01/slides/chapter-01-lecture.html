Congruence and Reflection

Isometry

A reflection preserves every length and angle.

Isosceles base angles

Mirroring through the apex swaps equal sides, forcing equal base angles.

Preservation

$|\sigma(P)-\sigma(Q)| = |P-Q|$

Printed pp. 3-25Congruence and Reflection
Congruence is only convincing once a preservation claim is measured. A reflection is an isometry, so it keeps every length and angle. Mirroring an isosceles triangle through its apex swaps the equal sides, which forces the two base angles to be equal. That measured equality, not the picture, is the proof.

Medians and the Centroid

Affine construction

Only straightness, midpoints, and averaging — never side lengths.

2:1 ratio

The centroid sits two thirds of the way from each vertex.

Barycentric average

$G = \tfrac{1}{3}(A+B+C)$

Printed pp. 3-25Medians and the Centroid
The median construction is purely affine: it uses straightness, midpoints, and averaging, never side lengths. The three medians meet at the centroid, the barycentric average of the vertices. On each median the centroid sits two thirds of the way from the vertex, dividing it in the clean two to one ratio.

Perpendicular Bisectors and the Circumcircle

Equal to vertices

The circumcenter is equidistant from $A$, $B$, $C$.

May escape

It lies outside an obtuse triangle.

Circumradius

$|O-A| = |O-B| = |O-C| = R$

Printed pp. 3-25Perpendicular Bisectors and the Circumcircle
The circumcenter is a metric center, defined by equal distance to the three vertices. It is the common intersection of the three perpendicular bisectors, and that equal distance is precisely the circumradius. The circle it centers passes through all three vertices, and the center may fall outside an obtuse triangle.

Altitudes and the Orthocenter

Perpendicular feet

Each altitude drops from a vertex onto the opposite side.

Can lie outside

For an obtuse triangle the orthocenter leaves the interior.

Concurrence

$H = \text{altitude}_A \cap \text{altitude}_B \cap \text{altitude}_C$

Printed pp. 3-25Altitudes and the Orthocenter
The three altitudes, each dropped from a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side, meet at the orthocenter. Unlike the centroid and circumcenter, the orthocenter can lie far outside the triangle when an angle is obtuse. Its position is the key ingredient that later unlocks both the Euler line and the nine-point circle.

Angle Bisectors and the Incircle

Equal to sides

The incenter is equidistant from the three side lines.

Weighted average

Side lengths $a,b,c$ weight the vertices.

Inradius

$I = \dfrac{aA+bB+cC}{a+b+c}, \quad d(I,\text{sides}) = r$

Printed pp. 3-25Angle Bisectors and the Incircle
The incenter is the second metric center, equally far from the three side lines rather than the three vertices. It is the meeting point of the internal angle bisectors and the side-length weighted average of the vertices. That common distance is the inradius, and the incircle it centers is tangent to all three sides.

The Euler Line

Three centers collinear

Circumcenter $O$, centroid $G$, orthocenter $H$ lie on one line.

A vector identity

Sharper than any drawn straightedge.

Euler relation

$H - G = 2\,(G - O)$

Printed pp. 3-25The Euler Line
For any non-equilateral triangle the circumcenter, centroid, and orthocenter are collinear. The straightedge can mislead, so we record the sharper vector identity instead: the orthocenter minus the centroid equals twice the centroid minus the circumcenter. The centroid therefore splits the segment from circumcenter to orthocenter in a one to two ratio.

The Nine-Point Circle

Nine special points

Three side midpoints, three altitude feet, three Euler points.

Half the circumradius

Center $N$ is the midpoint of $O$ and $H$.

Center and radius

$N = \tfrac{1}{2}(O+H), \quad \rho = \tfrac{1}{2}R$

Printed pp. 3-25The Nine-Point Circle
Once the orthocenter is known the nine-point circle is almost forced. Its center is the midpoint of circumcenter and orthocenter, and its radius is exactly half the circumradius. The single circle passes through nine special points: three side midpoints, three altitude feet, and three midpoints from the vertices to the orthocenter.

The Law of Areas

Base times height

Equivalently, two sides and the included angle.

Bridges the laws

Dividing out yields the law of sines.

Area identity

$K = \tfrac{1}{2}bh = \tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C = rs = \dfrac{abc}{4R}$

Printed pp. 3-25The Law of Areas
Area ties the metric quantities of a triangle together. One half base times height equals one half the product of two sides and the sine of their included angle. Dividing by the side lengths yields the law of sines, and pairing area with semiperimeter recovers both the inradius and the circumradius.

Two Extremum Problems

Fagnano

Least-perimeter inscribed triangle of an acute triangle is the orthic triangle.

Fermat

The point minimizing the distance sum sees the vertices at $120^\circ$.

Fermat point

$\min_P\,(|PA|+|PB|+|PC|) \Rightarrow \angle = 120^\circ$

Printed pp. 3-25Two Extremum Problems
Many clean constructions hide an optimization. Fagnano asks for the inscribed triangle of least perimeter in an acute triangle; the orthic triangle of altitude feet is the answer. Fermat asks for the interior point minimizing the distance sum; below one hundred twenty degrees it sees all three vertices under equal one hundred twenty degree angles.

Morley's Trisector Theorem

Trisect each angle

Intersect adjacent interior trisectors.

Always equilateral

The three meeting points form an equilateral triangle.

The surprise

$|PQ| = |QR| = |RP|$

Printed pp. 3-25Morley's Trisector Theorem
Trisectors usually misbehave, which makes Morley's result startling. Trisect each of the three angles, intersect adjacent interior trisectors, and the three meeting points always form an equilateral triangle. We construct the rays, intersect neighbors, and check that the three resulting side lengths agree, turning an unlikely picture into a measured equality.

Recap: Triangle as Laboratory

Centers, sorted

Centroid is affine; circumcenter and incenter are metric; orthocenter unlocks the rest.

Invariants, not pictures

Euler line is a vector identity; nine-point circle is one radius check.

Theorems as tests

Every claim is a measurable invariant: reflect, average, intersect, then verify the number.

Hidden optimization

Fagnano, Fermat, and Morley reward measurement over intuition.

Printed pp. 3-25Recap: Triangle as Laboratory
The chapter's habit is to treat each theorem as a testable invariant. Congruence is measured preservation; the centroid is affine while incenter and circumcenter are metric; the Euler line is a vector identity; the nine-point circle is one radius check for nine points; and Morley resists the eye but survives the numbers.
