Absolute geometry, Bolyai's neutral geometry, is the body of theorems provable from incidence, order, and congruence before any parallel postulate is chosen. This chapter spanning printed pages 263 to 286 isolates that shared core and treats the parallel axiom as a deferred branch point separating Euclidean from hyperbolic geometry.

The Common Core of Two Geometries

Admitted

Incidence, betweenness (order), and congruence axioms.

Withheld

Any statement about how many parallels a line admits.

Provability discipline

$\text{Axioms}_{\text{abs}} \;\vdash\; T \;\Rightarrow\; T \text{ in both worlds}$

Why it matters

Every absolute theorem survives whichever parallel branch you later take.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286The Common Core
We classify each axiom: incidence, order, and congruence are admitted, but the parallel postulate is withheld. Theorems proved without it hold in both Euclidean and hyperbolic worlds simultaneously. The discipline of absolute geometry is tracking exactly which conclusion depends on which hypothesis, so the parallel branch stays clean.

The Exterior Angle Theorem

Theorem

$\angle_{\text{ext}} \;>\; \angle_{\text{remote interior}}$

Proof tool

A half-turn about a side's midpoint — congruence and betweenness only, no parallels.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286The Exterior Angle Theorem
The exterior angle theorem is the engine of neutral geometry: an exterior angle of a triangle exceeds either remote interior angle. Its proof uses only congruence and betweenness through a midpoint half-turn, never parallels. From it follow comparison of sides and angles and the existence results that organize the rest of the chapter.

Perpendiculars: Existence and Uniqueness

Drop

From any point off a line, exactly one perpendicular reaches it.

Raise

At any point on a line, exactly one perpendicular stands up.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286Perpendiculars Exist and Are Unique
From any point exactly one perpendicular drops to a given line, and exactly one rises at a point on it. Existence and uniqueness both rest on congruence and the exterior angle theorem alone, so perpendicularity is fully absolute. Two distinct perpendiculars to one line would build a triangle with two right angles, which the exterior angle theorem forbids.

The Saccheri Quadrilateral

Construction

Two equal sides perpendicular to a base; the top edge is the summit.

Absolute fact

$\angle_{\text{summit, left}} \;=\; \angle_{\text{summit, right}}$

Open question

Are they right or merely acute? The parallel postulate decides.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286The Saccheri Quadrilateral
A Saccheri quadrilateral has two equal sides perpendicular to a base. A reflection across the perpendicular bisector of the base shows the summit angles are congruent. Absolute geometry proves they are equal but cannot prove them right; whether they are right or acute is precisely the question the parallel postulate decides.

The Lambert Quadrilateral

Three right angles

Half of a Saccheri figure, cut along its axis of symmetry.

Fourth angle

$\angle_4 \;\le\; \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \quad \angle_4 \ne \text{obtuse}$

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286The Lambert Quadrilateral
A Lambert quadrilateral carries three right angles by construction; half of a Saccheri figure is exactly such a quadrilateral. The fourth angle inherits the summit behavior: right in the Euclidean case, acute in the hyperbolic case. It can never be obtuse, a fact that already excludes the spherical alternative within absolute geometry.

The Saccheri–Legendre Theorem

Theorem

$\alpha + \beta + \gamma \;\le\; \pi$

Proof idea

Halve the defect repeatedly; an excess over $\pi$ would force a negative angle.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286Saccheri-Legendre Theorem
The Saccheri-Legendre theorem states the angle sum of any triangle is at most a straight angle. The proof repeatedly halves a triangle's angle defect; were the sum to exceed pi, iteration would force a negative angle, a contradiction. This bound is absolute, holding without any appeal to parallels.
Introduction to GeometryChapter 15

Angle Sum and the Defect

Definition

$\delta(T) \;=\; \pi - (\alpha+\beta+\gamma) \;\ge\; 0$

Additivity

Split a triangle and its defect splits too: $\delta(T)=\delta(T_1)+\delta(T_2)$.

Consequence

One zero-defect triangle forces zero defect everywhere — the question is global.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286Angle Sum and Defect
Define the defect as pi minus the angle sum; Saccheri-Legendre makes it nonnegative. Defect is additive: splitting a triangle splits its defect among the pieces. A single triangle with zero defect forces zero defect everywhere, which is why the angle sum question is global rather than local in absolute geometry.

The All-or-Nothing Dichotomy

Euclidean case

Every triangle has angle sum exactly $\pi$; defect is zero throughout.

Hyperbolic case

Every triangle has angle sum strictly below $\pi$; defect is positive throughout.

Theorem

$\big(\forall T:\, \delta=0\big)\ \ \text{or}\ \ \big(\forall T:\, \delta>0\big)$

No middle ground

Additive defect propagates a single triangle's verdict to the whole plane.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286The Dichotomy
Either every triangle has angle sum exactly pi, giving Euclidean geometry, or every triangle falls strictly below pi, giving hyperbolic geometry. There is no mixed world. This all-or-nothing dichotomy is the deepest absolute theorem: the additive defect propagates one triangle's verdict to the entire plane at once.

The Parallel Postulate as a Switch

Unique parallel

Summit angles right, defect $=0$, Euclidean geometry.

Many parallels

Summit angles acute, defect $>0$, hyperbolic geometry.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286The Parallel Postulate Branch
The parallel postulate is the switch. Assert a unique parallel and the summit angles become right, defect vanishes, and Euclid follows. Assert many parallels and the summit angles are acute, defect is positive, and hyperbolic geometry follows. Absolute geometry is everything proved before this switch is thrown.

Parallel Rays vs. Ultraparallels

Limiting parallels

Share an ideal endpoint; meet at angle zero on the boundary.

Ultraparallels

No shared endpoint, but a unique common perpendicular.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286Parallel Rays and Ultraparallels
Within the neutral framework parallelism splits in two. Limiting parallel rays share an ideal endpoint and meet at angle zero there; ultraparallel lines share no endpoint but admit a unique common perpendicular. The Poincare disk serves only as a consistency model, distinguishing endpoint behavior from common-perpendicular behavior without smuggling in Euclidean uniqueness.

What Absolute Geometry Keeps

Kept before the choice

  • Exterior angle theorem
  • Existence and uniqueness of perpendiculars
  • Saccheri–Legendre bound $\le\pi$

Deferred to the choice

  • Right vs. acute summit angles
  • Zero vs. positive defect
  • One vs. many parallels
One question, whole geometry

$\delta \equiv 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{Euclid}, \qquad \delta > 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{hyperbolic}$

Boundary layer

Read each result by asking what survives before the parallel axiom is chosen.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 263–286What Absolute Geometry Keeps
Absolute geometry is the boundary layer of the course: enough metric structure for congruence, perpendiculars, and the Saccheri-Legendre bound, yet deliberately silent on parallels. Read each result by asking what survives before the parallel axiom is chosen. The defect dichotomy then makes that single deferred choice decide the whole geometry.
