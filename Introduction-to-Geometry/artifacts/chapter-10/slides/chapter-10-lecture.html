What Makes a Solid Regular

Congruent faces

Every face is the same regular polygon.

Identical vertices

Each vertex sees the same arrangement of faces.

Equal edges

All edges share one length.

Regularity is equality

$\text{faces}\cong,\quad \text{edges}=,\quad \text{vertices}\sim$

Why it matters

Euler counts alone cannot certify any of these equalities; metric checks are separate.

Printed pp. 148-159What Makes a Solid Regular
Regularity is strict. Every face must be one congruent regular polygon, every vertex must look identical, and every edge the same length. The Schlaefli Symbol {p,q}

Two integers

$p$ sides per face; $q$ faces around each vertex.

Local rule, global object

The pair fixes every count and radius that follows.

Reading the symbol

$\{p,q\}:\ \text{faces are regular } p\text{-gons},\ q \text{ meet per vertex}$

Printed pp. 148-159The Schlaefli Symbol {p,q}
The symbol packs the whole local rule into two integers. Faces are regular p-gons and exactly q of them meet around each vertex. From this single pair every count and every radius in the chapter follows, which is why we never hide p and q inside a mesh. Read each solid by naming its pair first.

Where Four Solids Come From

Pyramid

Triangular pyramid gives the tetrahedron.

Prism

A square prism of the right height is the cube.

Antiprism

The triangular antiprism is the octahedron.

Capped antiprism

Pentagonal antiprism plus two caps gives the icosahedron.

Printed pp. 148-159Where Four Solids Come From
Four of the five appear inside familiar families. The tetrahedron is a triangular pyramid, the cube is a square prism, the octahedron is a triangular antiprism, and the icosahedron combines a pentagonal antiprism with two pentagonal caps. Inspecting the vertex pattern in each family is a combinatorial check; all three families have Euler characteristic two.

Drawings: Net and Schlegel Diagram

Net

Unfold the faces flat; fold edges are kept, angles are lost.

Schlegel diagram

Project the graph into the plane; the outside region counts as one face.

Printed pp. 148-159Drawings: Net and Schlegel
A solid can be drawn many ways and each drawing keeps different information. A net records faces and fold edges but loses the final angles. A Schlegel diagram projects the vertex-edge graph into the plane and counts the outside as one face. That projection is exactly why Euler's formula can be proved by studying flat planar maps.

Euler's Polyhedron Formula

Theorem

$V - E + F = 2$

New vertex

An edge to a fresh vertex raises $V$ and $E$ together.

Closing a region

An edge joining old vertices raises $E$ and $F$ together.

Invariant build

Start from one vertex and the exterior region. Each step preserves the alternating sum, so it stays $2$.

Either move

$\Delta(V-E+F) = (1-1+0) = (0-1+1) = 0$

Printed pp. 148-159Euler's Polyhedron Formula
Euler's formula bridges drawing and classification. Build the Schlegel map edge by edge: a new edge to a new vertex raises V and E together, while an edge closing a region raises E and F together. Starting from one vertex and the exterior region, the alternating sum V minus E plus F stays fixed at two throughout.

The Counting Relations

Edges at vertices

Each of $V$ vertices has $q$ edge-ends; each edge has two ends.

Sides of faces

Each of $F$ faces has $p$ sides; each edge borders two faces.

Two relations

$qV = 2E = pF$

Solve with Euler

$E = \dfrac{2pq}{2p+2q-pq}$

Printed pp. 148-159The Counting Relations
Two incidence counts complete the picture. Counting edge-ends at vertices gives qV equals twice E, and counting sides of faces gives pF equals twice E. Each edge is shared by two vertices and two faces, which is the source of both factors of two. Combined with Euler's formula these relations pin down V, E, and F exactly.

Why Exactly Five

Positivity forces

$\dfrac{1}{p} + \dfrac{1}{q} > \dfrac{1}{2} \iff (p-2)(q-2) < 4$

Constraints

$p \ge 3$ and $q \ge 3$ for any genuine polyhedron.

The five survivors

$\{3,3\},\ \{4,3\},\ \{3,4\},\ \{5,3\},\ \{3,5\}$.

No metric needed

The bound is purely combinatorial: the list closes at five.

Printed pp. 148-159Why Exactly Five
Solving the counting relations with Euler's formula forces a denominator that must stay positive. That positivity is equivalent to the inequality on p and q shown here, which only the five admissible pairs satisfy. The argument is purely combinatorial: no metric input is needed to prove that the list of regular convex polyhedra closes at five.

Duality: Faces Become Vertices

Reciprocal symbol

$\{p,q\}^{*} = \{q,p\}$

Face-center model

Put a point at each face center; join centers of edge-sharing faces.

Perpendicularity

Each reciprocal edge crosses its original edge at a right angle.

Printed pp. 148-159Duality: Faces Become Vertices
Reciprocation swaps the symbol p with q. A clean computational model places a point at each face center and joins two centers when their faces share an edge, exchanging vertices and faces while keeping the edge count. For a regular solid each reciprocal edge is perpendicular to the original edge it crosses, a fact we test numerically.

Radii and the Characteristic Tetrahedron

Three nested spheres

$R_0$ to a vertex, $R_1$ to an edge midpoint, $R_2$ to a face plane.

Central half-angle

$\cos\phi = \dfrac{\cos(\pi/p)}{\sin(\pi/q)}$

Dihedral angle

$\pi - 2\psi = 2\arcsin\!\dfrac{\cos(\pi/q)}{\sin(\pi/p)}$

Printed pp. 148-159Radii and the Characteristic Tetrahedron
The metric data is packaged by one right-angled tetrahedron whose corners are a vertex, an edge midpoint, a face center, and the solid's center. From it come three nested spheres: circumradius to a vertex, midradius to an edge midpoint, inradius to a face. The central half-angle and dihedral angle follow from the same right triangles.

Euler Is Necessary, Not Sufficient

The trap

A rectangular box has the cube's exact $V,E,F$ counts.

Same topology

$V-E+F = 8-12+6 = 2$

But not regular

Edge lengths split into three sizes; face-center radii are unequal.

The real test

Prove equal faces, equal edges, equal vertex neighborhoods, or use the $\{p,q\}$ formulas.

Printed pp. 148-159Euler Is Necessary, Not Sufficient
A closing guardrail. A rectangular box shares the cube's vertex, edge, and face counts and its reciprocal has the octahedral graph, yet it is not regular: edge lengths split into three sizes. So proving only that V minus E plus F equals two does not prove regularity; we must also check equal faces, equal edges, and equal vertex neighborhoods.
