The Argand Diagram

Why a plane?

$x^2+1=0$ has no point on the real line.

Density is not completeness

Rationals are dense yet leave gaps; the plane is the next home.

Identification

$z = x + iy \;\longleftrightarrow\; (x,y), \qquad i^2 = -1$

Two axes

Horizontal real part, vertical imaginary part.

Printed pp. 135-147The Argand Diagram
The equation x squared plus one equals zero has no point on the real line, so we enlarge the stage. Writing a number as x plus i y identifies it with the plane point with coordinates x and y. The Argand diagram is the coordinate system in which all later geometry happens.

Modulus and Argument

Modulus

$r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is distance to the origin.

Argument

$\theta=\arg z$ is the angle from the real axis.

Polar form

$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\,e^{i\theta}$.

Printed pp. 135-147Modulus and Argument
Each nonzero complex number carries two polar coordinates: its modulus r, the distance to the origin, and its argument theta, the angle from the positive real axis. Cartesian form suits addition; polar form suits multiplication, since the product law lives entirely in moduli and arguments.

Addition is Translation

Parallelogram rule

$u+v$ completes the parallelogram on $u$ and $v$.

Difference is displacement

$(u+v)-u = v$ reads the arrow between points.

Printed pp. 135-147Addition is Translation
Adding complex numbers obeys the parallelogram rule because it is exactly vector addition of position vectors. The difference v equals u plus v minus u recovers the displacement, so subtraction reads off the arrow from one point to another. Addition never touches the polar data of a single multiplier.

Multiplication: Rotate and Scale

Polar law

Moduli multiply, arguments add.

Action of $a=r e^{i\varphi}$

$z \;\mapsto\; a z = r\,e^{i\varphi}\,z$

As a real matrix

$\begin{bmatrix}p&-q\\q&p\end{bmatrix}$, columns equal-length and perpendicular.

Printed pp. 135-147Multiplication: Rotate and Scale
Multiplying z by a equals r e to the i theta scales z by r and rotates it through theta. The real matrix with columns p, q and minus q, p makes this concrete: its columns are equal length and perpendicular, so the map is a spiral similarity about the origin, never a shear.

Similarities of the Plane

Direct similarities

Orientation-preserving maps are exactly $z\mapsto az+b$.

Opposite similarities

Orientation-reversing maps are $z\mapsto a\bar z+b$.

Classification

$z\mapsto az+b \quad(\text{direct}),\qquad z\mapsto a\bar z+b \quad(\text{opposite})$

Reading the parts

$|a|$ is the scale factor, $\arg a$ the rotation, $b$ the translation.

Printed pp. 135-147Similarities of the Plane
Because multiplication is rotation and scaling, every direct similarity of the plane is z maps to a z plus b, fixing the orientation. Conjugating first gives the opposite similarities z maps to a z-bar plus b, which reverse orientation. Together these two families exhaust all similarity transformations of the Argand plane.

De Moivre's Theorem

Powers in polar form

Raising to a power scales radius and multiplies the angle.

de Moivre

$(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta$

Unit case

$|z|=1$ gives a pure rotation by $n\theta$.

Printed pp. 135-147De Moivre's Theorem
In polar form powers are trivial: r e to the i theta raised to the n equals r to the n e to the i n theta, which is de Moivre's theorem. Moduli multiply and arguments add, so taking a power scales the radius and multiplies the angle by n, turning repeated multiplication into a single geometric rule.

The $n$th Roots of Unity

The roots

$z^n=1$ has solutions $\omega^k$, $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$.

A regular polygon

They sit on the unit circle, equally spaced by $2\pi/n$.

$n=5$

$\omega^k = e^{2\pi i k/5},\quad k=0,\dots,4$

Printed pp. 135-147nth Roots of Unity
The n solutions of z to the n equals one are the powers of omega equals e to the two pi i over n. They sit on the unit circle at equal angular steps of two pi over n, so they are precisely the vertices of a regular n-gon. The pentagon shows the case n equals five.

Euler's Formula

Series to circle

The exponential series splits into cosine and sine.

Euler

$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$

The famous case

$e^{i\pi}+1=0$: a half-turn home.

Printed pp. 135-147Euler's Formula
Expanding the exponential series and splitting real from imaginary terms yields e to the i theta equals cosine theta plus i sine theta, so the exponential traces the unit circle. The special case e to the i pi plus one equals zero is a half turn to minus one followed by a return to the origin.

The Cross-Ratio of Four Points

Definition

One complex number built from four points.

Cross-ratio

$(z_1,z_2;z_3,z_4)=\dfrac{(z_1-z_3)(z_2-z_4)}{(z_1-z_4)(z_2-z_3)}$

Concyclicity test

Real value $\iff$ the four points are concyclic or collinear.

Printed pp. 135-147The Cross-Ratio
Four complex points have a cross-ratio, a single complex number built from their six pairwise differences. It is real exactly when the four points are concyclic or collinear, so the cross-ratio is the algebraic test for lying on a common circle or line, a fact central to inversive geometry.

Mobius Transformations

Linear fractional map

$z \mapsto \dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}, \qquad ad-bc \ne 0$

Built from pieces

Translations, dilative rotations, and inversion $z\mapsto 1/z$.

Invariants

Preserve generalized circles and the cross-ratio; conformal.

Printed pp. 135-147Mobius Transformations
Linear fractional maps z maps to a z plus b over c z plus d, with a d minus b c nonzero, compose translations, dilative rotations, and inversion. They send generalized circles to generalized circles, preserve the cross-ratio, and are conformal: wherever the derivative is nonzero, local angles are preserved exactly.

Geometry by Complex Coordinates

Coordinates that compute

Plane theorems become single algebraic identities.

Equilateral triangle

$z_1^2+z_2^2+z_3^2 = z_1z_2+z_2z_3+z_3z_1$

Recap

Add to translate, multiply to spiral, take a real cross-ratio to test circles.

Printed pp. 135-147Geometry by Complex Coordinates
Assigning complex coordinates turns plane theorems into algebra. Equilateral triangles satisfy a clean quadratic relation among their vertices, rotations are multiplications by unit numbers, and concyclicity is a real cross-ratio. The chapter's lesson: choose coordinates where the invariant you care about becomes a single computable expression.
