The Surface Patch

Two parameters

$\mathbf r(u,v)$ maps a region of the plane into space.

Tangent basis

$\mathbf r_u,\ \mathbf r_v$ span the tangent plane.

Regularity

$\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\ne 0$ keeps the patch smooth.

Tangent vectors

$\mathbf r_u=\partial_u\mathbf r,\qquad \mathbf r_v=\partial_v\mathbf r$

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365The Surface Patch
A regular patch is a smooth map of two parameters into space whose partial derivatives stay independent. Those two derivative vectors span the tangent plane at each point, and their cross product gives the unit normal. The sphere example shows why charts can fail at poles.

First Fundamental Form

The induced metric: distance from inside the surface

Definition

$\mathrm{I}=E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2$

Coefficients

$E=\mathbf r_u\!\cdot\!\mathbf r_u,\ F=\mathbf r_u\!\cdot\!\mathbf r_v,\ G=\mathbf r_v\!\cdot\!\mathbf r_v$

Length

Arc length of a curve integrates $\sqrt{\mathrm I}$.

Area

$dA=\sqrt{EG-F^2}\,du\,dv$.

Angle

Read directly from $E,F,G$ between tangent directions.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365First Fundamental Form
The first fundamental form is the dot product of tangent vectors written in the parameter coordinates. Its coefficients E, F, and G let us measure lengths of curves, angles between directions, and areas entirely from inside the surface, without ever referring to the ambient space.

Unit Normal and Second Form

Unit normal

$\mathbf n=\dfrac{\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v}{|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|}$

Second form

$\mathrm{II}=L\,du^2+2M\,du\,dv+N\,dv^2$

Coefficients

$L=\mathbf r_{uu}\!\cdot\!\mathbf n,\ M=\mathbf r_{uv}\!\cdot\!\mathbf n,\ N=\mathbf r_{vv}\!\cdot\!\mathbf n$

Meaning

$\mathrm{II}$ records how fast the surface leaves its tangent plane.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Unit Normal and Second Form
The unit normal points off the surface, and the second fundamental form measures how the surface pulls away from its tangent plane. Its coefficients L, M, and N come from dotting second derivatives of the patch with the normal, recording bending rather than intrinsic distance.

Normal Curvature

Curvature of a normal section in a chosen direction

Quotient of forms

$\kappa_n=\dfrac{L\,du^2+2M\,du\,dv+N\,dv^2}{E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2}$

Idea

Rotate a normal plane; each angle selects a section curve.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Normal Curvature
Slice the surface with a plane containing the normal and a chosen tangent direction. The curvature of that section is the normal curvature, equal to the second form divided by the first in that direction. It is a ratio of two quadratic forms in the direction coefficients.

Principal Curvatures and Euler's Theorem

Extremes

$\kappa_1=\max\kappa_n,\quad \kappa_2=\min\kappa_n$.

Directions

Principal directions are perpendicular in the metric.

Euler's theorem

$\kappa_n(\theta)=\kappa_1\cos^2\theta+\kappa_2\sin^2\theta$

Reading

Two numbers reconstruct every normal section.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Principal Curvatures and Euler
As the cutting direction rotates, normal curvature reaches a maximum and a minimum, the principal curvatures, along perpendicular principal directions. Euler's theorem expresses curvature in any direction as a cosine-squared blend of these two extremes, recovering every normal section from just two numbers.

Principal Directions as an Eigenproblem

Generalized eigenproblem

$B\,\mathbf a=\kappa\,G\,\mathbf a$

Eigenvalues

The two roots are the principal curvatures $\kappa_1,\kappa_2$.

Eigenvectors

Give the principal directions, $G$-orthogonal.

Rayleigh quotient

$\kappa_n=\dfrac{\mathbf a^{\!\top}B\,\mathbf a}{\mathbf a^{\!\top}G\,\mathbf a}$

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Principal Directions as Eigenproblem
Finding principal data is a generalized eigenvalue problem: the second form acting on a direction equals a curvature times the first form acting on it. The eigenvalues are the principal curvatures and the eigenvectors are the principal directions, automatically orthogonal in the surface metric.

Gaussian and Mean Curvature

Gaussian curvature

$K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\dfrac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}$

Product

The determinant ratio of the two forms.

Mean curvature

$H=\tfrac12(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)$

Average

$H=0$ characterizes minimal surfaces and soap films.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Gaussian and Mean Curvature
From the two principal curvatures we form two symmetric combinations. Gaussian curvature is their product and equals the ratio of the two form determinants. Mean curvature is their average. Gaussian curvature classifies local shape, while mean curvature governs minimal surfaces and soap films.

The Sign of K Classifies Local Shape

$K>0$ synclastic

Both curvatures same sign; surface stays on one side.

$K=0$ parabolic

One curvature vanishes; developable, like a cylinder.

$K<0$ anticlastic

Opposite signs; the surface crosses its tangent plane.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Sign of K Classifies Shape
The sign of Gaussian curvature sorts every surface point into three types. Positive curvature gives a bowl that stays on one side of its tangent plane, zero gives a flat or developable direction, and negative gives a saddle that crosses its tangent plane along two directions.
Introduction to GeometryChapter 19

Three Model Surfaces

Positive, zero, and negative Gaussian curvature

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Three Model Surfaces
The sphere has constant positive curvature everywhere, the cylinder has zero curvature because one principal curvature vanishes, and the saddle has negative curvature with principal curvatures of opposite sign. These three pictures anchor every later statement about how surfaces bend in space.

Gauss's Theorema Egregium

Theorem

$K$ depends only on $E,F,G$ and their derivatives.

Intrinsic

Curvature is computable from the first form alone.

Bending-invariant

Isometries preserve $K$, so flat paper cannot wrap a sphere.

Consequence

The plane and cylinder are isometric; both have $K=0$.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Theorema Egregium
Gauss's remarkable theorem states that Gaussian curvature, though defined using the ambient normal, can be computed from the first fundamental form alone. Curvature is therefore intrinsic and survives any bending that preserves distances, which is why flat paper cannot wrap a sphere without distortion.

Umbilics and Dupin's Indicatrix

Umbilic

$\kappa_1=\kappa_2$: every direction is principal.

Indicatrix

$L x^2+2M xy+N y^2=\pm1$

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Umbilics and Dupin's Indicatrix
At an umbilic the two principal curvatures coincide, so every direction is principal and the surface looks locally spherical. The Dupin indicatrix is the tangent-plane conic that encodes all normal curvatures at a point, an ellipse, hyperbola pair, or parallel lines by sign.

Dupin's and Liouville's Theorems

Dupin

Three mutually orthogonal surface families cut lines of curvature on each other.

Mechanism

Orthogonality forces $F=0$ and $M=0$: curvature-line coordinates.

Liouville

Angle-preserving space maps send spheres to spheres.

Model

Inversion maps a plane to a sphere; planes are spheres through infinity.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Dupin and Liouville
Dupin's theorem says three mutually orthogonal families of surfaces cut lines of curvature on one another, giving curvature-line coordinates for free. Liouville's theorem says angle-preserving maps of space send spheres to spheres, with planes counted as spheres through infinity, modeled by inversion.

Recap: The Local Toolkit

Two forms

$\mathrm I$ measures distance; $\mathrm{II}$ measures bending.

Curvature

$\kappa_1,\kappa_2$ give $K=\kappa_1\kappa_2$ and $H=\tfrac12(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)$.

Classification

Sign of $K$: sphere, cylinder, saddle.

Egregium

$K$ is intrinsic, an invariant of the surface itself.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · printed pp. 342-365Recap
We assembled a complete local toolkit: the first form measures intrinsic distance, the second form measures bending, their interplay yields principal and Gaussian curvature, and Theorema Egregium makes that curvature an invariant of the surface itself rather than of any particular embedding in space.
