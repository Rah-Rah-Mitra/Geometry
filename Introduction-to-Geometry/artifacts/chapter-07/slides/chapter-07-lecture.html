Isometry as Distance Preservation

Affine rule

A motion sends $x \mapsto Mx + t$ with orthogonal $M$.

Two tests

Distances fixed; $\det M = \pm 1$ records sense.

Definition

$M^{\mathsf T} M = I,\qquad \det M = \pm 1$

Detector

Sign of $\det\!\big[B{-}A,\,C{-}A,\,D{-}A\big]$ flags direct vs opposite.

Printed pp. 96-106Isometry as Distance Preservation
We model a spatial motion as an affine rule that sends x to Mx plus a translation, where M is orthogonal. Such a map fixes every pairwise distance, and the determinant of M, equal to plus or minus one, records whether sense is preserved or reversed.

Reflections Generate Everything

One mirror

Reflection fixes its plane and reverses sense.

Structure theorem

Every spatial isometry is a product of at most four reflections.

Reflection rule

$x \mapsto x - 2\,(n\!\cdot\! x - d)\,n$

Counting

0 reflections fix all; 1 is opposite; 2 give a direct motion.

Printed pp. 96-106Reflections Generate Everything
A single reflection fixes its mirror plane and reverses orientation. The fundamental structural theorem states that every isometry of three-space is a product of at most four plane reflections, so reflections are the atomic generators from which all six motion types are assembled.

Products of Two Reflections

Intersecting mirrors

Rotation about the common line by $2\alpha$.

Parallel mirrors

Translation by twice the gap, perpendicular.

Printed pp. 96-106Products of Two Reflections
Composing two reflections always yields a direct isometry. Intersecting mirrors produce a rotation about their common line through twice the dihedral angle, while parallel mirrors produce a translation perpendicular to them with length twice their separation. These are the two simplest direct motions.

Euler's Theorem

Statement

A direct isometry fixing a point is a rotation about an axis.

Why

It is a product of two reflections in planes through that point.

Theorem

$\det M = +1,\; Mp = p \;\Rightarrow\; M = R_{\text{axis}}(\theta)$

Consequence

Every point-fixing direct motion has a real eigenvector: the axis.

Printed pp. 96-106Euler's Theorem
Euler's theorem asserts that any direct isometry leaving a point fixed must be a rotation about an axis through that point. Because a product of two reflections in intersecting planes is exactly such a rotation, every point-fixing direct motion reduces to a single turn about one line.

The Six Isometry Types

Direct  ($\det = +1$)

  • Translation — no fixed point
  • Rotation — fixed axis
  • Screw — rotation + axial slide

Opposite  ($\det = -1$)

  • Reflection — fixed plane
  • Glide reflection — reflect + slide
  • Rotatory reflection — rotate + reflect
Printed pp. 96-106The Six Isometry Types
The full classification names three direct motions and three opposite ones. Direct: translation, rotation, and screw displacement. Opposite: reflection, glide reflection, and rotatory reflection. The determinant sign separates the two columns, and fixed-set structure separates rows within each column.

The Screw Displacement (Twist)

Definition

Rotation about an axis combined with a translation along it.

Orbits

Helices: constant radius, constant axial step per turn. No fixed point.

Printed pp. 96-106The Screw Displacement
A screw displacement, or twist, combines a rotation about an axis with a translation along that same axis. It is the generic direct isometry and has no fixed point. Its orbits are helices of constant radius advancing a fixed axial step after each full turn.

Rotatory Reflection

Definition

Rotate about an axis, then reflect in the perpendicular plane.

Special case

Half-turn plus that reflection gives the central inversion.

Printed pp. 96-106Rotatory Reflection
A rotatory reflection rotates about an axis and then reflects in the plane perpendicular to that axis. It is the generic opposite isometry. The special case of a half-turn followed by that reflection gives the central inversion, which reverses sense in three dimensions.

Central Inversion

Rule

Through a center: $x \mapsto 2O - x$; at the origin, $x \mapsto -x$.

Subtlety

Opposite in 3D, though a plane half-turn is direct.

Why opposite

$-I = R_x R_y R_z,\qquad \det(-I) = -1$

Reading

Product of three perpendicular plane reflections.

Printed pp. 96-106Central Inversion
Central inversion sends each point to its antipode through a fixed center, the rule x maps to minus x at the origin. In space it is the product of three perpendicular plane reflections, so its determinant is minus one and it is opposite, unlike the direct plane half-turn.

Similarities in Space

Dilative rotation

Fixed center; rotate about an axis and scale by $\lambda$.

One formula, many cases

Rotations, reflections, inversion, dilation all appear.

Model rule

$x \mapsto O + \lambda\,R_{\text{axis}}(\theta)\,(x - O)$

Orientation

$\det(\lambda R) = \lambda^3$, so $\lambda < 0$ reverses sense.

Printed pp. 96-106Similarities in Space
A non-isometric similarity has a fixed center and combines a rotation about an axis with a dilatation by a scale lambda. The model rule scales by lambda after rotating, and since the spatial determinant is lambda cubed, a negative scale factor reverses orientation while a positive one preserves it.
