The Power of a Point

Definition

The signed quantity $d^2 - r^2$ for a point at distance $d$ from the center of a circle of radius $r$.

Secant rule

Every line through $P$ meeting the circle gives the same product of signed segments.

Power

$\operatorname{pow}(P)= d^2 - r^2 = \overline{PA}\cdot\overline{PB}$

Tangent

Outside the circle the power equals the square of the tangent length; inside it is negative.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Power of a Point
The power of a point measures how far that point sits from a circle, signed by whether it lies outside or inside. Every secant or tangent drawn through the point yields the same product, which makes power a coordinate-free invariant we reuse throughout the chapter.

Radical Axis and Radical Center

Radical axis

Locus of points with equal power to two circles — a line perpendicular to the line of centers.

Radical center

Three circles give three radical axes that meet in one point.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Radical Axis and Center
Setting the powers with respect to two circles equal carves out the radical axis, a straight line perpendicular to the line joining their centers. Three circles taken pairwise give three radical axes that concur in a single radical center, a fact that anchors many constructions.

Coaxal Systems of Circles

Common axis

A pencil of circles sharing one radical axis.

Two types

Intersecting (through two points) or non-intersecting (two limiting points).

Orthogonality

The two types are mutually orthogonal pencils.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Coaxal Systems
A coaxal system is a one-parameter pencil of circles sharing a common radical axis. The intersecting type passes through two fixed points, while the non-intersecting type has two limiting point-circles. Each pencil is the orthogonal family of the other, forming a curved coordinate grid.

Inversion in a Circle

The map

Along each ray from $O$, send $P$ to $P'$ with $OP\cdot OP'=r^2$.

Involution

Applying inversion twice returns the original point.

Inversion

$\mathbf{x}\;\mapsto\;\dfrac{r^2\,\mathbf{x}}{|\mathbf{x}|^2}$

Fixed set

The circle of inversion is fixed point by point; its interior and exterior swap.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Inversion in a Circle
Inversion fixes a center and radius, then maps each point along its ray so the product of the two distances equals the radius squared. Points near the center fly outward, distant points move inward, the circle stays fixed pointwise, and applying the map twice restores the original.

Generalized Circles to Generalized Circles

Convention

A line is a circle through the point at infinity.

Four cases

Through the center or not decides line versus circle.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Circles to Circles
Treating a line as a circle through infinity, inversion sends generalized circles to generalized circles. Lines through the center stay lines, other lines become circles through the center, circles through the center straighten into lines, and all remaining circles map to circles.

Inversion Preserves Angles

Conformal

Angles between curves keep their magnitude; orientation reverses.

Consequence

Orthogonal circle pencils remain orthogonal after inversion.

Angle invariance

$\angle(C_1,C_2)=\angle(C_1',C_2')$

Proof idea

Track tangent directions of two curves through the radial map at a common point.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Conformality
Inversion is conformal: it preserves the magnitude of angles between curves while reversing their sense. The proof tracks tangent directions through the radial map. This angle-preserving property is exactly why orthogonal circle pencils stay orthogonal after inversion, keeping the coaxal grid intact.

Orthogonal Circles

Right angle

Tangents, hence radii, are perpendicular at each intersection.

Self-inverse

Inversion in one circle fixes any orthogonal circle as a set.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Orthogonal Circles
Two circles meet orthogonally when their tangents, equivalently their radii, are perpendicular at each intersection. Algebraically the squared distance between centers equals the sum of squared radii. Orthogonal circles are self-inverse, so inversion in one fixes the other as a set.
Introduction to GeometryChapter 06

Inversive Distance

Invariant

A number attached to two non-intersecting circles, unchanged by any inversion.

From centers

Computed from the centers and radii of the pair.

Inversive distance

$\delta=\dfrac{\,d^2-r_1^2-r_2^2\,}{2\,r_1 r_2}$

Use

Certifies when two coaxal configurations are inversively equivalent.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Inversive Distance
For two non-intersecting circles the inversive distance is an invariant of the inversive group, computable from the centers and radii. Because it survives every inversion, it certifies when two coaxal configurations are genuinely equivalent and supplies a metric on the space of disjoint circles.

The Circle of Apollonius

Ratio locus

Points whose distances to two fixed points keep a fixed ratio $\mu$.

Limiting case

$\mu=1$ gives the perpendicular bisector; otherwise a circle.

Apollonius

$|P-A'| = \mu\,|P-A|$

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95The Circle of Apollonius
Fixing two points and a ratio of distances traces the Apollonius locus. When the ratio equals one the locus is the perpendicular bisector; for every other positive ratio it is a circle whose center and radius follow directly from the two points and the ratio.

Spheres and Stereographic Projection

Sphere inversion

The same product rule $OP\cdot OP'=r^2$ in three dimensions.

Projection

Invert a tangent plane from the north pole onto the sphere.

Properties

Conformal; lines and circles map to circles on the sphere.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95Spheres and Stereographic Projection
The same product rule defines inversion in a sphere. Stereographic projection is inversion of a tangent plane from the north pole onto the sphere: it is conformal, sends circles and lines to circles, and explains geometrically why the inverse plane needs one added point at infinity.
Introduction to GeometryChapter 06

The Elliptic Plane

Antipodal model

Identify antipodal points on a sphere; lines become great circles.

Gnomonic

Sends great circles to straight lines; angles and lengths distort.

Girard's area

$\text{area}= A + B + C - \pi$

Angle excess

The triangle's area is its excess over $\pi$, the identity that survives identification.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry — printed pp. 77-95The Elliptic Plane
Identifying antipodal points on a sphere yields the elliptic plane, whose lines are great circles. Gnomonic projection straightens those great circles into lines. Girard's formula gives the area of a spherical triangle as its angle excess, the area identity that survives antipodal identification.
