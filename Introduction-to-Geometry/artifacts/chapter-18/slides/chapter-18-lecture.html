Upper and Lower Indices

Contravariant $u^i$

Coefficients that build the vector: $u = u^i\,\mathbf r_i$.

Covariant $u_i$

Projections onto the basis: $u_i = u\cdot\mathbf r_i$.

Caution

The upper index is an address, never an exponent.

Two languages, one vector

$u = u^i\,\mathbf r_i, \qquad u_i = \mathbf r_i\cdot u$

Printed pp. 328-341Upper and Lower Indices
Open by separating the two component languages. Contravariant components are coefficients that build a vector along the basis, so they vary inversely with basis length. Covariant components are projections onto the basis. The placement of the index records which transformation law applies, and this single discipline prevents almost every mistake students make in oblique frames.

The Einstein Summation Convention

The rule

An index repeated once up and once down is summed.

Free indices

Unrepeated indices must match on both sides.

Implied sum

$u^i\,\mathbf r_i \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} u^i\,\mathbf r_i$

Contraction

$u^i v_i \;=\; u^1v_1 + u^2v_2 + u^3v_3$

Why invariant

The up-down pair survives any coordinate change.

Printed pp. 328-341The Einstein Summation Convention
Introduce the convention that a repeated index appearing once up and once down is silently summed over its range. Emphasise that the surviving free indices on both sides of an equation must match exactly. This is not mere shorthand; the up-down pairing encodes a genuine contraction that is invariant under change of coordinates.

Dual Bases

Defining identity

$\mathbf r^i\cdot\mathbf r_j = \delta^i_{\,j}$.

Cross-product form

$\mathbf r^1 = \dfrac{\mathbf r_2\times\mathbf r_3}{J}$, cyclically.

Role

The dual frame reads off covariant components.

Printed pp. 328-341Dual Bases
Define the dual basis by the Kronecker condition, so each reciprocal vector is orthogonal to two primal vectors and scaled against the third. In three dimensions the cross-product formula divided by the Jacobian builds it explicitly. The notebook verifies the identity to machine precision, confirming the dual basis is the natural measuring frame for covariant components.

The Metric Tensor

Definition

$g_{ij} = \mathbf r_i\cdot\mathbf r_j$, symmetric.

Lowering

$u_i = g_{ij}\,u^j$ turns contravariant into covariant.

Reading

Diagonal: squared lengths. Off-diagonal: obliqueness.

Printed pp. 328-341The Metric Tensor
The fundamental tensor collects every inner product of basis vectors into a symmetric matrix. It is the device that lowers an index, turning contravariant components into covariant ones. Point out that the diagonal entries are squared basis lengths and off-diagonal entries measure obliqueness, so the metric is where all the geometry of the coordinate frame actually lives.

The Line Element

Master formula

$ds^2 = g_{ij}\,dx^i\,dx^j$

Descended quantities

Arc length, angle, and area all follow from $ds^2$.

Orthogonal case

If $g_{ij}=0$ for $i\neq j$, cross terms vanish.

Printed pp. 328-341The Line Element
Show the line element as the metric contracted twice with coordinate differentials. On a small coordinate parallelogram each side is a basis vector times a differential, and the squared diagonal expands into the sum of metric components. This is the master formula of the chapter; arc length, angle, and area all descend from it.

Raising and Lowering Indices

Lower with $g_{ij}$

$u_i = g_{ij}\,u^j$.

Raise with $g^{ij}$

$u^i = g^{ij}\,u_j$, the inverse metric.

Round trip

Raising then lowering returns the original.

Inverse identity

$g^{ik}\,g_{kj} = \delta^i_{\,j}$

Printed pp. 328-341Raising and Lowering Indices
Introduce the inverse metric as the contravariant tensor that raises an index, undoing what the metric lowered. The metric and its inverse contract to the Kronecker delta, the identity of tensor algebra. Stress that raising then lowering returns the original component exactly, which the notebook confirms through its reconstruction check at machine precision.

What Makes an Object a Tensor

Transformation law

Components change by Jacobian factors, one per index.

Rank

The number of indices is the tensor's rank.

Payoff

A tensor equation holds in every coordinate system.

Mixed rank-two tensor

$\bar T^{i}_{\;j} = \dfrac{\partial \bar x^i}{\partial x^k}\,\dfrac{\partial x^l}{\partial \bar x^j}\,T^{k}_{\;l}$

Kronecker delta

$\delta^i_{\,j}$ is the same tensor in every chart.

Printed pp. 328-341What Makes an Object a Tensor
A tensor is defined by how its components transform under a coordinate change: each upper index brings a Jacobian factor and each lower index its inverse. Rank counts the indices. The point is that tensor equations hold in every coordinate system at once, which is precisely why the notation deserves the central role it plays here.

Reciprocal Lattices

Duality made physical

Reciprocal basis $B = A^{-\mathsf T}$, so $A^{\mathsf T}B = I$.

Planes and normals

Reciprocal points are normals to lattice plane families.

Cubic case

FCC and BCC are reciprocal up to scale.

Printed pp. 328-341Reciprocal Lattices
Make duality physical with the reciprocal lattice. The reciprocal basis is the inverse transpose of the direct basis, and its points are normals to families of equally spaced lattice planes. Note the inverse relation between direct spacing and reciprocal length, and mention that face-centred and body-centred cubic lattices are reciprocal up to scale.

General Coordinates and the Jacobian

Varying basis

$\mathbf r_i = \partial\mathbf r/\partial x^i$ changes point to point.

Cylindrical metric

$g_{ij} = \mathrm{diag}(1,\,r^2,\,1)$.

Jacobian

$J = r$ is the local volume scale.

Printed pp. 328-341General Coordinates and the Jacobian
In curvilinear coordinates the basis vectors vary from point to point, being tangent to the coordinate curves. Cylindrical coordinates give the clean example: the metric is the diagonal one, r-squared, one, and the Jacobian equals r. The Jacobian is the local volume scale of the coordinate map, which the chapter will need for integration.

Christoffel Symbols and Covariant Derivatives

The problem

$\partial_j u^i$ alone is not a tensor; the basis turns.

Christoffel symbols

$\Gamma^k_{\;ij}$ record how $\mathbf r_i$ varies.

The fix

Add the correction to get a tensorial derivative.

Covariant derivative

$\nabla_j u^i = \partial_j u^i + \Gamma^i_{\;jk}\,u^k$

From the metric

$\Gamma^k_{\;ij} = \tfrac12\,g^{kl}\!\left(\partial_i g_{jl}+\partial_j g_{il}-\partial_l g_{ij}\right)$

Printed pp. 328-341Christoffel Symbols and Covariant Derivatives
Because the basis changes, an ordinary derivative of a vector field is not itself a tensor. The Christoffel symbols record how the basis turns, and adding their correction yields the covariant derivative, which is tensorial. This is the bridge from static index algebra to the differential geometry of surfaces that the following chapters develop.

The Alternating Symbol and Recap

Determinant as a contraction

$\det M = \varepsilon^{ijk}\,M_{1i}\,M_{2j}\,M_{3k}$

The symbol

$+1$ even, $-1$ odd, $0$ on a repeated index.

Survival kit

  • Dual basis: $\mathbf r^i\cdot\mathbf r_j=\delta^i_{\,j}$.
  • Metric $g_{ij}$ lowers, inverse $g^{ij}$ raises.
  • Line element $ds^2=g_{ij}\,dx^i\,dx^j$.
  • Tensors obey the Jacobian transformation law.
  • Covariant derivative corrects the turning basis.
Printed pp. 328-341The Alternating Symbol and Recap
Close with the alternating symbol, which writes determinants and oriented volume as a contraction: plus one for even permutations, minus one for odd, zero on repeats. Recap the survival kit: dual bases, the metric, the line element, raising and lowering, the transformation law, and covariant differentiation, all consistent index hygiene.
