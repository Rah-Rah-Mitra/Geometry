What the Geometry Keeps

Invariant

Incidence, parallel pencils, ratio along a line, area ratios.

Not invariant

Distance, angle, shape, eccentricity.

Affine test

$\text{ratio } \dfrac{AP}{PB} \text{ survives } x\mapsto Ax+b$

Rule of thumb

If it mentions a length or angle, it is not affine.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228What the Geometry Keeps
The affine dictionary separates what survives an arbitrary affinity from what does not. Incidence, parallelism, division ratios, and signed area ratios persist; distances, angles, and circularity vanish. Knowing which column a statement lives in tells you whether it is genuinely an affine theorem worth proving here.

The Parallel Axiom

Axiom

Through a point off a line, exactly one parallel exists.

Consequence

Parallelism is an equivalence relation; lines sort into pencils.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228The Parallel Axiom
Through a point off a line there is exactly one parallel, so parallelism becomes an equivalence relation that sorts every line into a pencil sharing one direction. This single axiom replaces the Euclidean metric scaffolding and lets us reason about whole families of lines at once rather than individual segments.

The Desargues Axiom

Central case

Parallel-sided triangles whose connectors meet at a center $O$.

Translation case

Connectors form a parallel pencil instead.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228The Desargues Axiom
The affine Desargues axiom controls triangles whose corresponding sides are parallel. Either the three connecting lines meet at one center, giving a central dilatation, or they form a parallel pencil, giving a translation. This dichotomy is the engine that makes dilatations well defined throughout the chapter.

Parallel Projection

Setup

Project line $\ell$ onto line $m$ by parallel rays.

Invariant

$\dfrac{AP}{PB} = \dfrac{A'P'}{P'B'}$

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Parallel Projection
Project one line onto another by a family of parallel lines. Lengths and angles distort, yet the ratio in which a point divides a segment is carried across unchanged. This invariance of division ratio is the cornerstone affine fact, and every later result on midpoints and centroids rests on it.

Dilatations and Translations

Central dilatation

$x \mapsto O + k(x-O)$, one fixed point.

Translation

$x \mapsto x + t$, no fixed point.

Half-turn

$x \mapsto A + B - x \;\;(k=-1)$

Common law

Every dilatation maps each line to a parallel line.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Dilatations and Translations
A dilatation sends every line to a parallel line. When the connecting lines share a finite center it is a central dilatation scaling by a factor k; when they are parallel it is a translation with no fixed point. Half-turns are the special dilatations of factor minus one that interchange two points.

Vectors as Translations

Definition

An ordered pair $(A,B)$ names the translation $A\to B$.

Group

Composition adds vectors and is commutative.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Vectors as Translations
An ordered pair of points names the translation that carries the first to the second, and composing translations adds these vectors. Because translation composition is commutative, the translations form an Abelian group. Vector addition, midpoints, and parallelograms thus emerge from affine structure alone, with no appeal to length or angle.

Affinities

Definition

Invertible map $x \mapsto Ax+b$ with $\det A \ne 0$.

Preserves

Collinearity, parallelism, ratios along lines.

Fundamental theorem

$\text{collineation} + \text{parallelism} \Rightarrow \text{affine}$

Group

Affinities are the symmetry group of affine geometry.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Affinities
An affinity is any invertible map x maps to Ax plus b with A nonsingular. It preserves collinearity, parallelism, and division ratios along every line, so it is exactly the symmetry group of affine geometry. The fundamental theorem states the converse: any collineation preserving parallelism is such an affine map.

Equiaffinities and Area

Area law

An affinity scales every signed area by $\det A$.

Equiaffinity

$\det A = 1$: area is the invariant.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Equiaffinities and Area
An affinity multiplies every signed area by the determinant of A. When the determinant equals one the map is an equiaffinity, and area becomes its natural invariant even though the figure is sheared or stretched. The shear family illustrates this: the triangle deforms continuously while its signed area stays exactly fixed.

Dividing a Segment

Signed ratio

$P$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $\lambda = \dfrac{AP}{PB}$.

Special cases

$\lambda=1$ midpoint; $\lambda=2$ point of trisection.

Section formula

$P = \dfrac{B\lambda + A}{1+\lambda}$

Why affine

The ratio survives every affinity and parallel projection.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Dividing a Segment
A point P on segment AB is fixed by the ratio AP to PB, written as a signed value. Affinities and parallel projections preserve this ratio, so the midpoint, the points of trisection, and any internal division are affine notions. This signed ratio is the algebraic quantity that all subsequent theorems manipulate.

Ceva's Theorem

Concurrence

$\dfrac{BX}{XC}\cdot\dfrac{CY}{YA}\cdot\dfrac{AZ}{ZB}=1$

Affine

Only ratios along lines appear, so it lives here.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Ceva's Theorem
For cevians from the vertices of a triangle through a common point, the three division ratios on the sides multiply to plus one. Concurrence is an affine condition because it involves only ratios along lines, so Ceva's theorem belongs entirely to affine geometry and needs no measurement of length or angle.

Menelaus and the Centroid

Menelaus (collinearity)

$\dfrac{BX}{XC}\cdot\dfrac{CY}{YA}\cdot\dfrac{AZ}{ZB}=-1$

Duality

$+1$ concurrent cevians; $-1$ collinear points.

Centroid

$G = \tfrac{1}{3}(A+B+C)$

Medians

$G$ divides each median in the ratio $2:1$.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Menelaus and the Centroid
Menelaus' theorem gives the dual: collinear points on the three sides yield a ratio product of minus one. Setting all cevian ratios to one recovers the medians, whose intersection is the centroid dividing each median two to one. The medial triangle and centroid are pure affine consequences of these ratio laws.

Barycentric Coordinates

Masses

Weights $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ at the vertices fix a balance point.

Areal meaning

Normalized weights are signed area shares.

Point

$P = \dfrac{\alpha A + \beta B + \gamma C}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}$

Power

Lines, cevians, transversals become determinant tests.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Barycentric Coordinates
Masses placed at the three vertices determine a balance point, and after normalization the coordinates are the signed area shares of the opposite subtriangles. Because they are origin independent weighted averages, barycentric coordinates convert lines, cevians, and transversals into clean determinant conditions encompassing both Ceva and Menelaus at once.

Conics and the Line at Infinity

Ellipse

Misses the line at infinity.

Parabola

Touches it once (tangent).

Hyperbola

Crosses it twice.

Coxeter, printed pp. 191-228Conics and the Line at Infinity
Adjoining the line at infinity lets us classify conics affinely by how they meet it. An ellipse misses the line, a parabola touches it once, and a hyperbola crosses it twice. This single incidence test distinguishes the three nondegenerate conics without any reference to foci, eccentricity, or distance.
