The Regular n-gon $\{n\}$

Definition

Equal sides and equal angles, Schlafli symbol $\{n\}$.

As an orbit

One rotation by $2\pi/n$, applied $n$ times.

Vertices

$v_k = R\big(\cos\tfrac{2\pi k}{n},\ \sin\tfrac{2\pi k}{n}\big)$

Examples

$\{3\}$ triangle, $\{4\}$ square, $\{6\}$ hexagon.

Printed pp. 26-38The Regular n-gon
A regular n-gon has equal sides and equal angles, written with the Schlafli symbol brace n. Its vertices are the n-th roots of unity scaled to the circumcircle, so the whole figure is exactly one basic rotation applied repeatedly until the orbit closes back on itself and returns.

Interior and Exterior Angles

Exterior angle

Always sums to one full turn around the figure.

Interior angle

The supplement of the exterior angle.

Angle sum

$\text{ext}=\dfrac{2\pi}{n},\quad \text{int}=\dfrac{(n-2)\pi}{n}$

Total interior

$(n-2)\,\pi$ for any $n$-gon.

Printed pp. 26-38Interior and Exterior Angles
The exterior angles of any convex polygon sum to one full turn, so each exterior angle of a regular n-gon is two pi over n. The interior angle is its supplement, and the interior angles together total n minus two straight angles, exactly the classical result here.

Circumradius and Apothem

Relations ($R=1$)

$r=\cos\tfrac{\pi}{n},\quad \tfrac{s}{2}=\sin\tfrac{\pi}{n}$

Area

$A=\tfrac{1}{2}\,n\,r\,s = \tfrac{n}{2}\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{n}$.

Printed pp. 26-38Circumradius and Apothem
Drop a perpendicular from the center to a side and you split the central isosceles triangle. The circumradius reaches a vertex while the apothem reaches a side midpoint, related by cosine of pi over n. These two lengths together control side length, perimeter, and the area formula.

Vertices as Roots of Unity

Algebraic view

Vertices are powers of a primitive root $\zeta$.

Rotation

Multiplication by $\zeta$ turns vertex to vertex.

n-th roots of unity

$\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n},\qquad v_k = \zeta^{\,k}$

Bridge

Polygon geometry becomes cyclotomic algebra.

Printed pp. 26-38Roots of Unity
Placing vertices at powers of a primitive n-th root of unity makes the regular polygon an algebraic object. The basic rotation is multiplication by zeta, and every vertex is a power of zeta, which connects polygon geometry directly to cyclotomic field theory and the constructibility question studied later.

Star Polygons $\{n/d\}$

Construction

join $v_k \to v_{k+d}$, with $0 < d < \tfrac{n}{2}$

Single orbit

When $\gcd(n,d)=1$ all vertices are visited.

Printed pp. 26-38Star Polygons
Keep the same vertices but join each to the one d steps away. When n and d are coprime a single orbit visits all vertices, giving a star polygon brace n over d. The pentagram brace five over two is the smallest genuine example, drawn with one continuous stroke here.

Density, gcd, and Components

Coprime

$\gcd(n,d)=1$: one connected star.

Shared factor

Splits into $\gcd(n,d)$ compound polygons.

Vertex angle

$\alpha = \Big(1 - \dfrac{2d}{n}\Big)\pi$

Example

$\{6/2\}$ is two triangles; $\{5/2\}$ is one star.

Printed pp. 26-38Density and gcd
The greatest common divisor of n and d decides everything: coprime gives one connected star, while a shared factor splits the drawing into gcd separate compound polygons. The density d counts how many times a central ray crosses edges, which fixes the vertex angle formula shown here.

The Symmetry Group $D_n$

Rotations

$n$ rotations form the cyclic group $C_n$.

Reflections

Adding one mirror gives $n$ reflections.

Presentation

$D_n=\langle r,s \mid r^n=s^2=e,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle$

Order

$|D_n| = 2n$ symmetries in total.

Printed pp. 26-38The Dihedral Group
The full symmetry group of a regular n-gon is the dihedral group D n, containing n rotations and n reflections for two n elements. Rotations alone form the cyclic group C n, and one reflection added with the relation s r s equals r inverse generates the entire group.

Two Reflections Make a Rotation

Composition

$S_{m_2}\, S_{m_1} = R(2\theta)$

Order matters

Swapping the mirrors gives $R(-2\theta)$.

Printed pp. 26-38Two Reflections
Reflect across one mirror, then a second mirror through the same point, and the composite is a rotation by twice the angle between the mirrors. Reversing the order inverts the rotation, which is the first visibly non-commutative product inside the dihedral group of the regular polygon under study.

Constructibility: Gauss-Wantzel

Fermat primes

$3, 5, 17, 257, 65537$ (known list).

Constructible

$\{3\},\{4\},\{5\},\{17\}$ all buildable.

Theorem

$n = 2^{k}\,p_1 p_2 \cdots p_m$, distinct Fermat $p_i$

Not constructible

$\{7\}, \{9\}, \{11\}, \{13\}$ fail the test.

Printed pp. 26-38Constructibility
Gauss and Wantzel settled which regular n-gons Euclid's tools can build: exactly those where the odd part of n is a product of distinct Fermat primes. So the seventeen-gon is constructible while the seven and nine-gon are not, a purely arithmetic verdict about a geometric drawing.

Why Angle Trisection Fails

Triple-angle

Trisecting $60^\circ$ needs $2\cos 20^\circ$.

Degree 3

Its minimal polynomial is an irreducible cubic.

Obstruction

$8x^3 - 6x - 1 = 0,\quad x=\cos 20^\circ$

Quadratic tower

Compass reaches only degrees $2^k$, never $3$.

Printed pp. 26-38Trisection Obstruction
Trisecting a sixty degree angle would build two cosine twenty degrees, whose minimal polynomial has degree three. Straightedge and compass only reach numbers through repeated quadratic steps, powers of two, so a degree three irreducible obstruction makes the construction impossible in the general case for arbitrary angles.

Regular Tessellations of the Plane

Angle condition

$\dfrac{(n-2)\pi}{n}\ \text{divides}\ 2\pi$

Only three

$\{3,6\}$ triangles, $\{4,4\}$ squares, $\{6,3\}$ hexagons.

Printed pp. 26-38Regular Tessellations
Only three regular polygons tile the plane edge to edge: triangles, squares, and hexagons, written brace three six, brace four four, and brace six three. The condition is that the interior angle divides a full turn, which holds solely for these three polygon orders and no others.

Recap and Takeaways

Geometry meets algebra

Vertices are roots of unity; rotation is $\times\zeta$.

Symmetry

$D_n$ with $2n$ elements organizes all motions.

Construction

Buildable iff odd part is distinct Fermat primes.

Tilings

Only $\{3,6\},\{4,4\},\{6,3\}$ fill the plane.

Printed pp. 26-38Recap and Takeaways
Regular polygons are circle orbits filtered by arithmetic for construction and organized by dihedral symmetry for motion. Roots of unity unify both views, star polygons reuse the same vertices with a longer step, and only three regular tilings survive the angle-sum constraint of the Euclidean plane.
