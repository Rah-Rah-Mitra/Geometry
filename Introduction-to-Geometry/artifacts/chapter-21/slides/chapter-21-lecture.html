The Euler Characteristic

Count the cells

Vertices, edges, faces of any polyhedral subdivision.

Sphere value

$\chi = 2$ for the sphere and every convex polyhedron.

Definition

$\chi = V - E + F$

Topological invariant

Independent of how the surface is meshed.

Printed pp. 379-395The Euler Characteristic
For any polyhedral subdivision of a surface the alternating count V minus E plus F is a topological invariant: refining or distorting the mesh never changes it. The sphere gives two, so every convex polyhedron satisfies Euler's celebrated relation. This single number organizes the entire chapter.

Invariance Under Subdivision

Edge split

$\Delta V=+1,\ \Delta E=+1 \Rightarrow \Delta\chi = 0$

Face split

$\Delta E=+1,\ \Delta F=+1 \Rightarrow \Delta\chi = 0$

Consequence

Any two subdivisions of one surface agree.

Printed pp. 379-395Invariance Under Subdivision
Why is the Euler characteristic invariant? Each elementary move that refines a subdivision leaves V minus E plus F unchanged. Splitting a face adds one edge and one face; splitting an edge adds one vertex and one edge. Because the moves cancel, any two subdivisions of the same surface agree.

The Fundamental Polygon

Edges in pairs

A closed surface is a polygon with boundary edges identified two by two.

The word

Reading the arrows around the boundary gives a word in $a,b,\dots$ and inverses.

Identification scheme

$\text{torus: } a\,b\,a^{-1}b^{-1}$

Why it helps

A flat diagram replaces a hard 3D picture, ready to compute on.

Printed pp. 379-395The Fundamental Polygon
Every closed surface is a polygon whose boundary edges are glued in pairs, recorded by a word in symbols and inverses. Reading the word tells us the gluing arrows. This combinatorial scheme replaces hard three-dimensional pictures with a flat diagram that we can compute on directly.

The Torus: $a\,b\,a^{-1}b^{-1}$

Cell count

$V=1,\ E=2,\ F=1$

Characteristic

$\chi = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$

All same-direction

No reversed arrows: the torus is orientable.

Printed pp. 379-395The Torus
Gluing the opposite sides of a square with matching arrows yields a torus, encoded by the word a b a inverse b inverse. All four corners become one vertex, the two edge pairs give two edges, and the single square is one face, so V minus E plus F equals zero.

Projective Plane and Klein Bottle

Projective plane

$a\,a, \qquad \chi = 1$

Antipodal gluing

Opposite boundary points of a disk identified.

Klein bottle

$a\,b\,a\,b, \qquad \chi = 0$

Reversed arrows

A repeated symbol $aa$ signals non-orientability.

Printed pp. 379-395Projective Plane and Klein Bottle
Changing the gluing word changes the surface. The word a a builds the projective plane with characteristic one; a b a b builds the Klein bottle with characteristic zero. Both contain reversed-arrow identifications, the signature of non-orientability that distinguishes them from the torus.

The Mobius Band

Half twist

A strip glued end to end with a single twist.

One-sided

A single side and a single boundary curve.

The cross-cap

Every non-orientable surface hides a Mobius band.

Printed pp. 379-395The Mobius Band
The Mobius band is the elementary non-orientable surface: a strip glued with a half twist. It has only one side and one boundary curve, and a normal arrow carried once around returns reversed. Every non-orientable closed surface contains a Mobius band as a cross-cap.

Orientability as a Transport Test

Carry a frame

Transport a small local arrow once around a loop.

Torus loop

$\text{frame}\cdot\text{frame} = +1$

Mobius loop

The returned arrow is mirror reversed.

Mobius cross-strip

$\text{frame}\cdot\text{frame} = -1$

Printed pp. 379-395Orientability as a Transport Test
Orientability is a transport test. Carry a small local frame once around a loop; on an orientable surface like the torus it returns compatible, dot product plus one, while on a Mobius-type strip it returns mirror reversed, dot product minus one. That sign cleanly separates the two surface classes.

Classification of Closed Surfaces

Orientable

$\text{sphere} + g\ \text{handles},\quad \chi = 2 - 2g$

Complete invariants

Genus together with orientability determines the surface.

Non-orientable

$\#^{k}\,\mathbb{RP}^2,\quad \chi = 2 - k$

Connected sums

A sphere carrying $k$ cross-caps.

Printed pp. 379-395Classification of Closed Surfaces
The classification theorem is the chapter's summit. Every closed orientable surface is a sphere with g handles and has characteristic two minus twice g. Every closed non-orientable surface is a connected sum of projective planes, a sphere carrying cross-caps. Genus and orientability are a complete pair of invariants.

Building Surfaces: Handles and Cross-Caps

Handle move

$\chi \mapsto \chi - 2$

Cross-cap move

$\chi \mapsto \chi - 1$

Generators

Both moves build every surface from the sphere.

Printed pp. 379-395Handles and Cross-Caps
Adding a handle to a surface lowers the characteristic by two; adding a cross-cap lowers it by one. These two moves generate every closed surface from the sphere. The genus counts handles, so the orientable family marches two two zero negative-two as we attach more handles.

The Gauss-Bonnet Bridge

Geometry meets topology

Total curvature is fixed by the characteristic alone.

Sphere check

$\chi=2$ gives total curvature $4\pi$.

Gauss-Bonnet

$\displaystyle \int_S K\, dA = 2\pi\,\chi(S)$

Curvature cannot escape

Bend the surface; the integral stays pinned.

Printed pp. 379-395The Gauss-Bonnet Bridge
Geometry meets topology in Gauss-Bonnet: the total Gaussian curvature integrated over a closed surface equals two pi times its Euler characteristic. Bend and stretch the surface however you like; the curvature redistributes but its integral is pinned by the topology. Curvature cannot escape the characteristic.
