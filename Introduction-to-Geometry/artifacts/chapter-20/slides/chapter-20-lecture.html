Two Faces of One Curve

Shortest, locally

Among nearby curves joining two points, a geodesic is stationary in length.

Caution

Local, not global. A long great-circle arc is a geodesic yet not the shortest route.

Arc length functional

$$L[\gamma]=\int_a^b \sqrt{g_{ij}\,\dot u^i \dot u^j}\;dt$$

Straightest

Acceleration has no component tangent to the surface.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Straightest and Shortest
We open with the two complementary definitions a geodesic satisfies at once. Pull a thread tight on a globe and it lies along the straightest available track while also minimizing length locally. Holding both pictures together prevents the common error of treating geodesics as merely globally shortest paths, which they are not in general.

Geodesic Curvature $\kappa_g$

Decomposition

$$\kappa^2=\kappa_n^2+\kappa_g^2$$

Intrinsic

$\kappa_g$ depends only on the first fundamental form, not the embedding.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Geodesic Curvature
Here we decompose the curvature vector of a surface curve into a normal part absorbed by the surface and a tangential part, the geodesic curvature kappa-g. This single scalar measures sideways bending felt within the surface. Emphasize that kappa-g is intrinsic: it depends only on the first fundamental form, not on how the surface sits in space.
Introduction to GeometryChapter 20

The Defining Property

Definition

$$\gamma \text{ is a geodesic} \iff \kappa_g \equiv 0$$

Principal normal

The curve's principal normal aligns with the surface normal; all bending is forced by the surface.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Zero Geodesic Curvature
This is the defining property of a geodesic: its geodesic curvature vanishes identically, so the principal normal of the curve coincides with the surface normal. All bending is forced by the surface itself. We contrast a geodesic with a latitude circle, whose curvature has a stubborn tangential component, so it bends within the surface.

The Geodesic Equations

Euler-Lagrange

Stationary arc length gives the equations of motion of a free particle on the surface.

Christoffel symbols

$\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are built from the metric $g_{ij}$ and its first derivatives.

Geodesic ODE

$$\ddot u^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot u^i \dot u^j = 0$$

Existence

A point and a direction fix a unique geodesic: a second-order initial-value problem.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378The Geodesic Equations
We derive the geodesic equations as the Euler-Lagrange conditions for stationary arc length. The Christoffel symbols of the first fundamental form encode how coordinate directions twist, and the equations say the acceleration has no tangential component. Stress that this is a second-order ODE: a starting point and direction determine a unique geodesic.

Geodesic Polar Coordinates

Radial metric template

$$ds^2 = dr^2 + f(r)^2\,d\theta^2$$

Conserved momentum

The equations reduce to $\big(f(r)^2\,\dot\theta\big)'=0$ along every geodesic.

Curvature from $f$

$$K=-\dfrac{f''(r)}{f(r)}$$

One function

$f=\sin r,\;r,\;\sinh r$ encodes $K=1,0,-1$. Circle circumference is $2\pi f(r)$.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Geodesic Polar Form
We specialize the metric to geodesic polar coordinates, ds-squared equals dr-squared plus f of r squared d-theta-squared. The radial function f carries all the geometry: angular momentum f-squared theta-prime is conserved along any geodesic, and curvature is minus f-double-prime over f. This compact template recurs through the rest of the chapter.

Great Circles: Geodesics of the Sphere

Sphere metric

$$ds^2 = du^2 + \sin^2\!u\,\,dv^2$$

Result

Every geodesic is the intersection of the sphere with a plane through its center: a great circle.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Great Circles on the Sphere
The flagship example: every geodesic of a sphere is a great circle, the intersection with a plane through the center. We contrast the short great-circle arc joining two cities with a latitude circle between the same points, which looks direct on a flat map but is genuinely longer. This is why flight paths bow poleward.

Clairaut's Theorem

Invariant on a surface of revolution

$$r\,\sin\psi = \text{const}$$

Meaning

$r$ is distance to the axis; $\psi$ is the angle the geodesic makes with the meridian.

Angular momentum

It is the conserved quantity $f(r)^2\dot\theta$ in disguise.

Turning barrier

Since $\sin\psi\le1$, a geodesic cannot pass below $r=\text{const}$; it turns there.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Clairaut's Theorem
On any surface of revolution, the product r times sine psi stays constant along a geodesic, where r is distance from the axis and psi the angle the geodesic makes with the meridian. We read this as conservation of angular momentum. As a geodesic climbs toward a thinner part of the surface, psi must grow to compensate.

Clairaut in Action

Reading the picture

$$r\,\sin\psi=c \;\Rightarrow\; \sin\psi=\dfrac{c}{r}$$

Wide vs narrow

At the waist $\psi$ is small; near the neck $\psi$ approaches a right angle.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Clairaut on a Surface of Revolution
Here we visualize Clairaut's relation directly. A geodesic winds around a vase-shaped surface; at the wide waist psi is small, and near the narrow neck where r shrinks, sine psi swells toward one until the geodesic grazes a barrier circle and turns back. The constant r sine psi sets that turning radius precisely.

Cylinder and Cone: Unroll to a Line

Cylinder

Geodesics are helices (plus the straight rulings and circles).

Cone

Unroll to a sector; geodesics are straight segments that may self-cross.

Why it works

Developable surfaces bend without stretching, so $\kappa_g$ is preserved.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Cylinder and Cone
Developable surfaces make geodesics easy: unroll them flat and geodesics become ordinary straight lines. On a cylinder the unrolled lines wind back into helices; on a cone they become straight segments that can self-intersect after unwrapping. We use this to reinforce that geodesics are intrinsic, surviving any bending that does not stretch the surface.

Geodesic Triangles and Total Curvature

Angle excess

$$\iint_\triangle K\,dS = A+B+C-\pi$$

Sign of $K$

Excess on the sphere ($K>0$); defect $\pi-A-B-C$ in the hyperbolic plane ($K<0$).

Gauss-Bonnet

$$\iint_S K\,dS = 2\pi\chi,\quad \chi=V-E+F$$

Budget

Edge contributions cancel; total curvature is a topological invariant.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Triangles and Total Curvature
We connect geodesics to curvature in the large. The integral of Gaussian curvature over a geodesic triangle equals its angle sum minus pi: excess on the sphere, defect in the hyperbolic plane. Summing such budgets over a closed surface cancels interior edges and yields the Gauss-Bonnet relation, total curvature equals two pi times the Euler characteristic.

Recap & Constant Curvature

One object, four views

$\kappa_g=0$, Euler-Lagrange, great circles, Clairaut's $r\sin\psi$ all describe the same curve.

Unified template

$f=\sin r,\,r,\,\sinh r$ gives spherical, flat, hyperbolic geometry from one metric.

Pseudosphere warning

$$K=-1,\quad \text{but the cusp edge cuts the model off}$$

Looking ahead

Constant-curvature surfaces seed the non-Euclidean geometries of later chapters.

Coxeter, Introduction to Geometry · pp. 366-378Recap and Constant Curvature
We close by tying the thread together: zero geodesic curvature, the Euler-Lagrange equations, great circles, and Clairaut's invariant all describe one object. The radial template with f equal to sin r, r, or sinh r packages spherical, flat, and hyperbolic geometry uniformly, and the pseudosphere reminds us a constant-negative-curvature surface need not hold the whole hyperbolic plane.
