Hyperbolic Geometry

Change one postulate; rebuild the plane

The single change

Replace the unique Euclidean parallel with the hyperbolic axiom.

What survives

All of absolute geometry: incidence, order, congruence.

What follows

Limiting parallels, angular defect, finite area, models.

Printed pp. 287-306Hyperbolic Geometry
This chapter spans printed pages 287 through 306 and replaces a single Euclidean assertion. Keeping all of absolute geometry, we swap the unique-parallel postulate for one that allows many non-meeting lines through a point, and follow the consequences into models, angular defect, and negative curvature.

The Hyperbolic Axiom

Euclid

Through a point not on a line, exactly one parallel.

Hyperbolic

Through that point pass infinitely many non-intersecting lines.

The axiom

$\exists\,\infty\text{-many } \ell' \ni P : \ \ell' \cap \ell = \varnothing$

Relative consistency

No contradiction follows; a model will prove it.

Printed pp. 287-306The Hyperbolic Axiom
Euclid guarantees exactly one parallel through an external point; the hyperbolic axiom permits infinitely many lines that never meet a given line. Everything absolute about incidence, order, and congruence survives, so the new geometry is internally honest rather than a casual contradiction of Euclid waiting to collapse.

Two Limiting Parallels

The boundary rays

Two lines separate the crossing lines from the avoiding ones.

Asymptotic

They approach $\ell$ forever without meeting it.

Printed pp. 287-306Two Limiting Parallels
Among the infinitely many non-meeting lines, exactly two are limiting: they separate the lines that cross the reference line from those that avoid it. These boundary rays are the asymptotic parallels, approaching the line without ever touching it, and they anchor every later hyperbolic measurement of angle and distance.

The Angle of Parallelism $\Pi(d)$

Lobachevsky's function

A distance $d$ determines an angle $\Pi(d)$.

Formula

$\Pi(d) = 2\arctan\!\big(e^{-d}\big)$

Limits

$\Pi(0)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, and $\Pi(d)\to 0$ as $d\to\infty$.

Printed pp. 287-306Angle of Parallelism
Lobachevsky's function ties a distance to an angle. Drop a perpendicular of length d to a line; the limiting parallel through the foot makes the angle Pi of d with that perpendicular. As d grows the angle shrinks from a right angle toward zero, so absolute distance becomes directly measurable through angle.

Angle Sum and Defect

Below two right angles

Every triangle has $\alpha+\beta+\gamma < \pi$.

Angular defect

The shortfall $\delta = \pi-\alpha-\beta-\gamma \ge 0$.

Defect

$\delta = \pi - (\alpha+\beta+\gamma) > 0$

Printed pp. 287-306Angle Sum and Defect
Every hyperbolic triangle has angle sum strictly below pi. The shortfall, called the angular defect, is never negative and grows as triangles enlarge. This single inequality distinguishes hyperbolic triangles from Euclidean ones and prepares the remarkable identification of defect with area that the next slide develops.

Defect Equals Area

Gauss's law

Area is proportional to the angular defect.

Bounded area

Even an ideal triangle has area at most $k\pi$.

Area formula

$\text{Area} = k\,(\pi - \alpha - \beta - \gamma)$

Negative curvature

The sign mirrors the spherical excess law $\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi$.

Printed pp. 287-306Defect Equals Area
Gauss's functional argument shows triangle area is proportional to its angular defect, so in a normalized unit area equals pi minus the three angles. Area is therefore bounded: even an ideal triangle with vanishing angles encloses only finite area pi, a fact with no Euclidean analogue whatsoever.

No Similar, Non-Congruent Triangles

Angle fixes area

The three angles determine the defect, hence the area.

AAA congruence

Equal angles force congruence, not mere similarity.

Consequence

$\alpha,\beta,\gamma \ \Rightarrow\ \text{size fixed}$

Absolute length

There is a natural unit of length; free scaling is impossible.

Printed pp. 287-306No Similar Triangles
Because the three angles fix the defect and hence the area, two hyperbolic triangles with equal angles must be congruent. There are no similar but non-congruent figures: angle alone determines size. Absolute length enters geometry, and scaling a figure freely, as one does in Euclid, becomes impossible here.

The Poincaré Disk Model

Geodesics

Arcs meeting the boundary at right angles, plus diameters.

Conformal

Angles are drawn truthfully; distance stretches near the rim.

Printed pp. 287-306The Poincare Disk Model
The Poincare disk proves the axiom consistent. Points are interior points of a disk; hyperbolic lines are circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles, plus diameters. The model is conformal, so angles are drawn truthfully while distances stretch enormously near the rim, where the ideal boundary sits.

The Axiom, Made Visible

One line, one point

Fix a geodesic and an interior point off it.

Many parallels

Infinitely many arcs through the point miss the line.

Printed pp. 287-306Many Lines Through a Point
In the disk a given chord-arc and an interior point not on it show the axiom directly: infinitely many arcs through the point fail to meet the line, and two limiting arcs share an ideal boundary endpoint with it. The picture makes the hyperbolic parallel property completely visible and concrete.

The Klein / Beltrami Model

Straight chords

Geodesics are ordinary straight chords of the circle.

Not conformal

Incidence is honest; angles are distorted.

Printed pp. 287-306The Klein Model
The Klein, or Beltrami, disk trades conformality for straightness. Hyperbolic lines become ordinary straight chords of the boundary circle, so incidence is easy to read, but angles are distorted. Comparing it with the Poincare disk teaches model literacy: each representation tells the truth about one feature while bending another.

Horocycles and Equidistant Curves

Horocycle

Limit of circles whose centre recedes to an ideal point.

Equidistant curve

Fixed distance from a line; not itself a geodesic.

Printed pp. 287-306Horocycles and Equidistants
Beyond ordinary circles, hyperbolic geometry has horocycles, limits of circles whose centre recedes to an ideal point, and equidistant curves tracing a fixed distance from a line. In the upper half-plane horocycles are horizontal lines and equidistants are slanted rays, none of them geodesics except the genuine perpendicular lines.

Hyperbolic Trigonometry

Hyperbolic Pythagoras

Right triangle with legs $a,b$ and hypotenuse $c$.

Identity

$\cosh c = \cosh a \,\cosh b$

Law of cosines

$\cosh c = \cosh a\cosh b - \sinh a\sinh b\cos\gamma$

Synthesis

With $\Pi(d)$, these compute every length and angle.

Printed pp. 287-306Hyperbolic Trigonometry
Hyperbolic trigonometry replaces the circular functions with hyperbolic ones. A right triangle obeys cosh of the hypotenuse equals the product of the cosh of the legs, the negatively curved analogue of Pythagoras. These formulas, together with the angle of parallelism, let us compute lengths and angles throughout the hyperbolic plane.
