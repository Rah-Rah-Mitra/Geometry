Tangential and Normal: Splitting the Ambient Connection

Setting

Let \((\tilde M,\tilde g)\) be Riemannian and \(M\hookrightarrow \tilde M\) an immersed submanifold with induced metric \(g=\iota^*\tilde g\).

Orthogonal Splitting

At each \(p\in M\) the ambient tangent space decomposes as \(T_p\tilde M = T_pM \oplus N_pM\), and every ambient vector field along \(M\) splits into its tangential and normal parts.

Induced metric and orthogonal splittingSlide 2 / 10
Start with the geometric picture: a submanifold sits inside a larger Riemannian manifold, and at every point of the submanifold the ambient tangent space splits orthogonally into the tangent and normal directions. Stress that the induced metric is just the restriction of the ambient metric to tangent vectors. The whole chapter exploits this decomposition repeatedly, so make sure students see the picture before any formulas appear.

The Gauss Formula: Induced Connection Plus II

\( \tilde\nabla_X Y \;=\; \nabla_X Y \;+\; \mathrm{II}(X,Y) \)

Tangential Piece

\(\nabla_X Y := (\tilde\nabla_X Y)^\top\) is the Levi-Civita connection of the induced metric on \(M\).

Normal Piece

\(\mathrm{II}(X,Y) := (\tilde\nabla_X Y)^\perp\) is the second fundamental form, valued in \(NM\).

Why Both Survive

The tangential part inherits torsion-freeness and metric compatibility; the normal part is automatically tensorial in \(X\) and symmetric in \((X,Y)\).

Gauss formula on a Riemannian submanifoldSlide 3 / 10
The Gauss formula is the central organizing identity of the chapter. Walk through why the tangential part of the ambient covariant derivative deserves to be called the Levi-Civita connection of the induced metric: it is torsion free and metric compatible, so uniqueness forces the identification. Emphasize that the normal part is not a new connection but a tensor, the second fundamental form, which measures how \(Y\) leaves the submanifold as one moves in the direction \(X\).

The Second Fundamental Form II(X,Y)

Definition

II : \mathcal X(M)\times \mathcal X(M)\to \Gamma(NM)

\(\mathrm{II}(X,Y) = (\tilde\nabla_X Y)^\perp\).

Symmetry & Tensoriality

Torsion freeness of \(\tilde\nabla\) plus \([X,Y]\in TM\) yield \(\mathrm{II}(X,Y)=\mathrm{II}(Y,X)\), and II is \(C^\infty(M)\)-bilinear.

Reading

II measures how \(Y\) accelerates away from \(M\) along the \(X\)-direction.

Pointwise tensor in two tangent slots, valued in the normal bundleSlide 4 / 10
Make sure students see two facts about the second fundamental form. First, it depends only on the pointwise values of the inputs, so it is genuinely a tensor field. Second, it is symmetric, which is the price the ambient connection pays for being torsion free. The geometric reading is that II records the failure of a tangent vector field to stay parallel to the submanifold under ambient transport, encoded as a vector in the normal bundle.

The Weingarten Equation and the Shape Operator

\( \langle \tilde\nabla_X N, Y\rangle \;=\; -\,\langle \mathrm{II}(X,Y),\,N\rangle \;=\; -\,\langle s_N(X),\,Y\rangle \)

Shape Operator

s_N : TM \to TM,\quad s_N(X) = -(\tilde\nabla_X N)^\top

For each normal field \(N\), the shape operator is a self-adjoint endomorphism of \(TM\) determined by II.

Two Faces, One Object

The Weingarten equation says that II and the shape operator are equivalent data: II records the normal part of \(\tilde\nabla_X Y\); \(s_N\) records the tangential part of \(\tilde\nabla_X N\).

Self-Adjointness

Symmetry of II implies \(\langle s_N(X),Y\rangle = \langle X,s_N(Y)\rangle\); the spectral theorem then gives real eigenvalues at every point.

Tangential derivative of a normal field is the shape operatorSlide 5 / 10
Present the Weingarten equation as the dual viewpoint to the Gauss formula. Instead of differentiating tangent vector fields and reading off the normal part, we differentiate a normal field and read off the tangential part. The minus sign comes from differentiating the constraint \(\langle N,Y\rangle = 0\). The payoff is the shape operator, a self adjoint endomorphism of the tangent space whose eigenvalues will become the principal curvatures.

The Gauss Equation: Ambient Curvature Meets Intrinsic Curvature

\( \langle R(W,X)Y,Z\rangle \;=\; \langle \tilde R(W,X)Y,Z\rangle \;+\; \langle \mathrm{II}(W,Z),\mathrm{II}(X,Y)\rangle \;-\; \langle \mathrm{II}(W,Y),\mathrm{II}(X,Z)\rangle \)

Intrinsic R

The Riemann tensor of \((M,g)\), depending only on the induced metric.

Ambient R̃

The Riemann tensor of \((\tilde M,\tilde g)\), restricted to tangent inputs at points of \(M\).

II Correction

A purely extrinsic correction quadratic in the second fundamental form.

Theorema Egregium, Modernized

The intrinsic curvature of \(M\) is determined by ambient curvature plus how \(M\) bends. When \(\tilde M\) is flat, R is encoded entirely by II — Gauss's original astonishing theorem in modern language.

Decomposes ambient curvature into intrinsic plus extrinsic termsSlide 6 / 10
The Gauss equation is the headline identity of the chapter. Spell out that the left side is a purely intrinsic quantity, the curvature of the induced metric; the first term on the right is the restriction of the ambient curvature; and the remaining terms are quadratic in the second fundamental form. The slide flags the connection to Gauss's theorema egregium: in flat ambient space, intrinsic curvature is built entirely from II, which is exactly the statement that Gaussian curvature is intrinsic.

The Codazzi Equation: How II Differentiates

\( \big(\tilde R(W,X)Y\big)^{\perp} \;=\; (\nabla^\perp_W \mathrm{II})(X,Y) \;-\; (\nabla^\perp_X \mathrm{II})(W,Y) \)

Normal Connection

\nabla^\perp_X N := (\tilde\nabla_X N)^\perp

The normal connection differentiates normal fields along tangent directions.

Covariant Derivative of II

\((\nabla^\perp_W \mathrm{II})(X,Y) = \nabla^\perp_W(\mathrm{II}(X,Y)) - \mathrm{II}(\nabla_W X, Y) - \mathrm{II}(X,\nabla_W Y)\).

Reading

The normal projection of ambient curvature equals the antisymmetric part of \(\nabla^\perp \mathrm{II}\) in its tangential slots. When the ambient space has constant curvature, the right side vanishes — II is a Codazzi tensor.

Integrability constraint on the second fundamental formSlide 7 / 10
Frame Codazzi as the compatibility condition that II is not free to be any symmetric tensor field; it must satisfy a differential constraint coming from the ambient curvature. Introduce the normal connection briefly: it is just the normal projection of the ambient connection applied to normal fields. The headline consequence is that in a constant-curvature ambient space, II is a Codazzi tensor, meaning its covariant derivative is symmetric in all three lower slots.

Euclidean Hypersurfaces: Principal and Mean Curvature

Principal Curvatures

The eigenvalues \(\kappa_1,\dots,\kappa_{n-1}\) of \(s_N\) at \(p\); their eigenvectors are the principal directions.

Mean Curvature

H = \tfrac{1}{n-1}\,\mathrm{tr}\,s_N = \tfrac{1}{n-1}\sum_i \kappa_i

Gauss–Kronecker

For a hypersurface in flat ambient space the sectional curvature in a principal plane equals \(\kappa_i\kappa_j\) — Gauss's theorem in arbitrary dimension.

Hypersurface = codimension-one submanifold; II becomes a scalar formSlide 8 / 10
Specialize to codimension one. With a chosen unit normal, the second fundamental form is recorded by a single symmetric bilinear form whose self-adjoint counterpart is the shape operator. Its eigenvalues are the classical principal curvatures, and their average is the mean curvature. Emphasize that for a hypersurface in Euclidean space the Gauss equation reduces to the elegant statement that the sectional curvature in a principal plane is the product of principal curvatures — the higher-dimensional analogue of Gauss's curvature formula for surfaces.

Sectional Curvature of a Submanifold

\( K(\sigma) \;=\; \tilde K(\sigma) \;+\; \dfrac{\langle \mathrm{II}(X,X),\mathrm{II}(Y,Y)\rangle - \lVert \mathrm{II}(X,Y)\rVert^2}{\lVert X\rVert^2\lVert Y\rVert^2 - \langle X,Y\rangle^2} \)

Plane Section

\(\sigma = \mathrm{span}\{X,Y\}\subset T_pM\), with the same plane viewed in \(T_p\tilde M\) for \(\tilde K\).

Hypersurface Case

If \(\tilde M\) is flat and \(X,Y\) are principal, the formula collapses to \(K(\sigma)=\kappa_i\kappa_j\).

Sign Reading

Convex bending in the same normal direction raises \(K\); saddle bending (opposite signs of II) lowers \(K\).

Constant Curvature Models

Submanifolds of spheres, Euclidean space, and hyperbolic space inherit \(\tilde K\in\{+1,0,-1\}\); the II-correction then governs whether the induced geometry beats, matches, or undershoots the ambient model.

Same plane, two curvatures: intrinsic and ambientSlide 9 / 10
This slide makes the Gauss equation operational. The intrinsic sectional curvature of a two-plane is the ambient sectional curvature of the same plane plus a correction built from the second fundamental form. The correction is nonnegative when II is definite on the plane and negative when II is saddle-like. Hand-wave this against students' mental images of a sphere and a saddle sitting in Euclidean space, and connect the formula to the Euclidean-hypersurface case from the previous slide.
