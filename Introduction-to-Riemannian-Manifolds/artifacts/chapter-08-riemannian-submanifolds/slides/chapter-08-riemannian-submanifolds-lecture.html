We fix an isometric immersion of a manifold into a Riemannian ambient space, giving each point a tangent space and an orthogonal normal space. The induced metric makes the submanifold Riemannian, and the orthogonal splitting of ambient vectors along the submanifold is the engine that drives every formula in this chapter.

The Gauss Formula

Differentiate, then split

The ambient derivative of tangent fields need not stay tangent.

Tangential part

Recovers the induced Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$.

Normal part

Is the second fundamental form $\mathrm{II}(X,Y)$.

Gauss formula

$\tilde\nabla_X Y = \nabla_X Y + \mathrm{II}(X,Y)$

Reading

Tangential term $=\nabla_X Y$; normal term measures bending.

Printed pp. 225-262The Gauss Formula
Covariantly differentiating two tangent fields with the ambient connection yields a vector that need not stay tangent. Projecting it splits the derivative into the induced tangential connection plus a normal correction. That decomposition is the Gauss formula, and its normal part is exactly the second fundamental form we study next.

The Second Fundamental Form

Normal-valued

$\mathrm{II}:T_pM\times T_pM \to N_pM$, symmetric and bilinear.

Tensorial

$C^\infty$-bilinear, so it depends only on values at $p$.

Printed pp. 225-262The Second Fundamental Form
The second fundamental form is the symmetric, normal-valued bilinear map measuring how the submanifold curves away from its tangent plane inside the ambient space. Symmetry follows from torsion-freeness of both connections, and bilinearity over functions makes it a genuine tensor rather than a differential operator depending on extensions.

The Shape Operator (Weingarten Map)

Fix a normal

A unit normal $N$ turns $\mathrm{II}$ into a scalar form $h_N$.

Self-adjoint

$S_N$ is symmetric: $\langle S_N X, Y\rangle = \langle X, S_N Y\rangle$.

Weingarten equation

$\langle S_N X, Y\rangle = \langle \mathrm{II}(X,Y), N\rangle$

As a derivative

$S_N X = -\,\pi^\top \tilde\nabla_X N$

Printed pp. 225-262The Shape Operator
Fixing a unit normal converts the normal-valued second fundamental form into a self-adjoint endomorphism of the tangent space, the shape operator or Weingarten map. The Weingarten equation gives it as the tangential derivative of the normal field, and self-adjointness guarantees real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigendirections everywhere.

Principal Curvatures and Directions

Spectral theorem

$S_N$ has real eigenvalues $\kappa_1,\dots,\kappa_n$ with orthonormal eigenvectors.

Euler's formula

Normal curvature $=\sum \kappa_i \cos^2\theta_i$ in principal frame.

Printed pp. 225-262Principal Curvatures
Because the shape operator is self-adjoint, the spectral theorem supplies orthonormal principal directions with real principal curvatures. These eigenvalues record the extreme normal curvatures of curves through the point, and every normal curvature lies between the smallest and largest principal value, a fact Euler's formula makes precise.

Mean and Gaussian Curvature

Mean curvature

Average of principal curvatures; trace of $S_N$.

Gaussian curvature

Product of principal curvatures; determinant of $S_N$.

Mean

$H = \tfrac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr} S_N = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i \kappa_i$

Gaussian (surface)

$K = \det S_N = \kappa_1 \kappa_2$

Printed pp. 225-262Mean and Gaussian Curvature
Two symmetric functions of the principal curvatures matter most. The mean curvature is their average, the trace of the shape operator, governing minimal surfaces. The Gaussian curvature is their product, the determinant, and the chapter's main surprise is that this extrinsic-looking product is secretly intrinsic.

The Gauss Equation

Tangential curvature

Relates ambient curvature to the submanifold's own.

Quadratic correction

Difference is built from the second fundamental form.

Gauss equation

$\langle \tilde R(W,X)Y,Z\rangle = \langle R(W,X)Y,Z\rangle$

$+\,\langle \mathrm{II}(W,Z),\mathrm{II}(X,Y)\rangle - \langle \mathrm{II}(W,Y),\mathrm{II}(X,Z)\rangle$

Use

Compute intrinsic sectional curvature from extrinsic data.

Printed pp. 225-262The Gauss Equation
The Gauss equation expresses the ambient curvature tensor restricted to tangent vectors in terms of the submanifold's own curvature plus quadratic terms in the second fundamental form. It cleanly separates intrinsic curvature from the extrinsic bending, letting us compute sectional curvatures of the submanifold from ambient data and the shape operator.

The Codazzi Equation

Normal component

Captures the $N_pM$-part of ambient curvature.

Constant curvature

If $\tilde M$ has constant curvature, the right side vanishes.

Codazzi equation

$\big(\tilde R(X,Y)Z\big)^\perp = (\nabla_X \mathrm{II})(Y,Z) - (\nabla_Y \mathrm{II})(X,Z)$

Consequence

$\mathrm{II}$ becomes a Codazzi tensor in space forms.

Printed pp. 225-262The Codazzi Equation
The Codazzi equation captures the normal component of the ambient curvature, equating it with the covariant derivative of the second fundamental form taken antisymmetrically. When the ambient space has constant curvature this normal component vanishes, forcing the second fundamental form to be a Codazzi tensor, a strong integrability constraint on hypersurfaces.

Hypersurfaces in Euclidean Space

One normal

Codimension one gives a single unit normal up to sign.

Single shape operator

All extrinsic data lives in one self-adjoint $S$.

Sectional curvature (Euclidean ambient)

$\operatorname{sec}(e_i,e_j) = \kappa_i \,\kappa_j$

Reading

Intrinsic curvature of a principal plane is a product of principal curvatures.

Printed pp. 225-262Hypersurfaces
For a hypersurface there is a single unit normal up to sign, so one shape operator and one second fundamental form carry all extrinsic information. The Gauss equation then reduces to a product formula: sectional curvature in a principal two-plane is the product of the two principal curvatures spanning it.

The Theorema Egregium

Gauss's remarkable theorem

Gaussian curvature is intrinsic, an isometry invariant.

Consequence

No isometry flattens a sphere onto a plane.

Corollary of Gauss equation

$K = \kappa_1 \kappa_2 = \operatorname{sec}(T_pM)$

Printed pp. 225-262Theorema Egregium
Gauss's remarkable theorem follows as a corollary: for a surface in Euclidean space the Gaussian curvature, defined as the product of principal curvatures, equals the intrinsic sectional curvature. Bending a surface without stretching preserves curvature, so a flat plane can never be wrapped onto a sphere without distortion.
