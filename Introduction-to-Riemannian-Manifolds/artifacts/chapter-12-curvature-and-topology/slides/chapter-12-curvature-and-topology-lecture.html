Chapter 12Curvature and Topology
Frame the whole chapter as one question: what does local curvature force about global shape? Curvature is computed in a single tangent plane, yet it dictates whether a manifold can be compact, whether its universal cover is Euclidean space, and how large its fundamental group can be. The four signpost theorems are Killing–Hopf for constant curvature, Cartan–Hadamard for nonpositive curvature, Bonnet–Myers and Synge for positive curvature. Tell students every result here trades an inequality on $K$ for a topological conclusion.

The Curvature → Topology Program

Mechanism

Curvature governs the Jacobi equation $J'' + R(J,\dot\gamma)\dot\gamma = 0$, so it controls how nearby geodesics spread, stay parallel, or refocus.

Three Regimes

  • $K<0$: geodesics diverge → no conjugate points.
  • $K=0$: linear spreading → flat models.
  • $K>0$: refocusing → conjugate points, compactness.
Jacobi fields are the bridge from local $K$ to global shapeSlide 2 / 10
The engine behind every theorem in the chapter is the Jacobi equation. A Jacobi field measures the separation of an infinitesimally nearby geodesic, and its second derivative is controlled by the curvature term $R(J,\dot\gamma)\dot\gamma$. When $K$ is negative this term pushes Jacobi fields to grow, so geodesics diverge and never refocus, which is why there are no conjugate points. When $K$ is positive the term pulls geodesics back together, creating conjugate points within a bounded distance, and that bounded distance is exactly what forces compactness in Bonnet–Myers. Make sure students see that the diagram is not decoration: it is the qualitative solution of an ODE.

Constant Curvature: Space Forms and Killing–Hopf

Killing–Hopf Theorem

A complete, connected manifold with constant curvature $c$ has universal cover isometric to exactly one model: $\mathbb{S}^n$, $\mathbb{R}^n$, or $\mathbb{H}^n$.

Space Form

Every such manifold is a quotient $\widetilde M / \Gamma$ by a discrete group $\Gamma$ of fixed-point-free isometries acting freely.

Consequence

Classifying constant-curvature manifolds reduces to classifying these quotient groups.

Constant curvature has exactly three universal coversSlide 3 / 10
Constant curvature is the cleanest case and sets the template for everything else. Killing–Hopf says the universal cover is rigidly determined by the sign of the constant: round sphere, flat space, or hyperbolic space, each unique up to scaling. Every complete connected space form is then one of these three models quotiented by a discrete group of isometries acting freely and properly discontinuously. Stress the word freely: the group must have no fixed points, otherwise the quotient is not a manifold. This reframes the classification problem as a group-theoretic one, for example flat tori and Klein bottles as quotients of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and lens spaces as quotients of the sphere.

Nonpositive Curvature: Cartan–Hadamard

Cartan–Hadamard

If $M$ is complete, connected, with sectional curvature $K\le 0$, then for each $p$ the exponential map $\exp_p:T_pM\to M$ is a smooth covering map.

Simply Connected Case

If in addition $\pi_1(M)=0$, then $\exp_p$ is a diffeomorphism, so $M\cong\mathbb{R}^n$.

Why

$K\le 0$ forbids conjugate points, so $\exp_p$ has no singular values and is a local diffeomorphism everywhere.

No conjugate points ⇒ the universal cover is $\mathbb{R}^n$Slide 4 / 10
Cartan–Hadamard is the headline result for nonpositive curvature. The key fact is that $K\le 0$ rules out conjugate points along any geodesic, so the exponential map has nonsingular differential everywhere; combined with completeness this makes $\exp_p$ a covering map onto $M$. The universal cover is therefore diffeomorphic to the tangent space $\mathbb{R}^n$, and if $M$ is already simply connected the exponential map itself is a global diffeomorphism. Emphasize the logical order: completeness gives surjectivity through Hopf–Rinow, absence of conjugate points gives the local diffeomorphism, and together they yield the covering. The geometry is rigid even though the manifold can be topologically interesting downstairs.

Consequences for $\pi_1$ and Aspherical Spaces

Contractible Cover

A Hadamard manifold $\widetilde M\cong\mathbb{R}^n$ is contractible, so all higher homotopy vanishes: $\pi_k(M)=0$ for $k\ge 2$.

Aspherical

Hence $M$ is a $K(\pi,1)$: its homotopy type is determined entirely by $\pi_1(M)=\Gamma$.

Torsion-Free $\pi_1$

$\Gamma$ acts freely on $\mathbb{R}^n$, so it can have no nontrivial finite subgroup—$\pi_1$ is torsion-free.

Nonpositive curvature forces aspherical, torsion-free topologySlide 5 / 10
This slide harvests the topological dividends of Cartan–Hadamard. Because the universal cover is contractible, the manifold is aspherical: every higher homotopy group vanishes and the entire homotopy type is encoded by the fundamental group. Such spaces are Eilenberg–MacLane spaces $K(\pi,1)$. A second consequence is that $\pi_1$ must be torsion-free, because a deck transformation of finite order would have a fixed point on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by an averaging or center-of-mass argument, contradicting free action. The closed hyperbolic surface of genus two is the canonical example: negatively curved, compact, with hyperbolic plane as universal cover and a torsion-free surface group as $\pi_1$.

Positive Curvature: Bonnet–Myers

Bonnet–Myers

If complete $M^n$ has $\operatorname{Ric}\ge (n-1)\kappa\,g$ with $\kappa>0$, then $\operatorname{diam}(M)\le \pi/\sqrt{\kappa}$.

Compactness

Bounded diameter plus completeness $\Rightarrow$ $M$ is compact (closed and bounded via Hopf–Rinow).

Finite $\pi_1$

The universal cover satisfies the same Ricci bound, so it too is compact—hence $\pi_1(M)$ is finite.

A positive Ricci lower bound caps the diameterSlide 6 / 10
Bonnet–Myers only needs a positive lower bound on Ricci curvature, not full sectional positivity, which makes it powerful. The proof is a second-variation argument: along any geodesic longer than $\pi/\sqrt{\kappa}$ you can build a variation field that decreases length, so such a geodesic cannot be minimizing, which caps the diameter. Bounded diameter plus completeness gives compactness through Hopf–Rinow. The fundamental-group corollary is elegant: the universal cover inherits the same Ricci bound and is therefore also compact, but a compact cover can only cover a base finitely many times, so $\pi_1(M)$ is finite. Contrast this sharply with the nonpositive case where $\pi_1$ was infinite and torsion-free.

Synge's Theorem: Orientability and Parity

Synge's Theorem

Let $M$ be compact with sectional curvature $K>0$. Then:

  • if $n$ is even and $M$ orientable, $M$ is simply connected;
  • if $n$ is odd, $M$ is orientable.

Proof Idea

A closed geodesic of minimal length in a free homotopy class can be shortened by parallel-transporting a fixed vector around it—a contradiction when curvature is positive.

Examples

$\mathbb{RP}^{2k}$ is non-orientable and excluded; $\mathbb{RP}^{2k+1}$ is orientable, consistent with odd dimension.

Positive curvature on compact even-dimensional manifolds kills $\pi_1$Slide 7 / 10
Synge's theorem extracts orientability and simple connectivity from positive sectional curvature, and the proof is a gem of second-variation reasoning. Take a shortest closed geodesic in a nontrivial free homotopy class. The holonomy of parallel transport once around it is an isometry of the normal space; a dimension-parity argument guarantees it fixes some unit vector. Varying the geodesic in the direction of that parallel field shortens it because $K>0$ makes the second variation negative, contradicting minimality. The conclusion splits by parity: even-dimensional orientable manifolds must be simply connected, and odd-dimensional ones must be orientable. The real projective spaces are the textbook illustration of why the parity hypotheses cannot be dropped.

Curvature Pinching and the Sphere Theorem

Sphere Theorem

A complete simply connected $M^n$ with strictly $\tfrac14$-pinched curvature, $\tfrac14<K\le 1$, is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{S}^n$.

Differentiable Version

Brendle–Schoen (2009) sharpened this to a diffeomorphism $M\cong\mathbb{S}^n$ under the same pinching.

Sharpness

The constant $\tfrac14$ is optimal: $\mathbb{CP}^n$ with the Fubini–Study metric is exactly $\tfrac14$-pinched and not a sphere.

Strong enough positive pinching forces the sphereSlide 8 / 10
This is the capstone teaser. Bonnet–Myers and Synge constrain topology but do not pin down the manifold; the sphere theorem says that if curvature is positive and tightly pinched, the manifold must actually be a sphere. The classical topological version uses Toponogov comparison and Berger–Klingenberg injectivity-radius estimates. The pinching constant one-quarter is sharp, and the borderline example is complex projective space with the Fubini–Study metric, which is exactly quarter-pinched and is emphatically not a sphere, so strict inequality is essential. Mention the 2009 differentiable sphere theorem of Brendle and Schoen, proved with Ricci flow, which upgraded homeomorphism to diffeomorphism and closed a decades-old gap.

Reading the Ledger

Nonpositive curvature spreads geodesics and inflates $\pi_1$; positive curvature refocuses them and shrinks $\pi_1$. The diameter, the conjugate-point distance, and the size of $\pi_1$ all move together with the sign of $K$.

One table, the whole chapterSlide 9 / 10
Use this ledger as the synthesis slide. Walk down the rows and emphasize the symmetry: as curvature moves from constant through nonpositive to strictly positive, the universal cover shrinks from one of three models, to contractible Euclidean space, to a compact manifold, and finally to the manifold itself. In lockstep the fundamental group moves from a free discrete quotient group, to infinite torsion-free, to finite, to trivial. The single unifying mechanism is geodesic spreading governed by Jacobi fields. Encourage students to memorize the pattern rather than the individual proofs: the sign of curvature and the size of the fundamental group are inversely linked.

Diagnostic Question

Given a curvature hypothesis, ask three things in order: do conjugate points exist, is the universal cover compact or contractible, and what does that force on $\pi_1$? Every theorem in this chapter is one route through that chain.

Close by replaying the chapter as a single argument template. Local curvature feeds the Jacobi equation, the Jacobi equation decides whether conjugate points appear within a bounded distance, the conjugate-point behavior decides whether the universal cover is compact or contractible, and the cover's topology pins down the fundamental group. Killing–Hopf, Cartan–Hadamard, Bonnet–Myers, Synge, and the sphere theorem are five instances of that one pipeline run with different curvature inputs. Leave students with the diagnostic habit: when they meet a curvature condition anywhere later in the course, trace it through conjugate points, to the universal cover, to $\pi_1$.
