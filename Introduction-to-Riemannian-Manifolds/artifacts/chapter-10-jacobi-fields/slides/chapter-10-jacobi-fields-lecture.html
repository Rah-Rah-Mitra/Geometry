Chapter 10Jacobi Fields
This chapter is where curvature finally becomes visible as the behavior of nearby geodesics. The single equation we build today controls focusing, the failure of minimization, and the global shape of the cut locus. Tell students to keep one mental picture throughout: a one-parameter family of geodesics, and the variation field that records how they drift apart. Everything else is the linearization of the geodesic equation around one geodesic.

Geodesic Variations and Variation Fields

Variation Through Geodesics

A smooth map \(\Gamma(t,s)\) with \(\Gamma(t,0)=\gamma(t)\), where each \(s\mapsto\Gamma(\cdot,s)\) is itself a geodesic.

Variation Field

The infinitesimal drift \(J(t)=\partial_s\Gamma(t,0)\) records how neighbors separate from \(\gamma\).

Key Move

Symmetry of the connection gives \(D_s\partial_t\Gamma=D_t\partial_s\Gamma\), the bridge to the equation.

Variation field = linearized geodesic driftSlide 2 / 10
Start concretely: a variation is a sheet of geodesics, and the variation field is the velocity of that sheet in the transverse direction. Stress that the symmetry lemma exchanging the two covariant derivatives is the only tool that turns the geodesic condition into a clean equation for J. Draw the arrows yourself on the board so students see J as living along a single fixed geodesic. The whole chapter is the study of which fields J can arise this way.

The Jacobi Equation

\(D_t^2 J + R(J,\gamma')\,\gamma' = 0\)

Derivation

Differentiate the geodesic equation \(D_t\partial_t\Gamma=0\) in \(s\), swap derivatives, and the curvature term appears.

Linear ODE

A linear second-order system along \(\gamma\). Its solutions are exactly the Jacobi fields.

Existence

Given \(J(0)\) and \(D_tJ(0)\) there is a unique Jacobi field: a \(2n\)-dimensional space.

Reading the Terms

Curvature acts as a position-dependent “force.” Where sectional curvature is positive, the curvature term pulls geodesics back together; where it is negative, the term pushes them apart. The tangential part \(J=t\gamma'\) and \(J=\gamma'\) are always trivial solutions, so the geometry lives in the normal component.

The linearization of the geodesic equationSlide 3 / 10
This is the heart of the chapter, so derive it slowly: it is just the geodesic equation differentiated transversally, with the Riemann tensor measuring the failure of second derivatives to commute. Emphasize that it is a genuine linear ODE, so existence and uniqueness give a 2n-dimensional solution space parameterized by initial value and initial covariant derivative. Point out the sign intuition immediately: positive curvature is restoring, negative curvature is repelling. Note the two tangential Jacobi fields so students can always quotient them out and focus on normal fields.

Jacobi Fields in Constant Curvature

Normal Jacobi Field, \(J(0)=0\)

\(|J(t)| = \dfrac{\sin(\sqrt{k}\,t)}{\sqrt{k}}\)   (k > 0)

\(|J(t)| = t\)   (k = 0)

\(|J(t)| = \dfrac{\sinh(\sqrt{-k}\,t)}{\sqrt{-k}}\)   (k < 0)

Why

With constant \(K=k\), write \(J=f(t)E\) for a parallel normal field \(E\); then \(f''+kf=0\).

First Zero

For \(k>0\) the field returns to zero at \(t=\pi/\sqrt{k}\): a conjugate point. Flat and negative curvature never refocus.

Curvature dictates sin, linear, or sinh growthSlide 4 / 10
This is the slide students remember: three curvature signs, three growth laws. Show how the vector equation collapses to the scalar ODE f double-prime plus k f equals zero once you align J with a parallel normal frame. The positive case oscillates and so it returns to zero, producing the first conjugate point on the sphere at distance pi over root k. Emphasize that flat and negative curvature give monotone growth, so geodesics never refocus there, which is the analytic seed of the Cartan-Hadamard picture.

Conjugate Points

Definition

\(q=\gamma(t_0)\) is conjugate to \(p=\gamma(0)\) if there is a nonzero Jacobi field \(J\) with \(J(0)=J(t_0)=0\).

Singular Exponential

Equivalently, \(d(\exp_p)_{t_0\gamma'(0)}\) is singular: nearby geodesics refocus to first order.

Multiplicity

The dimension of such fields is the order of the conjugate point; on \(S^n\) the antipode has order \(n-1\).

Where the exponential map degeneratesSlide 5 / 10
Define conjugate points twice: once as a Jacobi field vanishing at both ends, and once as a singularity of the differential of the exponential map. Make the equivalence explicit because students often hold only one of these and miss that they are the same statement. The sphere is the canonical example, where every great circle refocuses at the antipode with high multiplicity. Foreshadow the next slide: a conjugate point is precisely where a geodesic stops being a local minimum of length.

The Second Variation of Energy

\(\left.\dfrac{d^2}{ds^2}\right|_{0} E(\gamma_s) = \displaystyle\int_0^b \!\Big(|D_tJ|^2 - \langle R(J,\gamma')\gamma', J\rangle\Big)\,dt\)

Setup

For a fixed-endpoint variation of a geodesic with field \(J\), the first variation vanishes; the second variation is the quadratic form above.

Interpretation

The first term rewards bending the field; the curvature term, when \(K>0\), subtracts and can drive the energy down, destroying minimality.

Integration by Parts

For a field vanishing at the endpoints, \(\;\dfrac{d^2}{ds^2}E = -\displaystyle\int_0^b \big\langle\, D_t^2J + R(J,\gamma')\gamma',\,J\,\big\rangle\,dt.\) The integrand is exactly the Jacobi operator: critical directions are Jacobi fields.

Curvature enters the Hessian of energySlide 6 / 10
Frame this as computing the Hessian of the energy functional at a geodesic. The first variation vanishing is exactly the geodesic equation; the second variation is where curvature decides stability. Show the integration by parts so students see the Jacobi operator emerge as the kernel of this quadratic form, tying the analysis back to slide three. Emphasize the sign: positive sectional curvature can make the second variation negative, which is the analytic reason great circles past the antipode no longer minimize.

The Index Form and the Index Lemma

\(I(V,W)=\displaystyle\int_0^b\!\big(\langle D_tV,D_tW\rangle - \langle R(V,\gamma')\gamma',W\rangle\big)dt\)

The Bilinear Form

The symmetric form whose diagonal \(I(V,V)\) is the second variation. Its null space consists of Jacobi fields vanishing at the endpoints.

Index Lemma

If \(\gamma\) has no interior conjugate point, then among all fields with the same endpoints the Jacobi field uniquely minimizes \(I\).

Past a Conjugate Point

Beyond the first conjugate point \(I\) acquires a negative direction: the geodesic is no longer even a local minimum of length.

Morse Index

The index of \(I\) counts conjugate points with multiplicity (the Morse Index Theorem).

Conjugate points = negative directions of the index formSlide 7 / 10
Introduce the index form as the polarization of the second variation, so its diagonal is what we computed before. The Index Lemma is the workhorse: with no conjugate point inside, Jacobi fields minimize against all competitors sharing the same endpoints, which underlies most comparison theorems. Then state the converse intuition: each conjugate point passed contributes a negative direction, so the index counts them with multiplicity. Mention Morse theory lightly to motivate why this counting matters for the topology of path spaces.

Cut Points and the Cut Locus

Cut Point

The last point \(\gamma(t_c)\) up to which \(\gamma\) is minimizing from \(p\). Beyond it a shorter route exists.

Two Causes

A cut point occurs either at the first conjugate point or where a second minimizing geodesic arrives.

Cut Locus

\(\mathrm{Cut}(p)\) is the set of all cut points; its complement is a star-shaped domain on which \(\exp_p\) is a diffeomorphism.

Where global minimization finally failsSlide 8 / 10
Distinguish carefully between conjugate points, a local first-order phenomenon, and cut points, a global minimization phenomenon. The cut point is whichever comes first: a conjugate point or the arrival of a competing minimizer of equal length. Use the sphere where both coincide at the antipode, then contrast with a flat torus where the cut locus is entirely from competing geodesics with no conjugate points at all. Conclude that the injectivity domain of the exponential map is the star-shaped region bounded by the cut locus, which is the natural chart for global geometry.

Locally Symmetric Spaces: \(\nabla R = 0\)

Definition

A Riemannian manifold is locally symmetric when its curvature tensor is parallel: \(\nabla R = 0\).

Jacobi Fields Simplify

Along a geodesic, transport \(J\) into a parallel frame; \(R\) becomes a constant symmetric operator \(\mathcal{R}\) and

\(J'' + \mathcal{R}\,J = 0\)

solves explicitly via \(\sin,\;\cos,\;\sinh,\;\cosh\) of \(\sqrt{\mathcal{R}}\).

Eigenframe Picture

Diagonalizing \(\mathcal{R}\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) splits the Jacobi equation into independent scalar equations \(f_i''+\lambda_i f_i=0\) — constant-curvature behavior in each eigendirection.

Why It Matters

Conjugate points, the index form, and the cut locus all become computable from the spectrum of \(\mathcal{R}\). Symmetric spaces are the exactly-solvable models of the whole chapter.

Parallel curvature makes Jacobi fields explicitSlide 9 / 10
Present locally symmetric spaces as the place where everything from this chapter becomes computable. With parallel curvature the Riemann operator is constant in a parallel frame, so the Jacobi equation becomes a constant-coefficient linear system. Diagonalizing the curvature operator splits it into scalar oscillators, recovering the sin and sinh behavior from slide four in each eigendirection. Emphasize that this is why symmetric spaces are the standard testbeds: conjugate points and the cut locus are read straight off the eigenvalues.

What Jacobi Fields Buy Us

1

One Equation

\(D_t^2J+R(J,\gamma')\gamma'=0\) linearizes geodesic spreading; curvature is the force term.

2

Focusing

Zeros of Jacobi fields are conjugate points and singular directions of \(\exp_p\).

3

Minimization

The index form ties conjugate and cut points to when a geodesic stops minimizing.

Diagnostic Question

Given a geodesic and the sign of curvature along it, predict: do nearby geodesics spread (\(\sinh\)), drift linearly, or refocus (\(\sin\))? Where is the first conjugate point, and is the cut point caused by focusing or by a competing minimizer?

Chapter 10 recapSlide 10 / 10
Close by collapsing the chapter to three ideas: one linear equation, the focusing it predicts, and the minimization it controls. Ask students to run the diagnostic out loud for the sphere, the plane, and hyperbolic space so the sin, linear, and sinh trichotomy is automatic. Remind them that conjugate points are local and first-order while cut points are global, and that locally symmetric spaces make all of it explicit. This sets up the comparison theorems that use these tools to convert curvature bounds into statements about distance, volume, and topology.
