Families of Geodesics

Variation

A smooth family $\Gamma(s,t)$ with each $t\mapsto\Gamma(s,t)$ a geodesic.

Variation field

Differentiate in $s$ at $s=0$ to read off the spread.

Variation field

$J(t) = \left.\dfrac{\partial}{\partial s}\right|_{s=0}\Gamma(s,t)$

Claim

Every such $J$ obeys one linear equation, derived next.

Printed pp. 283-318Families of Geodesics
Begin with a smooth one-parameter family of geodesics. Differentiating in the family parameter produces a variation field along the central geodesic. This is the natural source of Jacobi fields, and it ties the abstract equation to a picture of geodesics fanning out from a common origin.

The Jacobi Equation

Linearization

Differentiate the geodesic equation through the family.

Curvature appears

Commuting $D_s$ and $D_t$ introduces $R$.

Jacobi equation

$D_t^2 J + R(J,\gamma')\,\gamma' = 0$

Reading

A second-order linear ODE for $J$ along $\gamma$.

Printed pp. 283-318The Jacobi Equation
The Jacobi equation is the linearization of the geodesic equation about a fixed geodesic. Differentiating the geodesic condition through the family and commuting covariant derivatives introduces the curvature term, giving a second-order linear ODE along the geodesic that every variation field must satisfy.

Existence and Dimension

Initial data

Prescribe $J(0)$ and $D_tJ(0)$ to fix $J$ uniquely.

Solution space

Dimension $2n$ on an $n$-manifold.

Cauchy data

$J(0)=v,\quad D_tJ(0)=w$

Splitting

Tangential fields separate from normal ones.

Printed pp. 283-318Existence and Dimension
Because the Jacobi equation is linear and second order, prescribing the field and its covariant derivative at one point determines it uniquely. Along an n-dimensional manifold the solution space has dimension two n, and tangential solutions split off cleanly from the geometrically interesting normal ones.

Jacobi Fields and the Exponential Map

Radial family

Rotate the initial velocity to sweep geodesics from $p$.

Differential

The field at the endpoint is $d(\exp_p)$ applied to $w$.

Key identity

$J(t) = d(\exp_p)_{tv}\,(t\,w)$

Payoff

ODE solutions become derivatives of the chart.

Printed pp. 283-318Jacobi Fields and the Exponential Map
The differential of the exponential map is computed by a Jacobi field. A radial geodesic carries a Jacobi field vanishing at the source whose value at the endpoint equals d of the exponential map applied to the initial velocity, turning ODE solutions into chart derivatives.

Constant Curvature Solutions

Normal field

Write $J = u(t)\,E$ for a parallel unit field $E\perp\gamma'$.

Scalar ODE

The equation collapses to $u'' + K\,u = 0$.

Printed pp. 283-318Constant Curvature Solutions
In constant curvature the Jacobi equation has explicit trigonometric solutions. Positive curvature gives sine factors that return to zero, flat space gives linear growth, and negative curvature gives hyperbolic sine factors that grow without bound, exactly mirroring how geodesics focus or spread.

Conjugate Points

Definition

$q=\gamma(b)$ is conjugate to $p=\gamma(a)$ if a nontrivial $J$ vanishes at both.

Picture

A pencil of geodesics leaves $p$ and reconverges at $q$.

Vanishing field

$J\not\equiv 0,\quad J(a)=J(b)=0$

Sphere

On $S^n$ antipodes are conjugate: geodesics refocus.

Printed pp. 283-318Conjugate Points
Two points are conjugate along a geodesic when a nontrivial Jacobi field vanishes at both. Geometrically a whole infinitesimal pencil of geodesics leaving the first point reconverges to the second, signalling that the exponential map is becoming singular there.

Conjugacy and the Exponential Map

Singularity

$q$ is conjugate iff $d(\exp_p)$ is singular at the corresponding point.

Critical value

Conjugate points are critical values of $\exp_p$.

Printed pp. 283-318Conjugacy and the Exponential Map
A point is conjugate exactly when the differential of the exponential map is singular. So conjugate points are critical values of the exponential map, where the radial chart folds and geodesics can stop being locally minimizing beyond that radius.

The Second Variation of Energy

Energy

Geodesics are critical points of $E(\gamma)=\tfrac12\int|\gamma'|^2$.

Second derivative

Its Hessian is the index form $I(V,W)$.

Second variation

$\left.\dfrac{d^2}{ds^2}\right|_{0} E = I(V,V)$

Verdict

Its sign decides whether $\gamma$ minimizes locally.

Printed pp. 283-318The Second Variation
The second variation of energy at a geodesic yields the index form, a symmetric bilinear form on vector fields built from covariant derivative and curvature terms. Its sign decides whether a geodesic is a genuine local minimum of length among nearby competitors.

The Index Form

Definition

Curvature competes with the rate of change $D_tV$.

Null directions

Jacobi fields fixed at the ends are the kernel.

Index form

$I(V,V)=\!\int_a^b\!\big(|D_tV|^2 - \langle R(V,\gamma')\gamma',V\rangle\big)\,dt$

Consequence

Past a conjugate point $I$ acquires negative directions.

Printed pp. 283-318The Index Form
Integrating by parts rewrites the index form so the Jacobi operator appears explicitly. Jacobi fields are exactly its null directions among fields fixed at the endpoints, which is why conjugate points mark the onset of negative directions and lost minimality.

Curvature Controls Focusing

$K>0$: focus

Geodesics bend back; conjugate points are forced.

$K\le 0$: spread

No conjugate points; $\exp_p$ stays a local diffeomorphism.

Printed pp. 283-318Curvature Controls Focusing
Curvature is the dial for focusing. Positive curvature pulls geodesics back together and forces conjugate points, the sphere being the extreme case with antipodal refocusing. Nonpositive curvature forbids conjugate points entirely, so geodesics spread monotonically and the exponential map stays a local diffeomorphism.
