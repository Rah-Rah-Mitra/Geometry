Frame the chapter as a catalog of reference geometries. Every later result in the book is calibrated against constant-curvature behavior, so today's job is to know the three canonical models, their isometry groups, and the structural property that ties them together: each is homogeneous and isotropic. Tell students that by the end of the lecture they should be able to write down the metric and the symmetry group of each model from memory.

The Euclidean Model: Flat Reference

Metric on R^n

g̅ = dx¹⊗dx¹ + ... + dx^n⊗dx^n

The components are constant: every point looks identical.

Curvature

All sectional curvatures vanish. The Euclidean model is the zero-curvature anchor.

Isometry Group

E(n) = O(n) ⋉ R^n

Orthogonal transformations composed with translations.

Flat: K ≡ 0Slide 2 / 10
Open with the flat model because the formula needs no apology. The metric has constant components, the connection coefficients vanish in the standard chart, and the curvature is identically zero. Emphasize that the isometry group is a semidirect product: translations act simply transitively while the orthogonal factor pins down the isotropy at any point. This decomposition foreshadows the homogeneous-space picture later in the lecture.

The Round Sphere: Constant Positive Curvature

Round Metric (Induced)

Pull back the Euclidean metric on R^{n+1} along the inclusion of the sphere of radius R.

g̊_R = ι* g̅

Sectional Curvature

K ≡ 1/R²

Constant, positive, and independent of the plane chosen.

Isometry Group

O(n+1)

Linear orthogonal transformations of the ambient space restrict to isometries of S^n.

Positive curvature anchor: K = 1/R²Slide 3 / 10
Stress that the round metric is not invented; it is inherited from the ambient Euclidean structure by pullback through the inclusion. That is the cleanest construction available, and it gives the isometry group for free: any orthogonal map of the ambient space sends the sphere to itself. Mention that radius enters as an overall scale, with curvature inversely proportional to the square of the radius, so a very large sphere looks locally flat.

Three Faces of Hyperbolic Space

Hyperboloid Model

{x ∈ R^{n,1} : ⟨x,x⟩ = -1, x⁰ > 0}

Pull back the Minkowski form; the induced metric is positive definite on the upper sheet.

Poincaré Ball

g = 4(1 - |x|²)^{-2} g̅

Conformal to the Euclidean ball; geodesics are arcs perpendicular to the boundary sphere.

Half-Space Model

g = y^{-2} g̅, y > 0

Conformal to the upper half-space; geodesics are vertical lines and semicircles.

Constant negative curvature K = -1Slide 4 / 10
The pedagogical point is that hyperbolic space admits several models because each is convenient for a different question. The hyperboloid model makes the symmetry group transparent through the Lorentz form. The Poincaré ball makes conformal structure visible and supports the cleanest boundary at infinity. The half-space presentation is the one to use for explicit computations and matrix actions of PSL(2,R). All three are isometric, so the choice is rhetorical.

Use the table to consolidate. Each model is recognized by its isometry group rather than by an arbitrary parametrization. The shared feature is that the isotropy subgroup at a point is the full orthogonal group on the tangent space, which is what we will define as isotropy in the next slide. The amber box previews the structural theorem: maximal symmetry and constant curvature are two names for the same property.

Homogeneous, Isotropic, and Both

Homogeneous

The isometry group acts transitively: every pair of points has an isometry between them.

Isotropic at p

The isotropy subgroup at p acts transitively on unit vectors in T_pM.

Consequence

Homogeneous + isotropic ⇒ constant sectional curvature.

Two symmetry conditions force one curvatureSlide 6 / 10
Distinguish the two conditions carefully. Homogeneity equalizes points, isotropy equalizes directions at a point. Either alone is weaker than both together. The combination forces the sectional curvature to be the same on every two-plane, which is exactly the property that identifies our three models. Mention that even isotropy at a single point, combined with homogeneity, suffices to spread isotropy everywhere.

Invariant Metrics on Lie Groups

Left-Invariant Metric

Pick any inner product on the Lie algebra g = T_e G, then translate it across the group by left multiplication.

g_h(X,Y) = ⟨(dL_{h^{-1}})X, (dL_{h^{-1}})Y⟩_e

Bi-Invariant Metric

Invariant under both left and right translation. Exists when the inner product is Ad-invariant.

Compact Lie groups always admit one (average over the group).

Bi-Invariant Geodesics

One-parameter subgroups t ↦ exp(tX) are geodesics through the identity.

Caution

A general left-invariant metric is not bi-invariant; the right-translation symmetry can fail badly on noncompact groups.

Symmetry from the group, not from the chartSlide 7 / 10
Frame the construction in three steps: choose an inner product on the Lie algebra, spread it by left translation to get a left-invariant metric, then average over the group to obtain bi-invariance when the group is compact. Stress that bi-invariant metrics are the geometrically richest case because the exponential map of the Lie group and the Riemannian exponential agree, so one-parameter subgroups become geodesics.

Homogeneous Spaces and Symmetric Spaces

Homogeneous Space

G/H with G a Lie group and H a closed subgroup. The model carries a G-invariant metric when the isotropy representation preserves an inner product.

Symmetric Space

A homogeneous space with a geodesic-reversing isometry at every point.

Includes the three constant-curvature models as special cases.

Examples

S^n = O(n+1)/O(n), H^n = O⁺(n,1)/O(n), Grassmannians, complex projective spaces.

Quotient construction unifies the catalogSlide 8 / 10
Show how the homogeneous-space formalism unifies the chapter. Each model is a quotient G/H where G is the isometry group and H is the isotropy subgroup. The metric descends from a G-invariant tensor on G provided the isotropy representation preserves an inner product. Symmetric spaces are the homogeneous spaces with the extra reflection symmetry at every point, and our three constant-curvature models all qualify.

Pseudo-Riemannian Models

Minkowski Space R^{n,1}

q = -dt² + dx¹² + ... + dx^n²

Flat Lorentzian model. Isometry group is the Poincaré group O(n,1) ⋉ R^{n+1}.

de Sitter and Anti-de Sitter

Lorentzian analogues of the sphere and hyperbolic space, obtained as quadric submanifolds of flat spaces of signature (n,1) or (n-1,2).

Signature changes; constructions persistSlide 9 / 10
End the substantive content by relaxing positive-definiteness. The same recipes that produced the round sphere and hyperboloid models still apply once the ambient quadratic form has mixed signature. Minkowski space is the flat Lorentzian model and underwrites special relativity; de Sitter and anti-de Sitter spaces are the constant-curvature Lorentzian analogues. Mention that geodesics now split into timelike, spacelike, and null classes, and physical interpretation gives the casework its meaning.
