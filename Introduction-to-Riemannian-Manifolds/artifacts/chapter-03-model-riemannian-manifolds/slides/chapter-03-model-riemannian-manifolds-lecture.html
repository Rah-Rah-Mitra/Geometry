Symmetry: Homogeneous, Isotropic, Frame-Homogeneous

Homogeneous

The isometry group acts transitively on points — every point looks the same.

Isotropic at $p$

Isometries fixing $p$ act transitively on unit tangent directions.

Frame-homogeneous

Transitive action on orthonormal frames $(p, e_1, \dots, e_n)$.

Maximal symmetry

$\dim \mathrm{Isom}(M) = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$

Consequence

Frame-homogeneity forces constant sectional curvature.

Printed pp. 55-84Symmetry of the Models
Before naming the models we fix the symmetry vocabulary. A manifold is homogeneous when its isometry group acts transitively on points, and isotropic at a point when isometries fixing that point rotate the tangent space freely. Frame-homogeneity strengthens this to transitivity on orthonormal frames, the property that pins down our three models.

Euclidean Space $\mathbb{R}^n$

Flat metric

$\bar g = \delta_{ij}\, dx^i\, dx^j$

Curvature

$K \equiv 0$; the Riemann tensor vanishes identically.

Geodesics

Straight lines $\gamma(t) = p + tv$.

Isometry group

$\mathrm{E}(n) = \mathbb{R}^n \rtimes \mathrm{O}(n)$: translations and rotations.

Printed pp. 55-84Euclidean Space
Euclidean space is the flat model with sectional curvature zero everywhere. Its isometry group is the semidirect product of translations and orthogonal rotations, geodesics are straight lines, and the metric is the familiar dot product written in standard coordinates. It is the curvature-zero reference point for the comparison geometry.

The Round Sphere $\mathbb{S}^n(R)$

Induced metric

$\mathring g = \iota^*\bar g, \quad K = \dfrac{1}{R^2}$

Geodesics

Great circles; antipodes joined by many minimizers.

Isometry group

$\mathrm{O}(n+1)$ acting linearly on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

Printed pp. 55-84The Round Sphere
The round sphere of radius R carries the metric induced from the surrounding Euclidean space and has constant positive curvature one over R squared. Its geodesics are great circles, its isometry group is the orthogonal group, and antipodal points are joined by infinitely many minimizing geodesics, the first sign of conjugate points.

Stereographic Coordinates on the Sphere

Conformal form

$\mathring g = \dfrac{4R^4}{(R^2+|u|^2)^2}\,\delta_{ij}\,du^i\,du^j$

Conformal

The chart rescales the flat metric — angles are preserved.

Pattern

The same conformal trick reappears for $\mathbb{H}^n$.

Printed pp. 55-84Stereographic Coordinates
Stereographic projection sends the sphere minus a pole onto a Euclidean plane, expressing the round metric as a conformal rescaling of the flat metric. This conformal factor recurs for hyperbolic space and shows why angles, but not lengths, are preserved by the projection chart we use repeatedly later.

Hyperbolic Space: Four Models

Hyperboloid

Upper sheet in Minkowski space; Lorentz isometries.

Poincare ball

Conformal; geodesics are arcs meeting $\partial$ orthogonally.

Upper half-space

Vertical lines and semicircles as geodesics.

Beltrami-Klein

Geodesics are straight chords; angles distorted.

Printed pp. 55-84Four Hyperbolic Models
Hyperbolic space has constant negative curvature and appears in four standard guises: the hyperboloid, the Poincare ball, the upper half-space, and the Beltrami-Klein ball. Each model trades clarity of one feature, such as isometries or geodesics, against convenience in another, but all four are mutually isometric realizations.

The Hyperboloid Model

Minkowski form

$q(x) = -(x^0)^2 + \textstyle\sum_i (x^i)^2 = -1,\ \ x^0>0$

Metric

Induced from the Lorentz form $q$ on $\mathbb{R}^{1,n}$.

Isometries

$\mathrm{O}^+(1,n)$, the orthochronous Lorentz group.

Printed pp. 55-84Hyperboloid Model
The hyperboloid model realizes hyperbolic space as the upper sheet of a two-sheeted hyperboloid in Minkowski space, with the metric induced from the Lorentz form. Its isometry group is the orthochronous Lorentz group, making the analogy with the sphere sitting inside Euclidean space exact and structurally elegant.

The Poincare Ball Model

Conformal metric

$g = \dfrac{4\,\delta_{ij}\,du^i\,du^j}{(1-|u|^2)^2}, \quad |u|<1$

Geodesics

Diameters and circular arcs $\perp$ to the boundary.

Angles

Conformal: measured exactly as Euclidean angles.

Printed pp. 55-84Poincare Ball
The Poincare ball is the unit ball with a conformally flat metric that blows up near the boundary sphere. Geodesics are diameters and circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles, angles are measured Euclideanly, and the boundary sits at infinite metric distance from any interior point.

Upper Half-Space and Beltrami-Klein

Half-space metric

$g = \dfrac{\delta_{ij}\,dx^i\,dx^j}{(x^n)^2}, \quad x^n>0$

Beltrami-Klein

Geodesics are straight Euclidean chords of the ball.

Printed pp. 55-84Half-Space & Klein
The upper half-space model places hyperbolic geometry on points with positive last coordinate, with geodesics that are vertical lines and semicircles centered on the boundary. The Beltrami-Klein ball instead renders geodesics as straight Euclidean chords, at the cost of distorting angles throughout the picture.

Equivalence of the Hyperbolic Models

One manifold

All four models are isometric to the same $\mathbb{H}^n$.

To the ball

Project the hyperboloid from $(-1,0,\dots,0)$ to the Poincare ball.

To Klein

Central projection through the origin gives the Klein model.

Printed pp. 55-84Model Equivalence
All four hyperbolic models describe the same Riemannian manifold. Central projection from the hyperboloid through the origin lands on the Beltrami-Klein ball, while projection from a lower vertex produces the Poincare ball, and an inversion carries the ball to the upper half-space. The dictionary between them is explicit.

Isometry Groups and Frame-Homogeneity

Isometry groups

$\mathrm{E}(n),\quad \mathrm{O}(n+1),\quad \mathrm{O}^+(1,n)$

Euclidean

$\mathbb{R}^n \rtimes \mathrm{O}(n)$.

Sphere

$\mathrm{O}(n+1)$, linear on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

Hyperbolic

$\mathrm{O}^+(1,n)$, Lorentz transformations.

Theorem

frame-homogeneous $\;\Longrightarrow\; K \equiv \text{const}$

Homogeneous + isotropic

Each group acts transitively on orthonormal frames, so geometry is identical in every direction at every point.

Printed pp. 55-84Isometry Groups
Each model space is frame-homogeneous: its isometry group acts transitively on orthonormal frames, so the geometry looks identical from every point in every direction. This maximal symmetry is exactly what forces constant sectional curvature and singles out these three families of model spaces uniquely.

Placing the flat plane, the round sphere, and the hyperbolic disk together summarizes the chapter. Their geodesics diverge at zero, converging, and exponential rates respectively, and the sign of curvature governs angle sums of triangles.
