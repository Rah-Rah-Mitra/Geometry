The Parallel Postulate

Euclid's fifth

Through a point off a line, exactly one parallel exists.

Two millennia

Every attempt to prove it from the others failed.

Resolution

Consistent geometries deny it. Curvature is the difference.

Printed pp. 1-8The Parallel Postulate
Euclid's fifth postulate resisted proof for two thousand years. Attempts to derive it failed because consistent geometries exist where it is false. Replacing it yields spherical geometry with no parallels and hyperbolic geometry with many, and curvature is precisely what distinguishes these alternatives.

Curvature of a Plane Curve

Idea

How fast does the unit tangent turn as we walk along the curve?

Definition

$\kappa(s) = \left\lVert \dfrac{dT}{ds} \right\rVert$

Line

A straight line never turns: $\kappa = 0$.

Printed pp. 1-8Curvature of a Plane Curve
For a plane curve the curvature measures how fast the unit tangent turns with arc length. A straight line never turns, so its curvature is zero. A tight bend turns quickly, giving large curvature, which we now make precise through the best-fitting circle at each point.
Introduction to Riemannian ManifoldsChapter 1

The Osculating Circle

Best second-order fit

Matches position, tangent, and bending at the point.

Radius of curvature

Its radius $R$ records how tightly the curve bends.

Parametrization-free

The circle depends only on the curve's shape.

Printed pp. 1-8The Osculating Circle
At a point of a curve there is a unique circle that matches position, tangent direction, and second-order bending. This osculating circle has radius R, the radius of curvature. Its reciprocal one over R measures how sharply the curve bends, independent of how we parametrize the curve.
Introduction to Riemannian ManifoldsChapter 1

Curvature: $\kappa = 1/R$

Key formula

$\kappa = \dfrac{1}{R}$

Large radius

Gentle bend, small $\kappa$.

Small radius

Sharp turn, large $\kappa$.

Printed pp. 1-8Kappa Equals One Over R
The signed curvature kappa equals one over the radius of the osculating circle. Large radius means gentle bending and small kappa; small radius means a sharp turn and large kappa. This single scalar captures everything about second-order shape for a plane curve.

From Curves to Surfaces

Many directions

Slice the surface by normal planes; each cut is a plane curve.

Normal curvatures

Each slice has its own $\kappa$ at the point.

Principal curvatures

$\kappa_1 = \max\,\kappa_n,\quad \kappa_2 = \min\,\kappa_n$

Printed pp. 1-8From Curves to Surfaces
On a surface curvature becomes richer because we may slice in many directions. Each normal plane cuts out a plane curve with its own curvature. The principal curvatures are the extreme values among all these normal sections, and their product defines the Gaussian curvature of the surface.
Introduction to Riemannian ManifoldsChapter 1

Gaussian Curvature

Definition (Gauss)

$K = \kappa_1 \, \kappa_2$

One number

The product of the two principal curvatures at a point.

Sign matters

The sign of $K$ classifies the local shape.

$K>0$

Dome-like: both principal curvatures share a sign.

$K<0$

Saddle-like: principal curvatures have opposite signs.

$K=0$

Flat in at least one principal direction (a plane or cylinder).

Printed pp. 1-8Gaussian Curvature
Gauss defined the curvature of a surface as the product of the two principal curvatures, kappa one times kappa two. Its sign classifies local shape: positive where the surface is dome-like, negative where it is saddle-like, and zero where one principal direction stays flat.

Three Model Surfaces

Printed pp. 1-8The Sign of Curvature
Three model surfaces fix intuition. The sphere bends the same way in every direction and has positive Gaussian curvature. The flat plane has zero curvature everywhere. The saddle bends oppositely in two directions, so the product of principal curvatures is negative.

Gauss's Theorema Egregium

Theorem (remarkable)

$K \text{ is intrinsic: } K = K(g)$

Intrinsic

$K$ depends only on lengths and angles measured within the surface.

Bending preserves it

Roll paper into a cylinder: $K$ stays zero throughout.

Printed pp. 1-8Theorema Egregium
Gauss's remarkable theorem states that Gaussian curvature is intrinsic: it can be computed from measurements made entirely within the surface, with no reference to the surrounding space. Bending paper preserves it, which is why a flat sheet cannot wrap a sphere without distortion.

Curvature and Topology

Gauss-Bonnet

$\displaystyle \int_S K \, dA = 2\pi \, \chi(S)$

Local meets global

Total curvature is fixed by the Euler characteristic.

Example

For any sphere, $\chi = 2$, so the integral is $4\pi$.

Printed pp. 1-8Curvature and Topology
The Gauss-Bonnet theorem ties total curvature to topology: integrating Gaussian curvature over a closed surface gives two pi times its Euler characteristic. Local bending and global shape are therefore inseparable, the third classical problem that motivates the whole subject.

Generalizing to Manifolds

Riemannian metric

A smooth choice of lengths and angles: $g$.

Geodesics

Straightest paths replace straight lines.

Curvature tensor

$R$ generalizes $K$ to every dimension.

Sectional curvature

$K(\sigma) = \langle R(e_1,e_2)e_2,\, e_1\rangle$

The road ahead

Surfaces were the prototype; the rest of the book builds the machinery for general $n$-manifolds.

Printed pp. 1-8Generalizing to Manifolds
The book's program is to lift these surface ideas to Riemannian manifolds of any dimension. A metric measures lengths and angles, geodesics generalize straight lines, and the Riemann curvature tensor encodes in higher dimensions exactly what Gaussian curvature captured for surfaces.
