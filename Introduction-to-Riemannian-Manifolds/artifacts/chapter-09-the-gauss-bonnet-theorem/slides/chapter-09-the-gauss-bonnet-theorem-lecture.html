Chapter 9The Gauss-Bonnet Theorem
Open by framing Gauss-Bonnet as the first place in a Riemannian course where local differential data and global topological data are forced to agree. The chapter starts with plane geometry, because the familiar fact that triangle angles sum to pi is the boundary case of a deeper identity. Promise the audience that by the end of the lecture they will see angle defect, geodesic turning, and Euler characteristic as three avatars of the same conservation law, and that the proof strategy is to integrate curvature against a triangulation rather than against arbitrary regions.

Angle Defect on a Geodesic Triangle

α+β+γ = π
α+β+γ > π
α+β+γ < π

Local Bonnet Identity

For a geodesic triangle T on a surface with Gaussian curvature K:

α + β + γ - π = ∫T K dA

Curvature is the angle excess per unit areaSlide 2 / 10
Three pictures, one identity. On the plane the interior angles add to pi exactly because curvature is zero everywhere. On the sphere there is a positive excess that equals the curvature integral. On a saddle there is a deficit that equals a signed curvature integral. The slide is deliberately framed before any proof, because the equality of an algebraic quantity, the angle sum minus pi, with a geometric integral, is the first surprise of the chapter. Encourage students to read the right hand side as an averaged curvature weighted by area, and to notice that the equation is dimensionally exact rather than approximate.

Geodesic Curvature and Signed Turning

Geodesic Curvature

κg = ⟨ ∇T T, N ⟩

The signed rate at which a unit-speed curve turns away from being a geodesic, measured against the inward normal.

Vertex Turn

At a corner the smooth turning fails; the curve absorbs a discrete exterior angle θi = π - αi.

Sign Convention

A geodesic has κg = 0. Curving toward the interior counts positively.

Boundary turning splits into smooth and discrete partsSlide 3 / 10
Introduce geodesic curvature as the local measurement of how hard a curve tries to leave geodesic motion. Stress that the sign is fixed by the choice of inward normal so that students do not confuse it with a free parameter. On a polygonal boundary the smooth turning is supplemented by jump turns at vertices, and those jumps are exterior angles. The slide also previews the algebraic identity used in the next slide, where smooth geodesic curvature and vertex jumps add up to two pi for a simple loop in the plane.

Local Gauss-Bonnet on a Polygon

∫P K dA + ∫∂P κg ds + ∑i θi = 2π

Curvature Integral

Total Gaussian curvature held inside the polygonal region P.

Boundary Turn

Accumulated smooth geodesic curvature along the edges of ∂P.

Vertex Jumps

Sum of exterior angles at the corners, counted with sign.

Reading

Three sources of turning combine into a fixed budget of 2π for a single positively oriented loop. Bending the boundary, denting the interior, or sharpening a corner trades against the other two terms but never against the total.

One conservation law, three accounting columnsSlide 4 / 10
Frame the local theorem as a budgeting law. The right hand side is fixed; the three quantities on the left rearrange their contributions whenever the polygon is deformed. Sketch how the geodesic triangle case recovers slide two: smooth geodesic curvature vanishes on geodesic edges, and the exterior angles sum to three pi minus the interior angles. Subtracting that arrangement from two pi reproduces the angle defect identity. Stress that the proof of the local statement uses an orthonormal frame along the boundary together with a careful interpretation of the rotation angle, but that the budget interpretation is what you want the class to remember.

Global Gauss-Bonnet for Compact Surfaces

Theorem

For a compact, oriented Riemannian surface (M,g) without boundary:

∫M K dA = 2π χ(M)

Proof Sketch

Triangulate, apply the local identity on each triangle, and sum. Smooth boundary integrals cancel along shared edges, and vertex turns assemble into a count of faces, edges, and vertices.

The right hand side is metric-blindSlide 5 / 10
Now write the headline result. Two pi times the Euler characteristic is independent of the metric, the orientation, and any chosen triangulation, while the left hand side is built from local curvature data. Walk through the proof sketch by imagining edges shared by adjacent triangles: their boundary contributions enter with opposite orientations and cancel, while the vertex sums regroup into a star around each vertex. After the regrouping the three counts that survive are the face count, the edge count, and the vertex count, which is exactly the alternating sum that defines the Euler characteristic.

Euler Characteristic via Triangulation

Combinatorial Formula

χ(M) = V - E + F

Independent of the chosen triangulation; only the topology of M matters.

Genus Form for Surfaces

For a closed orientable surface of genus g: χ = 2 - 2g. The sphere gives 2, the torus gives 0, a double torus gives -2.

One topological number, fixed across all metricsSlide 6 / 10
Linger on the table. Each row is a separate diffeomorphism class, but within a single row the integrated curvature is forced to a specific value regardless of how the metric is deformed. This is the moment to remind students that triangulation invariance of the alternating sum is a topological theorem that comes from algebraic topology, while Gauss-Bonnet is the bridge that ties this integer to differential geometry. The class should leave the slide knowing exactly which Euler characteristic belongs to each standard surface.

Curvature Constraints from Topology

Sphere

Any metric on S2 has ∫ K dA = 4π > 0, so the average curvature is strictly positive.

Torus

Any metric on T2 has ∫ K dA = 0; positive and negative curvature must balance exactly.

Higher Genus

For Σg with g ≥ 2, total curvature is strictly negative; average curvature must be negative.

Rigidity Slogan

The sign of 2 - 2g dictates the average sign of K. No metric can violate that average. Pointwise curvature is free, but its integral is locked.

Topology dictates the sign of the averageSlide 7 / 10
Move the lecture into its first set of corollaries. The takeaway is that the topology of a compact surface determines the sign of its average curvature. A sphere cannot be coerced into a flat or negatively curved geometry on average, no matter how it is stretched. A torus must allocate equal positive and negative regions if it is curved at all. Higher genus surfaces cannot avoid a negative average. Use these statements to motivate the hyperbolic geometries of higher genus surfaces and the uniformization theorem coming in later chapters.

Rigidity and Topological Obstructions

No Flat Sphere

There is no flat Riemannian metric on S2: a metric with K ≡ 0 would force 0 = 4π.

No Negative Torus

There is no metric with K < 0 everywhere on T2; the integral could not vanish.

Positive Curvature Forbids Genus

If K > 0 everywhere on a compact (M,g), then χ(M) > 0, so M ≅ S2.

Sign Inversion

If K < 0 everywhere, then χ(M) < 0, so the genus is at least two.

Pattern

The local sign condition on K is amplified into a global topological constraint. Local differential information becomes a global gatekeeper for which manifolds admit which curvatures.

Local sign forces global genusSlide 8 / 10
Crystallize the obstructions slide by slide. The first row eliminates flat metrics on a sphere and uniformly negative metrics on a torus. The second row inverts the implication: if curvature has a fixed sign, only one class of topology can host it. These are the cleanest applications of Gauss-Bonnet in the chapter and they generalize the elementary fact that you cannot flatten an orange peel without tearing. Encourage students to test the implication graph by trying to construct counterexamples and watching them fail.

Worked Examples

Round Sphere

K = 1/r2; area 4πr2; integral 4π = 2πχ.

Flat Torus

K ≡ 0; integral 0 = 2π(2 - 2); consistent with χ = 0.

Hyperbolic Surface

K = -1; area 4π; integral -4π = 2π(-2).

Reading Across the Row

Each example fixes a curvature and reads off the area, or fixes the topology and reads off the area integral. Gauss-Bonnet is the matching condition that ties the two readings together.

Three model surfaces, one identitySlide 9 / 10
Walk through three canonical models. The round sphere multiplies a constant positive curvature by a known area to give exactly four pi, which is two pi times the Euler characteristic. The flat torus has zero curvature everywhere and zero Euler characteristic, so both sides vanish. The hyperbolic surface of genus two has constant curvature negative one and total area four pi, giving minus four pi, which matches two pi times negative two. Each row is a sanity check that students should be able to reproduce on the board. Emphasize that genus two surfaces are not embeddable as smooth hyperbolic surfaces in three-space, so the picture is schematic.

One Diagnostic Question

Given a compact surface and a candidate metric, ask whether the integrated curvature equals 2πχ. If it does not, the metric does not exist on that topology, no matter how plausible it looks pointwise.

Chapter 9 recapSlide 10 / 10
Close the lecture by lining up the three layers in order: local angle defect, polygonal accounting, and the global theorem. Reinforce the diagnostic that any proposed metric on a compact surface must integrate to two pi times the Euler characteristic. If the test fails, the construction is invalid before any further analysis is needed. This pattern, where a local differential quantity integrates to a topological invariant, sets up the higher-dimensional Gauss-Bonnet-Chern story and the index theorems students will encounter in later courses.
