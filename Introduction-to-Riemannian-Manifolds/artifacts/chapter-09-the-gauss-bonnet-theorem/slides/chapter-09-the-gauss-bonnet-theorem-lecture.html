The Gauss-Bonnet Theorem

Where local curvature meets global topology

Local side

Gaussian curvature $K$, geodesic curvature $\kappa_g$, and exterior angles of a region.

Global side

The Euler characteristic $\chi(M)$, a number fixed by topology alone.

The grand identity

$\displaystyle\int_M K\,dA = 2\pi\,\chi(M)$

Printed pp. 263-282The Gauss-Bonnet Theorem
This chapter, printed pages 263 to 282, ties local curvature to global topology. We open with the promise of the climax: the integral of Gaussian curvature over a closed surface equals two pi times its Euler characteristic, a quantity fixed by topology alone and blind to the metric we chose.

Geodesic Curvature

Turning within the surface

$\kappa_g$ measures the tangential acceleration of a unit-speed curve, seen intrinsically.

Geodesics

A curve is a geodesic exactly when $\kappa_g \equiv 0$: it does not turn inside the surface.

Definition

$\kappa_g = \langle D_t\dot\gamma,\, N\times\dot\gamma\rangle$

Printed pp. 263-282Geodesic Curvature
Before any integral theorem we need the right notion of bending. Geodesic curvature measures how far a curve on a surface deviates from being a geodesic, recording only the tangential turning seen from within the surface and discarding the normal bending forced by the ambient embedding.

Turning Angle and Exterior Angles

Smooth turning

Along a smooth arc the tangent rotates; its total rotation is $\int \kappa_g\,ds$.

Corner jumps

At a vertex the tangent jumps by the exterior angle $\varepsilon_i = \pi - \theta_i$.

Rotation index

$\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\!\kappa_g\,ds + \sum_i \varepsilon_i = 2\pi$ (flat)

Printed pp. 263-282Turning Angle and Exterior Angles
Along a smooth boundary the unit tangent rotates continuously, and that rotation rate integrates the geodesic curvature. At a corner the tangent jumps; the exterior angle records that instantaneous turn. Together continuous turning and corner jumps account for the total rotation of the tangent.

The Gauss-Bonnet Formula for a Region

Three contributions

Interior curvature, boundary geodesic curvature, and corner angles together total $2\pi$.

Shape independence

The right side is always $2\pi$, no matter how the region is bent or sized.

Local Gauss-Bonnet

$\displaystyle\int_\Omega K\,dA + \int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa_g\,ds + \sum_{i}\varepsilon_i = 2\pi$

Reading it

Curvature inside trades off against turning on the boundary and angles at the corners.

Printed pp. 263-282The Gauss-Bonnet Formula for a Region
The local Gauss-Bonnet formula balances three contributions over a polygonal region: the curvature integrated over the interior, the geodesic curvature integrated along the smooth edges, and the exterior angles summed at the vertices. Their total is exactly two pi, independent of the region's shape.

Geodesic Polygons

Edges vanish

If every edge is a geodesic then $\kappa_g\equiv 0$, killing the boundary integral.

Interior angles

Substitute $\varepsilon_i = \pi - \theta_i$ to expose the angle sum directly.

$n$-gon with geodesic edges

$\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_i = (n-2)\pi + \int_\Omega K\,dA$

Flat check

When $K=0$ this recovers the Euclidean angle sum $(n-2)\pi$.

Printed pp. 263-282Geodesic Polygons
When every edge is a geodesic the boundary integral vanishes, leaving curvature plus exterior angles equal to two pi. Rewriting exterior angles as pi minus interior angles converts the statement into a clean relation between total curvature and the sum of interior angles of the polygon.

Geodesic Triangles and Angle Excess

The excess

For a triangle ($n=3$) the angle sum minus $\pi$ equals the integrated curvature.

Angle excess

$\displaystyle(\theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3)-\pi = \int_\Omega K\,dA$

Sign of curvature

Excess $>0$, $=0$, $<0$ marks positive, flat, negative curvature.

Printed pp. 263-282Geodesic Triangles and Angle Excess
For a geodesic triangle the formula says the sum of interior angles minus pi equals the integrated curvature over the triangle. This angle excess is positive where curvature is positive, zero in the flat plane, and negative on saddle-like surfaces, making curvature directly measurable by angles.

Worked Example: A Sphere Triangle

Constant curvature

On a sphere of radius $R$, $K = 1/R^2$, so excess $=\mathrm{Area}/R^2$.

Spherical excess

$\displaystyle\sum\theta_i - \pi = \frac{\mathrm{Area}(\Omega)}{R^2}$

The octant

Three right angles: $\tfrac{3\pi}{2}-\pi=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, and area $=\tfrac18(4\pi R^2)$.

Printed pp. 263-282Spherical Triangle Example
On a sphere of radius R the Gaussian curvature is the constant one over R squared, so the excess equals the area divided by R squared. A triangle with three right angles, an octant of the sphere, has angle sum three halves pi and area exactly one eighth of the total sphere.

From Local to Global by Triangulation

Triangulate

Cover a compact surface with geodesic-edged triangles and sum the local formula.

Edges cancel

Shared edges are traversed twice in opposite senses; their $\kappa_g$ integrals cancel.

Printed pp. 263-282From Local to Global
To globalize we triangulate a compact surface and sum the local formula over every triangle. Interior edge integrals cancel in pairs because adjacent triangles traverse shared edges in opposite directions, and the vertex angles regroup into the combinatorial Euler characteristic of the triangulation.

The Euler Characteristic

Combinatorial count

From any triangulation, $\chi = V - E + F$, independent of the choice.

Genus formula

$\chi(M) = 2 - 2g$

Examples

Sphere $\chi=2$, torus $\chi=0$, genus-two $\chi=-2$.

Printed pp. 263-282The Euler Characteristic
The Euler characteristic equals vertices minus edges plus faces and is a topological invariant independent of the chosen triangulation. For an orientable closed surface of genus g it equals two minus two g, so the sphere gives two, the torus gives zero, and higher genus gives negative values.

The Global Gauss-Bonnet Theorem

Statement

For a compact surface $M$ without boundary, total curvature is fixed by topology.

Theorem

$\displaystyle\int_M K\,dA = 2\pi\,\chi(M)$

Metric blind

The left side bends with the metric; the right side never moves.

Printed pp. 263-282The Global Gauss-Bonnet Theorem
The global theorem states that the integral of Gaussian curvature over a compact surface without boundary equals two pi times its Euler characteristic. The left side depends on the metric; the right side depends only on topology. Hence total curvature is a topological invariant, deformable but conserved.

Sphere versus Torus

Sphere

$\chi=2 \Rightarrow \int K\,dA = 4\pi$. No metric of everywhere-negative $K$ exists.

Torus

$\chi=0 \Rightarrow \int K\,dA = 0$. A genuinely flat metric is allowed.

Printed pp. 263-282Sphere versus Torus
The sphere has Euler characteristic two, forcing total curvature four pi, so a metric of all-negative curvature is impossible there. The torus has characteristic zero, forcing total curvature zero, which is why the flat torus exists. Topology dictates what curvatures a surface may carry.

Consequences and Recap

Topology constrains geometry

$\int K\,dA > 0$ forces $\chi>0$; a flat closed surface must have $\chi=0$.

Key formula

$\int_M K\,dA = 2\pi\,\chi(M)$

What we built

  • Geodesic curvature $\kappa_g$ and exterior angles $\varepsilon_i$.
  • Local Gauss-Bonnet: $\int K + \int\kappa_g + \sum\varepsilon_i = 2\pi$.
  • Angle excess $=\int_\Omega K\,dA$ for geodesic triangles.
  • Global identity linking total curvature to $\chi(M)$.
Printed pp. 263-282Consequences and Recap
Gauss-Bonnet constrains geometry by topology: positively curved closed surfaces must be spheres or projective planes, and a flat metric forces Euler characteristic zero. We recap geodesic curvature, the local formula, angle excess, and the global identity linking total curvature to two pi chi.
