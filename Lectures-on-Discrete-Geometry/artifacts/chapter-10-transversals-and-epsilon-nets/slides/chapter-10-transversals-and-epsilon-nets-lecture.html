Set Systems and Ranges

Range space

A ground set $X$ plus a family $\mathcal{R}$ of admissible subsets.

Geometric ranges

Points pierced by halfplanes, disks, or boxes.

Range space

Printed pp. 231-264Set Systems and Ranges
A range space pairs a ground set with a family of admissible subsets, for instance points pierced by halfplanes or disks. Counting how many distinct ranges a finite point set can carve out is the combinatorial quantity that controls every sampling theorem appearing later in this chapter.

Shattering and VC Dimension

Shattered

$A\subseteq X$ is shattered if every subset of $A$ equals $A\cap R$ for some range $R$.

VC dimension

The size of the largest shattered set.

Definition

$\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{R})=\max\{|A|:A\text{ shattered}\}$

Printed pp. 231-264Shattering and VC Dimension
A set is shattered when every one of its subsets is cut out by some range. The VC dimension is the size of the largest shattered set, a single integer that measures the combinatorial richness of the range space and bounds its complexity throughout the sampling arguments to come.

The Shatter Function and Sauer-Shelah

Shatter function

$\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(m)$ = max number of distinct ranges on $m$ points.

Polynomial ceiling

Bounded VC dimension forbids exponential growth.

Sauer-Shelah lemma

$\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(m)\le \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{m}{i}=O\!\big(m^{d}\big)$

Printed pp. 231-264The Shatter Function and Sauer-Shelah
Sauer and Shelah prove that bounded VC dimension forces the shatter function to grow only polynomially, never exponentially. This polynomial ceiling is the structural miracle that makes random sampling work, turning a potentially exponential family of ranges into a tame, low-complexity object we can union-bound over.

Epsilon-Nets

Heavy range

A range carrying at least an $\varepsilon$ fraction of the measure.

Net

A subset that meets every heavy range.

Definition

$N\cap R\neq\varnothing\;\;\forall R:\;\mu(R)\ge\varepsilon$

Reading

No large region escapes the net, though the net is small.

Printed pp. 231-264Epsilon-Nets
An epsilon-net is a subset of the ground set that meets every range heavier than an epsilon fraction of the measure. It is a guarantee of coverage: no large region escapes the net, even though the net itself may be tiny compared with the entire ground set we are sampling from.

The Epsilon-Net Theorem

Haussler-Welzl

For VC dimension $d$, a random sample is a net with high probability.

Striking feature

Size is independent of $|X|$.

Theorem

$|N|=O\!\Big(\tfrac{d}{\varepsilon}\log\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big)$

Printed pp. 231-264The Epsilon-Net Theorem
Haussler and Welzl show that for a range space of VC dimension d, a random sample of size proportional to d over epsilon times the logarithm of one over epsilon is an epsilon-net with high probability. The size depends on dimension and epsilon, never on the ground set size.

Why Random Sampling Works

Double sample

Compare the true sample with a ghost sample.

Symmetrization

Reduce to a finite event on $2m$ points.

Union bound

Polynomial $\pi(2m)$ beats exponential decay.

Proof skeleton

$\Pr[\text{miss}]\le 2\,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(2m)\,2^{-\varepsilon m/2}$

Closure

Polynomial growth is crushed by $2^{-\varepsilon m/2}$.

Printed pp. 231-264Why Random Sampling Works
The proof uses a double-sample and a symmetrization argument. We compare the true sample with a ghost sample, bound the bad event by the polynomial shatter function from Sauer-Shelah, and the union bound closes because polynomial growth is crushed by the exponential decay of the miss probability.

Epsilon-Approximations

Density preserving

Every range keeps its relative weight up to $\varepsilon$.

Stronger than a net

Approximations imply nets, not conversely.

Definition

$\Big|\tfrac{|A\cap R|}{|A|}-\mu(R)\Big|\le\varepsilon\;\;\forall R$

Printed pp. 231-264Epsilon-Approximations
An epsilon-approximation is stronger than a net: it preserves the relative density of every range up to an additive epsilon, not merely nonemptiness. Such samples let us estimate how heavy any range is, which underpins discrepancy bounds and the efficient approximate range counting we meet at the end of the chapter.

Transversals and Piercing

Transversal

A point set meeting every member of a family $\mathcal{F}$.

Piercing number

$\tau(\mathcal{F})$ = smallest transversal size.

Bridge

Nets pierce the heavy part; Helly controls the rest.

Printed pp. 231-264Transversals and Piercing
A transversal, or piercing set, is a set of points meeting every member of a family of convex sets. Epsilon-nets supply transversals for the heavy part of a family, and Helly-type hypotheses control when a small piercing number is guaranteed to exist for the whole collection.

Weak Epsilon-Nets

Relaxation

Net points may lie outside the ground set $X$.

Payoff

Far smaller nets for convex ranges.

Convex weak net

$|N|=f(\varepsilon,d),\quad\text{independent of }|X|$

Printed pp. 231-264Weak Epsilon-Nets
A weak epsilon-net may use points outside the ground set to pierce every heavy convex range. Dropping the membership restriction buys dramatically smaller nets for convex ranges, with bounds independent of the number of points, a cornerstone of selection lemmas and centerpoint-style results.

The Hadwiger-Debrunner $(p,q)$ Problem

Hypothesis

Among every $p$ sets, some $q$ share a common point.

Conclusion

The family has a bounded piercing number.

Alon-Kleinman

$(p,q)\text{ with }q\ge d+1\;\Rightarrow\;\tau(\mathcal{F})\le c(p,q,d)$

Engine

Fractional Helly plus the epsilon-net theorem.

Printed pp. 231-264Hadwiger-Debrunner (p,q)
The Hadwiger-Debrunner problem asks: if among every p convex sets some q have a common point, is the whole family pierced by few points? Alon and Kleinman answered yes, and their proof leans on fractional Helly together with the epsilon-net machinery developed across this chapter.

Application: Range Searching

Query

How many points lie in a halfplane or box?

Compression

Replace data by an $\varepsilon$-approximation.

Approximate count

$\widehat{|R\cap X|}=|X|\cdot\tfrac{|R\cap A|}{|A|}\pm\varepsilon|X|$

Printed pp. 231-264Application: Range Searching
In range searching we preprocess points to answer how many lie in a query halfplane or box. Epsilon-approximations shrink the data while preserving counts, and bounded VC dimension certifies that the approximate structure stays small and provably accurate, the practical dividend of the whole chapter.
