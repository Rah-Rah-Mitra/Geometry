Volumes in High Dimension

Why the unit ball almost disappears as dimension grows

The paradox

The unit ball's volume rises, peaks, then collapses to zero.

Consequences

Mass clusters near boundaries and equators; volume becomes hard to compute.

Printed pp. 311-328Volumes in High Dimension
This chapter spans printed pages 311 through 328 and confronts the strange behaviour of volume as dimension grows. We open with the central paradox: the unit ball, the simplest convex body, has a volume that first rises with dimension and then collapses toward zero, a fact that reshapes all of high-dimensional geometry and intuition.

Volume of the Unit Ball

Closed form

$\operatorname{vol}(B^n) = \dfrac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)}$

Why it vanishes

The numerator $\pi^{n/2}$ grows, but the Gamma factorial grows far faster.

Sample values

$n{=}2{:}\ \pi$, $\ n{=}3{:}\ \tfrac{4}{3}\pi$, $\ n{=}5{:}\ \approx 5.26$, $\ n{=}20{:}\ \approx 0.026$.

Asymptotics

$\operatorname{vol}(B^n)\sim \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n\pi}}\Big(\dfrac{2\pi e}{n}\Big)^{n/2}\to 0$

Printed pp. 311-328Volume of the Unit Ball
The Gamma-function formula for the unit ball volume is the workhorse of the chapter. Students should notice the numerator is bounded relative to the denominator factorial which grows super-exponentially, so the ratio must eventually vanish. We compute a few values to make the rise then fall concrete rather than purely abstract.

Rise, Then Collapse

Non-monotone

Volume increases up to about $n=5$, then decreases for every larger dimension.

Surprising peak

The maximal-volume unit ball lives in dimension five, not in low or high $n$.

Printed pp. 311-328Rise, Then Collapse
Here we plot the volume sequence so the non-monotone shape is unmistakable. The peak near dimension five surprises everyone the first time they see it. Emphasize that the body still contains the same inscribed structure, yet its total measure shrinks faster than any geometric series once dimension passes the maximum.

Cube versus Inscribed Ball

Volume ratio

$\dfrac{\operatorname{vol}(B^n)}{\operatorname{vol}([-1,1]^n)} = \dfrac{\pi^{n/2}}{2^n\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)} \to 0$

Cube wins

The cube keeps volume $2^n$; its inscribed ball touches a vanishing fraction.

Printed pp. 311-328Cube versus Inscribed Ball
The unit cube keeps volume one when normalized in every dimension while its inscribed ball withers away. We contrast the two side by side. The lesson is that the corners of the cube hold essentially all of its mass, and the inscribed ball touches a vanishing fraction of the cube as dimension climbs without bound.

Everything Is Near the Boundary

Shrinking shell

$\dfrac{\operatorname{vol}\big((1-\varepsilon)B^n\big)}{\operatorname{vol}(B^n)} = (1-\varepsilon)^n \to 0$

Thin shell

Almost all volume sits within an $O(1/n)$ shell of the boundary.

Printed pp. 311-328Mass Near the Boundary
Scaling a convex body by a factor slightly below one removes almost all of its volume in high dimension. We derive the one minus epsilon to the n estimate and interpret it carefully: nearly every point of a high-dimensional body lies in a thin shell hugging the boundary, an idea that powers concentration arguments throughout the entire field.

Mass Concentrates at the Equator

Cap bound

$\Pr\big[|x_1| \ge t\big] \le 2\,e^{-n t^2/2}$

Almost orthogonal

Two random unit vectors meet at angle close to $90^\circ$ with high probability.

Printed pp. 311-328Mass Near the Equator
For the sphere, almost all surface area sits in a narrow band around any chosen equator. We sketch the Gaussian-width estimate of the cap measure. This concentration of measure means a random direction is nearly orthogonal to any fixed vector, the geometric engine behind dimension reduction and random projection methods.

Circumscribed versus Inscribed

Sandwich

$rB^n \subseteq K \subseteq RB^n,\qquad \dfrac{R}{r}=\sqrt{n}\ \text{for the cube}$

Exponential gap

The two balls differ in volume by $(\sqrt{n})^n$, ruining crude bounds.

Printed pp. 311-328Circumscribed versus Inscribed
Sandwiching a body between an inscribed and a circumscribed ball gives volume bounds, but in high dimension the gap between the two balls is enormous. We make the radius ratio explicit for the cube and show why crude sandwiching loses an exponential factor, motivating the search for better approximating ellipsoids.

John's Ellipsoid

Theorem (John)

$E \subseteq K \subseteq n\,E,\qquad \sqrt{n}\,E\ \text{if } K=-K$

Best fit

The maximal-volume inscribed ellipsoid $E$ approximates $K$ optimally up to $n$.

Printed pp. 311-328John's Ellipsoid
John's theorem provides the optimal ellipsoid inside a convex body, tight up to a factor of the dimension for general bodies and only the square root of the dimension for symmetric ones. We state the containment chain and explain that this is the best affine-invariant approximation one can guarantee in general.

$\varepsilon$-Nets and Covering Numbers

Volumetric bound

$N(B^n,\varepsilon) \le \Big(1+\tfrac{2}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{n} = \Big(\tfrac{3}{\varepsilon}\Big)^{O(n)}$

Counting idea

Compare the volume of inflated balls to the body to bound net size.

Printed pp. 311-328Epsilon-Nets and Covering
A volumetric counting argument bounds the size of an epsilon-net on the sphere or inside a body. We derive the covering number estimate from the ratio of inflated to original volume. This bound is the combinatorial bridge that turns continuous volume facts into finite, checkable, and algorithmically useful statements.

Computing Volume Is Hard

Exact

Volume of an $H$-polytope is $\#\mathrm{P}$-hard to compute exactly.

Deterministic

No deterministic poly algorithm approximates within an exponential factor.

Randomized escape

$\widehat{V}\in\big[(1-\delta)V,\,(1+\delta)V\big]\ \text{w.h.p. in poly}(n,1/\delta)$

Method

A random walk samples the body; ratios of nested bodies estimate $V$.

Printed pp. 311-328Hardness of Volume
Computing the volume of a polytope given by inequalities is hard: exact volume is sharp-P-hard, and deterministic approximation cannot beat an exponential factor. We outline why this happens, contrasting it with the randomized polynomial-time approximation scheme that uses random walks to sample inside the body.

Large-Volume Polytopes

Facet lower bound

$P\subseteq B^n,\ \operatorname{vol}(P)\ge \tfrac12\operatorname{vol}(B^n)\ \Rightarrow\ \#\text{facets} \ge e^{cn}$

No cheap fit

Approximating the ball by a polytope needs exponentially many facets.

Printed pp. 311-328Large-Volume Polytopes
Few facets cannot capture most of the ball's volume. We sketch the lower bound showing that any polytope approximating the ball to within a constant factor needs exponentially many facets in the dimension. This is the polytope-approximation companion to the covering bound and explains why simple descriptions necessarily fail.

Isoperimetry and Concentration

Isoperimetric inequality

$\operatorname{vol}_{n-1}(\partial K) \ge \operatorname{vol}_{n-1}(\partial B)\ \text{when } \operatorname{vol}(K)=\operatorname{vol}(B)$

Unifying view

Boundary and equator concentration are two faces of isoperimetry.

Printed pp. 311-328Isoperimetry in High Dimension
The isoperimetric inequality says the ball minimizes surface area for a fixed volume, and its high-dimensional form drives concentration. We connect the boundary-mass and equator-mass phenomena to a single isoperimetric principle, then preview how these volume facts feed the later chapters on embeddings and approximation.

Recap and Outlook

Core facts

  • $\operatorname{vol}(B^n)\to 0$ after peaking near $n=5$.
  • Mass hugs the boundary and the equator.

Approximation

  • John's ellipsoid, $\varepsilon$-nets, covering numbers.
  • Volume is $\#\mathrm{P}$-hard, randomization saves it.

Through-line

Every phenomenon is a facet of high-dimensional concentration governed by isoperimetry.

Next
