What Is a Lattice

Definition

Given independent $b_1,\dots,b_d$, the lattice is every integer combination of them.

Discrete subgroup

Closed under addition, with a gap around every point — no accumulation.

Examples

The grid $\mathbb{Z}^d$; skewed bases give slanted but equally regular lattices.

Lattice

$\Lambda = \Big\{ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d} z_i\, b_i : z_i \in \mathbb{Z} \Big\}$

Printed pp. 17-28What Is a Lattice
A lattice is the set of all integer combinations of a fixed basis of independent vectors. It is a discrete subgroup of Euclidean space, infinite yet evenly spaced. The integer grid is the simplest example, but skewed bases give richer tilings that we will measure carefully.

The Fundamental Parallelepiped

Tiling

Translates of one parallelepiped by lattice vectors tile $\mathbb{R}^d$ exactly.

Covolume

Its volume is the lattice determinant $\det\Lambda$ — the density of points.

Determinant

$\det\Lambda = \big|\det[\,b_1\;\cdots\;b_d\,]\big|$

Printed pp. 17-28Fundamental Parallelepiped
Each lattice carves space into translated copies of one fundamental parallelepiped spanned by the basis. Its volume, the determinant, equals the absolute value of the basis matrix determinant. Different bases give the same lattice, and crucially the same determinant, so covolume is a genuine lattice invariant.

The Determinant Is Invariant

Change of basis

Two bases give the same $\Lambda$ iff an integer matrix $U$ relates them.

Unimodular

Such $U$ has $\det U = \pm 1$, so the covolume never changes.

Invariance

$B' = BU,\quad \det U = \pm 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \det\Lambda \text{ fixed}$

Consequence

We may write $\det\Lambda$ for the lattice itself, free of any chosen basis.

Printed pp. 17-28The Determinant Is Invariant
Two bases generate the same lattice exactly when a unimodular integer matrix relates them. Such matrices have determinant plus or minus one, so the covolume is unchanged. This invariance lets us speak of the lattice determinant without referencing any particular basis we happened to choose.

Minkowski's Theorem

Theorem

$\operatorname{vol}(C) > 2^{d}\det\Lambda \;\Rightarrow\; C \cap (\Lambda\setminus\{0\}) \neq \varnothing$

Hypotheses

$C$ convex and symmetric about $0$: if $x \in C$ then $-x \in C$.

Sharp

The cube $(-1,1)^d$ has volume exactly $2^d$ and only the origin — sharpness.

Printed pp. 17-28Minkowski's Theorem
Minkowski's theorem is the chapter's centerpiece. If a convex body is symmetric about the origin and its volume exceeds two to the dimension times the lattice determinant, it must contain a nonzero lattice point. Convexity and symmetry are both essential; dropping either breaks the conclusion immediately.

Proof: Folding modulo $2\Lambda$

Halve

Set $C' = \tfrac12 C$. Then $\operatorname{vol}(C') > \det\Lambda = \operatorname{vol}(\mathbb{R}^d/2\Lambda)$.

Pigeonhole

Folding $C'$ onto the torus $\mathbb{R}^d/2\Lambda$ forces two points to overlap.

Conclude

Their difference $x-y \in 2\Lambda$ is nonzero; $\tfrac{x-y}{2} \in C$ by symmetry and convexity.

Printed pp. 17-28Proof by Folding mod 2L
Scale the body by one half and fold it onto the torus modulo two times the lattice. The folded volume exceeds the cell, so by pigeonhole two distinct points collide. Their difference is a nonzero lattice point, and symmetry plus convexity place it inside the original body.

Blichfeldt's Lemma and General Lattices

Blichfeldt

$\operatorname{vol}(S) > \det\Lambda \Rightarrow \exists\, x\neq y \in S,\; x-y\in\Lambda$

Engine of the proof

This is the pigeonhole made precise; Minkowski is the symmetric, convex corollary.

Any lattice

A linear map $T$ sends $\mathbb{Z}^d$ onto $\Lambda$ and scales volumes by $\det\Lambda$.

General statement

So Minkowski holds verbatim for every lattice, not only the integer grid.

Printed pp. 17-28Blichfeldt and Generality
Blichfeldt's lemma underlies the folding step: any set of volume exceeding the determinant has two points differing by a lattice vector. Minkowski's theorem follows for arbitrary lattices, not just the integer grid, because we may transform any lattice to the standard one by a linear map.

Successive Minima

Definition

$\lambda_k$ is the smallest $t$ with $tK$ containing $k$ independent lattice vectors.

First theorem, refined

$\lambda_1$ recovers the single short vector; the full chain $\lambda_1\le\dots\le\lambda_d$ says more.

Minkowski's second theorem

$\dfrac{2^{d}}{d!}\det\Lambda \le \lambda_1\cdots\lambda_d \cdot \operatorname{vol}(K) \le 2^{d}\det\Lambda$

Printed pp. 17-28Successive Minima
The successive minima record the smallest dilations at which a symmetric body captures one, two, up through d independent lattice directions. Minkowski's second theorem bounds their product against the determinant, refining the first theorem from a single short vector to a whole independent system.

Application: Sums of Two Squares

Theorem

$p \equiv 1 \pmod 4 \;\Rightarrow\; p = a^2 + b^2$

Lattice trick

Choose $q$ with $q^2\equiv -1\pmod p$; take $\Lambda=\{(x,y): y\equiv qx\}$, $\det\Lambda=p$.

Apply Minkowski

A disk of area $> 4p$ catches a nonzero $(a,b)\in\Lambda$ with $a^2+b^2 < 2p$.

Finish

Then $a^2+b^2\equiv 0\pmod p$ and lies in $(0,2p)$, forcing $a^2+b^2=p$.

Printed pp. 17-28Sums of Two Squares
A classic payoff: every prime congruent to one modulo four is a sum of two squares. Build a lattice from a square root of minus one modulo the prime, apply Minkowski to a disk, and the captured lattice point furnishes exactly the two integers whose squares sum to the prime.

Application: Diophantine Approximation

Simultaneous approximation

$\Big|\alpha_i - \dfrac{p_i}{q}\Big| < \dfrac{1}{q\,Q^{1/d}},\quad 1\le q \le Q$

Setup

Build a thin symmetric box capturing the residuals $q\alpha_i - p_i$.

Apply Minkowski

Tune the box to volume $> 2^{d+1}$; a nonzero lattice point appears inside it.

Read off

That point's coordinates are the common denominator $q$ and numerators $p_i$.

Printed pp. 17-28Diophantine Approximation
Minkowski's theorem proves simultaneous Diophantine approximation: any reals admit integer numerators sharing one denominator that approximate them all well. The proof picks a thin symmetric box of the right volume, and the guaranteed nonzero lattice point inside it encodes the simultaneous rational approximation we wanted.

Short Vectors: Existence vs. Algorithm

Minkowski bound

$\lambda_1(\Lambda) \le 2\,(\det\Lambda)^{1/d}\sqrt{d}/\sqrt{\pi}\,$ (order of magnitude)

Existence

A short nonzero vector is guaranteed by applying Minkowski to a ball.

Computation

Finding the shortest vector (SVP) is NP-hard as the dimension grows.

Why it matters

This existence-versus-search gap underpins lattice-based, post-quantum cryptography.

Printed pp. 17-28Short Vectors and Hardness
Minkowski guarantees a lattice vector no longer than a determinant-based bound, but finding the shortest vector is computationally hard in high dimension. This gap between existence and algorithm powers lattice-based cryptography, where short-vector difficulty secures schemes believed safe even against quantum adversaries.

Recap and Outlook

Definitions

Lattice as integer span; fundamental cell; the invariant determinant $\det\Lambda$.

Main theorem

Symmetric convex $C$ with $\operatorname{vol}(C)>2^d\det\Lambda$ holds a nonzero point.

Method

Folding mod $2\Lambda$ — pigeonhole through Blichfeldt's lemma.

Harvest

Sums of two squares, Diophantine approximation, guaranteed short vectors.

Printed pp. 17-28Recap and Outlook
We defined lattices, measured them by the invariant determinant, proved Minkowski's theorem by folding modulo two times the lattice, and harvested number-theoretic fruit. The themes of covolume, convex symmetry, and pigeonhole recur throughout discrete geometry and feed directly into later counting and packing chapters.
