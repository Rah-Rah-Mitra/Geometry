A High-Dimensional Surprise

Low dimensions mislead

A picture of $S^2$ suggests measure spreads evenly over caps and bands alike.

High dimensions concentrate

As $n\to\infty$ the mass collapses toward any single equator.

Consequence

Random points are nearly orthogonal and nearly equatorial.

Mass near the equator

$\mathbb{P}\big[\,|x_n| \ge t\,\big] \le e^{-t^2 n/2}$

Reading

Fix a width $t$: the fraction of points outside the band decays exponentially in $n$.

Printed pp. 329-354A High-Dimensional Surprise
Intuition trained in two or three dimensions fails badly on the sphere of large dimension. Almost all of the surface measure sits within a thin band around any equator, and the mass spreads ever closer to it as the dimension grows. This counterintuitive packing of measure is the engine of the chapter.

The Sphere and Its Measure

Object

Unit sphere $S^{n-1}=\{x:\|x\|_2=1\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Measure

Rotation-invariant probability measure $\sigma$, with $\sigma(S^{n-1})=1$.

Metric

Geodesic distance $\rho(x,y)$ along great circles.

t-enlargement

$A_t = \{\,x : \rho(x,A) \le t\,\}$

Why probability?

Stating results as probabilities of random points matches the concentration estimates ahead.

Printed pp. 329-354The Sphere and Its Measure
We fix the unit sphere with its rotation-invariant probability measure and the geodesic metric. Working with a normalized measure lets us speak of the probability that a random point lands in a region, which is the natural language for the concentration estimates that follow throughout the chapter.

Levy Isoperimetric Inequality

Extremal sets

Among sets of fixed measure, spherical caps minimize the enlarged measure $\sigma(A_t)$.

Half-sphere case

If $\sigma(A)\ge \tfrac12$, compare $A_t$ against the cap of the same measure.

Theorem (Levy)

$\sigma(A)\ge\tfrac12 \;\Rightarrow\; \sigma(S^{n-1}\setminus A_t)\le e^{-t^2 n/2}$

Printed pp. 329-354Levy Isoperimetric Inequality
The isoperimetric inequality on the sphere says that among all sets of a given measure, spherical caps have the smallest neighborhood enlargement. Comparing any half-sphere to its t-enlargement gives the sharp bound that drives concentration: the complement of a halved set shrinks exponentially in the enlargement radius.

The Equatorial Band Carries the Mass

Choose a hemisphere

Let $A$ be a closed hemisphere, so $\sigma(A)=\tfrac12$.

Its enlargement

$A_t$ is a band reaching $t$ past the equator on both sides.

Conclusion

The two polar caps outside the band hold tiny measure.

Band estimate

$\sigma\big(\{x:|\langle x,e\rangle|>t\}\big)\le 2\,e^{-t^2 n/2}$

Printed pp. 329-354The Equatorial Band Carries the Mass
Taking the set to be a closed hemisphere, its t-neighborhood is a band around the equator. Isoperimetry forces the measure outside that band below exp of minus t squared n over two. So a band of fixed angular width already captures essentially all measure once the dimension is large.

Lipschitz Functions Concentrate

Setup

$f:S^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|\le \rho(x,y)$.

Centering

Let $m_f$ be a median of $f$.

Idea

The sublevel set $\{f\le m_f\}$ has measure $\ge\tfrac12$; apply the band bound.

Levy's lemma

$\sigma\big(\{\,|f - m_f| > t\,\}\big)\le 2\,e^{-t^2 n/2}$

Meaning

A non-stretching function is essentially constant on the sphere of large $n$.

Printed pp. 329-354Lipschitz Functions Concentrate
A function that does not stretch distances cannot vary much where almost all the measure lives. Applying the band estimate to the sublevel and superlevel sets of a one-Lipschitz function shows it stays within t of its median except on a set of measure at most twice exp of minus t squared n over two.

Median versus Mean

Two centers

The median $m_f$ and the average $\mathbb{E}f$ are both natural reference values.

They nearly agree

Integrating the exponential tail bounds their gap.

Closeness

$\big|\,\mathbb{E}f - m_f\,\big| \le \dfrac{C}{\sqrt{n}}$

Use

We may state concentration around either center; the choice is a matter of convenience.

Caution

The constant $C$ is absolute and independent of $f$ and of $n$.

Printed pp. 329-354Median versus Mean
Because the deviation tails are exponentially small, the median and the expectation of a Lipschitz function differ by only a constant over the square root of the dimension. This lets us state concentration around either centering value, a convenience used repeatedly when estimating averages of random projections.

Gaussian Concentration

Standard Gaussian

$\gamma_n$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with independent $N(0,1)$ coordinates.

Norm concentrates

$\|g\|_2 \approx \sqrt{n}$ with sub-Gaussian fluctuations.

Link to sphere

Normalizing $g/\|g\|$ recovers the uniform $\sigma$ on $S^{n-1}$.

Gaussian Levy lemma

$\gamma_n\big(\{\,|f-\mathbb{E}f|>t\,\}\big)\le 2\,e^{-t^2/2}$

Why useful

Independent coordinates make moment and tail calculations clean and dimension-free.

Printed pp. 329-354Gaussian Concentration
The same phenomenon appears for the standard Gaussian measure in high dimension, where a one-Lipschitz function concentrates around its mean with a clean dimension-free sub-Gaussian tail. Gaussian concentration is often easier to apply because the coordinates are independent and the norm concentrates near the square root of n.

Almost Spherical Sections

Symmetric convex body

$K=-K$ defines a norm $\|x\|_K$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Round section

A subspace $F$ gives an almost-spherical section if $\|\cdot\|_K$ is near constant on $F\cap S$.

Eccentricity $\varepsilon$

Smaller $\varepsilon$ means a rounder slice.

$(1+\varepsilon)$-spherical

$a\|x\|_2 \le \|x\|_K \le (1+\varepsilon)\,a\|x\|_2,\quad x\in F$

Printed pp. 329-354Almost Spherical Sections
A central section of a symmetric convex body is almost spherical when its norm is squeezed between two close multiples of the Euclidean norm. The eccentricity epsilon measures how round the section is. The goal is to guarantee such a section of large dimension inside an arbitrary body.

Dvoretzky's Theorem

Statement

Every $n$-dimensional symmetric convex body has an almost-spherical central section.

Dimension grows

The round slice has dimension $k \sim c\,\dfrac{\log n}{\varepsilon^2}$.

Universality

No matter how distorted $K$ is, a round slice of growing dimension survives.

Theorem (Dvoretzky)

$\dim F \ge c\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\log n \;\Rightarrow\; F\cap K \text{ is } (1+\varepsilon)\text{-spherical}$

Heuristic

$\|\cdot\|_K$ is Lipschitz on $S^{n-1}$, so it concentrates and is flat on a random subspace.

Printed pp. 329-354Dvoretzky's Theorem
Dvoretzky's theorem states that every n-dimensional symmetric convex body has a central section of dimension proportional to log n over epsilon squared that is epsilon-close to a Euclidean ball. The dimension of the round slice grows without bound as the ambient dimension increases, no matter how distorted the body is.

Proof Strategy via Nets

Step 1 — Random subspace

Draw $F$ of dimension $k$ uniformly at random.

Step 2 — Pointwise control

Concentration keeps $\|x\|_K$ near its median for each fixed $x\in F\cap S$.

Step 3 — $\varepsilon$-net

Pick a finite net $\mathcal{N}$ of size $\le (3/\varepsilon)^k$ on the unit sphere of $F$.

Union bound

$\mathbb{P}[\text{bad}] \le |\mathcal{N}|\cdot 2e^{-t^2 n/2} < 1$

Printed pp. 329-354Proof Strategy via Nets
The proof picks a random subspace and shows the norm is nearly constant on its unit sphere. Concentration controls the norm at each point, an epsilon-net discretizes the sphere of the subspace, and a union bound over the net keeps the failure probability small while the net size stays exponential in the subspace dimension.

Synthesis and Takeaways

Concentration

Lipschitz functions on $S^{n-1}$ are nearly constant: deviation tails are $\le 2e^{-t^2 n/2}$.

Isoperimetry

Levy's inequality and the equatorial band drive every estimate.

Dvoretzky

Random sections of dimension $\sim \log n/\varepsilon^2$ are almost round.

One-line summary

$\text{high } n \;\Rightarrow\; \text{rigidity, not chaos}$

Looking ahead

These tools reappear in metric embeddings, random projections, and the probabilistic method.

Printed pp. 329-354Synthesis and Takeaways
Concentration of measure converts high dimension from an obstacle into structure: Lipschitz functions flatten, mass hugs equatorial bands, and random low-dimensional sections of convex bodies become almost round. These ideas reappear across embeddings, random projections, and the probabilistic method throughout discrete geometry.
