A combinatorial gap nobody has closed

The question

How many subsets can one line cut off from $n$ points?

The tension

A cube-root upper bound against a root-log lower bound.

Printed pp. 265-288Attempts to Count k-Sets
This chapter, spanning printed pages 265 to 288, asks a deceptively simple combinatorial question: how many distinct subsets of a planar point set can a line separate off? We frame the chapter as a gap between an upper bound and a lower bound that nobody has yet closed.

What Is a k-Set

Definition

A size-$k$ subset cut off by an open halfplane.

Counted once

Many lines may give the same subset; it counts a single time.

k-set

$S' = S \cap h, \quad |S'| = k, \; h \text{ an open halfplane}$

Goal

Count distinct $S'$, not separating lines.

Printed pp. 265-288What Is a k-Set
A k-set is a size-k subset of our points cut off by some open halfplane. We stress that the same k points may be reachable by many lines, but it counts once. The count is over distinct subsets, not over the separating lines themselves.

Halving Lines and Edges

Halving case

When $k = \tfrac{n}{2}$ the subset and its complement match in size.

Halving edges

Directed point pairs that split the rest evenly.

Printed pp. 265-288Halving Lines and Edges
When k is half of n, the separated subset and its complement have equal size, giving halving lines. Combinatorially these become halving edges: directed point pairs splitting the rest evenly. Bounding their number is the cleanest model problem of the whole chapter.

Duality: k-Levels in Arrangements

Point to line

Each point dualizes to a line in the arrangement.

k-level

Points lying above exactly $k$ of the dual lines.

Printed pp. 265-288Duality: k-Levels in Arrangements
Point-line duality turns each point into a line and each k-set boundary into a vertex at level k. The k-level is the polyline of points lying above exactly k of the dual lines, so counting k-sets becomes counting the complexity of one level.

The Upper-Bound Problem

The quantity

$a_k(n)$ is the max number of $k$-sets over all $n$-point sets.

Trivial bound

Quadratic in $n$; we want the $k$-exponent below one.

Target

$a_k(n) = \max_{|S|=n} \#\{ k\text{-sets of } S \}$

Why it matters

Controls the worst case for halving lines.

Printed pp. 265-288The Upper-Bound Problem
We define a_k(n) as the maximum number of k-sets over all n-point configurations. The trivial bound is quadratic; the chapter's challenge is to push the exponent on k well below one, which controls the worst case for halving lines too.

Crossing Number Tool

The inequality

Dense graphs force cubically many edge crossings.

The plan

Build a graph from $k$-set edges, bound its crossings.

Crossing number

$\operatorname{cr}(G) \ge \dfrac{e^3}{64\,n^2}, \quad e \ge 4n$

Inversion

A crossing cap turns into an edge-count cap.

Printed pp. 265-288Crossing Number Tool
The crossing number inequality says any drawing of a graph with many edges relative to vertices forces a cubic surplus of crossings. We build a graph from k-set edges, bound its crossings geometrically, and invert the inequality to limit edge count.

The Lovasz Lemma

Statement

Any fixed line stabs the halving edges only linearly often.

Role

This bounded crossing is the input the machine needs.

Printed pp. 265-288The Lovasz Lemma
Lovasz observed that any fixed line meets the collection of halving edges only a linear number of times. This bounded-crossing property is exactly the geometric input the crossing-number machine needs, and it converts a counting question into an inequality about line stabs.

Best Known Upper Bound

Result

Combining Lovasz with crossing numbers caps the count.

Status

Still the best general planar estimate decades on.

Planar upper bound

$a_k(n) = O\!\big( n\,(k+1)^{1/3} \big)$

Cube root

The $1/3$ exponent is the prize of the argument.

Printed pp. 265-288Best Known Upper Bound
Feeding the Lovasz stabbing bound into the crossing-number inequality yields the celebrated planar bound of order n times the cube root of k plus one. This remains, decades later, the best known general upper estimate for the number of k-sets in the plane.

The Lower Bound

Constructions

$k$-sets really can exceed linear growth.

Consequence

The truth is not merely $O(n)$.

Lower bound

$a_k(n) = n \cdot 2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log k})}$

Trapped

The real rate lies between this and the cube-root ceiling.

Printed pp. 265-288The Lower Bound
Constructions show k-sets really can be superlinear. The best lower bound multiplies n by two raised to a slowly growing root-log-k term, proving the truth is not merely linear. The genuine growth rate sits trapped somewhere between this and the cube-root ceiling.

Halving Lines Special Case

Hardest instance

Set $k = \tfrac{n}{2}$ to isolate halving lines.

The gap

Upper $n^{4/3}$ versus a mildly superlinear lower bound.

Halving bound

$h(n) = O\!\big( n^{4/3} \big)$

Stakes

Closing this gap nearly resolves all of $k$-set counting.

Printed pp. 265-288Halving Lines Special Case
Setting k to n over two isolates halving lines, the hardest instance. Here the upper bound becomes n to the four-thirds and the lower bound stays mildly superlinear. Closing this single gap would essentially resolve the entire k-set counting problem.

Higher Dimensions

Same question

$k$-sets defined by halfspaces in $\mathbb{R}^d$.

Weaker control

The gap to constructions widens with $d$.

Dimension d

$a_k(n) = O\!\big( n^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} k^{\lceil d/2 \rceil - \varepsilon} \big)$

Open

No clean high-dimensional cube-root analogue.

Printed pp. 265-288Higher Dimensions
In dimension d the same questions return with weaker control. Upper bounds grow with the dimension in the exponent and the gap to known constructions widens sharply. The planar cube-root argument has no clean high-dimensional analogue, leaving the area wide open.

Recap and Open Problem

Upper

$O(n (k+1)^{1/3})$ via crossing numbers and Lovasz.

Lower

$n\,2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log k})}$ from explicit constructions.

Printed pp. 265-288Recap and Open Problem
We close by restating the standing gap: cube-root upper bound against root-log lower bound. The exact order of the maximum number of halving lines is one of the most stubborn open problems in combinatorial geometry, and this chapter maps the terrain around it.
