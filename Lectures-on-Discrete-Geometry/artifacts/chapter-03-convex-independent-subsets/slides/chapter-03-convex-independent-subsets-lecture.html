Points in Convex Position

Definition

$x \notin \operatorname{conv}(S \setminus \{x\})\ \ \forall x \in S$

Convex independent

No point lies in the hull of the others; each is a vertex.

Affine invariance

The property survives any invertible affine map of the plane.

Printed pp. 29-40Points in Convex Position
A finite set is convex independent when none of its points lies in the convex hull of the others, so every point is a genuine vertex of the polygon they span. This vertex condition is the exact object Erdos and Szekeres count, and it survives any affine map of the plane.

The Erdos-Szekeres Theorem

Theorem

$\exists\, ES(n):$ any $\geq ES(n)$ points in general position contain $n$ in convex position.

General position

No three points collinear, so every orientation test is decisive.

Finiteness

The threshold $ES(n)$ exists for every $n$ — a Ramsey-type guarantee.

Small cases

$ES(3)=3$, $ES(4)=5$ (the happy-ending pentagon), $ES(5)=9$.

Goal

Bound $ES(n)$ from above and below.

Printed pp. 29-40The Erdos-Szekeres Theorem
For each target size we seek the threshold count above which every general-position set must hide a convex polygon with that many vertices. Generality rules out three collinear points so orientations stay decisive. The chapter proves this threshold is finite and bounds it from both directions.

Cups and Caps

Cup

Points ordered by $x$ whose slopes increase — a convex arc opening upward.

Cap

Points ordered by $x$ whose slopes decrease — opening downward.

Idea

Sort by $x$; cups and caps are monotone subsequences of slopes.

Printed pp. 29-40Cups and Caps
The proof replaces hard convex polygons with two ordered chains. A cup is a sequence whose slopes increase, bending upward, while a cap has decreasing slopes bending downward. Sorting points by x-coordinate turns the geometric question into monotone subsequence bookkeeping that recursion can attack cleanly.

The Cup-Cap Lemma

Lemma

$f(k,\ell)=\dbinom{k+\ell-4}{k-2}+1$

Meaning

Any $f(k,\ell)$ points in general position contain a $k$-cup or an $\ell$-cap.

Recursion

$f(k,\ell)=f(k-1,\ell)+f(k,\ell-1)-1$

Pascal echo

The split mirrors the binomial identity exactly.

Base

$f(2,\ell)=f(k,2)=1$: two points are both a cup and a cap.

Printed pp. 29-40The Cup-Cap Lemma
The central lemma counts points needed to force a k-cup or an l-cap. Its bound is one plus the binomial of k plus l minus four choosing k minus two. The recursion splits points by whether they end a long cup or begin a long cap, mirroring Pascal's identity exactly.

The Recursion Engine

Label points

Give each point the length of the longest cup ending there.

Pigeonhole

If no label reaches $k$, many points share a label.

Read a cap

Equal-label neighbors force a slope drop, building an $\ell$-cap.

Printed pp. 29-40The Recursion Engine
Each point gets a label: the longest cup ending there. If no label reaches k we read a long cap from points sharing a label, because between equal-label neighbors the slope must drop. This pigeonhole between cup growth and cap formation drives the binomial induction to its conclusion.

From Chains to Polygons

Decomposition

Any convex $n$-gon splits into an upper cap and a lower cup along its $x$-extremes.

Upper bound

$ES(n)\le \dbinom{2n-4}{n-2}+1 \approx 4^{\,n}$

Printed pp. 29-40From Chains to Polygons
A convex n-gon contains both a cup and a cap, so forcing one chain of size n forces convex position. Combining the lemma gives an upper threshold of one plus the binomial of two n minus four choosing n minus two points, roughly four to the n, guaranteeing n convex-independent points.

The Lower Bound

Construction

$ES(n)\ \ge\ 2^{\,n-2}+1$

Recursive clusters

Combine two smaller sets free of convex $n$-gons, one steep cup-like, one shallow.

Tightness

Walls $2^{n-2}$ and $4^n$ bracket the true threshold.

Printed pp. 29-40The Lower Bound
To show the threshold is genuinely exponential we build large sets with no convex n-gon. Stacking carefully scaled cup-shaped clusters, each tiny relative to the previous, prevents any long cup or cap from spanning groups. This yields sets of size two to the n minus two avoiding convex n-gons.

Empty Convex Polygons

Stronger demand

$\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{conv}(P)) \cap S = \varnothing$

Empty $k$-gon

A convex polygon whose interior contains no other point of $S$.

Small $k$ forced

Five points always yield an empty quadrilateral; large sets force empty pentagons.

The surprise

Empty heptagons ($k=7$) need not appear at all.

Why it matters

Empty-polygon Ramsey behaviour breaks from ordinary Erdos-Szekeres.

Printed pp. 29-40Empty Convex Polygons
A sharper demand asks for an empty convex polygon, one whose interior holds no other point of the set. Five points always give an empty quadrilateral and large sets force empty pentagons, but Horton discovered that empty heptagons can be avoided forever by special configurations.

Horton Sets

Recursive build

$H = H_{\text{even}} \cup H_{\text{odd}},\ \ H_{\text{up}} \gg H_{\text{low}}$

Split and lift

Two scaled copies, one placed high, one low, interleaved in $x$.

Theorem

No seven points of a Horton set form an empty convex heptagon.

Printed pp. 29-40Horton Sets
Horton sets are built recursively by splitting into a high part and a low part, each a scaled Horton set, interleaved in x so neither can be enclosed emptily by the other. The key property is that no seven points of a Horton set form an empty convex heptagon, defeating naive expectations.

Why Horton Sets Defeat Heptagons

Shielding

Any cup in the lower copy is pierced by an upper point sitting inside it.

Symmetry

Any cap in the upper copy is pierced by a lower point — by construction.

Consequence

Every candidate empty heptagon contains a witness point, so none stays empty.

Printed pp. 29-40Why Horton Sets Defeat Heptagons
The defeating mechanism is that any cup in the lower half is shielded by a point of the upper half sitting inside it, and symmetrically for caps. This interleaving destroys every candidate empty heptagon, so empty-polygon Ramsey behaviour diverges sharply from the ordinary Erdos-Szekeres guarantee.

Synthesis and Invariants

Definition layer

Convex independence: every point a vertex, $x\notin\operatorname{conv}(S\setminus\{x\})$.

Bound layer

Cup-cap recursion brackets $ES(n)$ between $2^{n-2}$ and $4^{n}$.

Gap layer

Horton sets show empty heptagons are not forced.

Checkable quantities

$|\,\text{hull}\,|,\quad \text{cup length},\quad \mathbb{1}[\text{empty}]$

Lab habit

Perturb one point; watch hull size, longest cup, or emptiness flip.

Printed pp. 29-40Synthesis and Invariants
We close by aligning the three layers: the convex-independence definition, the cup-cap recursion bounding the threshold between exponential walls, and Horton sets exposing the empty-polygon gap. Each invariant, hull size, cup length, and emptiness, is a checkable quantity the notebook turns into executable diagrams and counts.
