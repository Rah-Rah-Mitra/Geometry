What Is a Lower Envelope

Definition

The pointwise minimum of a family of functions.

Complexity

The number of maximal arcs where one function wins.

Lower envelope

$E(x) = \min_{1 \le i \le n} f_i(x)$

Breakpoints

Each switch of the winning function adds one arc.

Printed pp. 165-194What Is a Lower Envelope
Given n continuous functions on the line, their lower envelope is the function returning the smallest value at each abscissa. Geometrically it is a thick boundary that hugs whichever curve currently sits lowest, and its complexity is the number of maximal arcs where one fixed function wins.

Segments and a First Bound

Setup

$n$ line segments; two segments cross at most once.

Question

Worst-case number of envelope breakpoints?

Surprise

Not quadratic — the truth is almost linear.

Printed pp. 165-194Segments and a First Bound
For n line segments any two cross at most once, yet the envelope can still break into many arcs because segments start and stop. We want the worst-case number of breakpoints as a function of n, and naive counting suggesting a quadratic answer turns out to be far too pessimistic.

Davenport-Schinzel Sequences

Encode the envelope

Write the index of the lowest function as $x$ increases.

Rule 1

No two equal symbols are adjacent.

Rule 2

No alternation $a\,b\,a\,b\dots$ of length $s+2$.

DS sequence of order s

$u = u_1 u_2 \cdots u_m, \quad u_i \ne u_{i+1}$

Reading

The sequence records who wins, in order.

Printed pp. 165-194Davenport-Schinzel Sequences
Encode the envelope by writing down which function is lowest as x increases, producing a sequence over an n-letter alphabet. No two equal letters sit adjacent, and crucially no alternation of length s plus two appears. These constraints define a Davenport-Schinzel sequence of order s.

The Forbidden Alternation

Banned pattern

A long alternation $a\,b\,a\,b\,a$ cannot appear.

Geometric reason

Two bounded-degree curves cross only a few times.

Printed pp. 165-194The Forbidden Alternation
The defining ban is on a subsequence a b a b a of the prescribed length. Two functions of bounded degree cross only a few times, so the lowest role cannot ping-pong between them too often. This is exactly the combinatorial fingerprint that two curves intersect at most s times.

The Order Captures Crossings

Meaning of $s$

Max times two functions swap who is lower.

Examples

Segments: $s=3$. Parabolas: $s=4$. Degree $d$: $s=d$.

Order from geometry

$s = \max_{i \ne j}\,\#\{x : f_i(x) = f_j(x)\}$

Engine

Geometry becomes a forbidden-pattern condition.

Printed pp. 165-194Order Captures Crossings
The order parameter s equals the maximum number of times two of the underlying functions can swap who is lower. Line segments give order three, parabolas order four, and degree-d polynomials order d. Translating geometry into a forbidden pattern is the whole engine of the chapter.

The Length Bound $\lambda_s(n)$

Definition

$\lambda_s(n)$ = max length of an order-$s$ DS sequence on $n$ symbols.

Headline

Almost linear — only barely superlinear in general.

Near-linear bound

$\lambda_s(n) = n \cdot 2^{O(\alpha(n)^{s})}$

Inverse Ackermann

$\alpha(n)$ grows slower than any iterated logarithm.

Printed pp. 165-194The Length Bound
Let lambda_s of n be the maximum length of a Davenport-Schinzel sequence of order s on n symbols. The headline result is that lambda_s of n is almost linear: linear for small orders and only barely superlinear in general, with the inverse Ackermann function controlling the gap.
Lectures on Discrete GeometryChapter 7

Why Near-Linear

Small orders

$\lambda_1(n)=n$, $\lambda_2(n)=2n-1$.

Order three

$\lambda_3(n)=\Theta(n\,\alpha(n))$ — barely above linear.

Printed pp. 165-194Why Near-Linear
Order one and two sequences have length at most n and 2n minus one. Order three already needs the inverse Ackermann function, giving length n times alpha of n. The slow growth means envelopes are essentially as simple as a single function up to a near-constant factor.

Envelope Complexity Theorem

Bridge

Each envelope arc is one symbol of a DS sequence.

Consequence

The length bound becomes an arc-count bound.

Theorem

$|E| = \lambda_s(n)$ for $n$ curves crossing $\le s$ times

Tightness

Both upper and lower bounds match $\lambda_s(n)$.

Printed pp. 165-194Envelope Complexity Theorem
The bridge theorem states the complexity of the lower envelope of n curves pairwise crossing at most s times is exactly lambda of s comma n. Combinatorics and geometry coincide: counting forbidden patterns counts envelope arcs, so the near-linear bound transfers directly to the picture on the line.

Computing the Envelope

Divide

Split the $n$ functions into two halves.

Conquer

Recursively build each half's envelope.

Merge

Sweep left to right, keep the lower piece.

Running time

$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(\lambda_s(n))$

Result

$O(\lambda_s(n)\log n)$ — near-linear.

Printed pp. 165-194Computing the Envelope
A divide and conquer scheme splits the functions into two halves, recursively computes each envelope, then merges the two by sweeping left to right and taking the lower piece. The merge cost is proportional to the combined envelope length, so the total running time is near-linear times a logarithm.

Recap and Outlook

What we proved

  • Envelope complexity equals a DS sequence length.
  • $\lambda_s(n)$ is near-linear.
  • Envelopes compute in $O(\lambda_s(n)\log n)$.

Outlook

  • Upper envelopes by symmetry.
  • Surface patches in higher dimensions.
  • Single cells and zones in arrangements.
Takeaway

geometry $\;\to\;$ forbidden pattern $\;\to\;$ $\lambda_s(n)$

Printed pp. 165-194Recap and Outlook
We turned envelope complexity into a forbidden-pattern count, proved the near-linear lambda_s of n bound, and connected it to fast algorithms and motion planning. Higher dimensional envelopes of surfaces extend the story, where the analogous bounds grow harder but the Davenport-Schinzel philosophy still guides the way.
