V-Polytopes and H-Polytopes

Vertex description

Start from points, build the smallest convex set containing them.

Halfspace description

Start from inequalities, keep what every constraint allows.

V-polytope

$P = \operatorname{conv}(S),\ \ |S| < \infty$

H-polytope

$P = \{x : Ax \le b\}\ \text{bounded}$

Trade-off

One form is easy to store; the other is easy to optimize over.

Printed pp. 77-124V-Polytopes and H-Polytopes
A V-polytope is the convex hull of finitely many points; an H-polytope is a bounded intersection of finitely many halfspaces. Each definition is computationally natural in a different setting, and the chapter's first job is to argue that the two definitions describe exactly the same family of objects.

The Equivalence Theorem

Theorem

A set $P \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is a V-polytope if and only if it is a (bounded) H-polytope.

Two directions

Hull of vertices $\to$ inequalities; project inequalities $\to$ vertices.

Tool

Fourier-Motzkin elimination removes variables from a halfspace system.

Cost

Either conversion may grow the description exponentially.

Printed pp. 77-124The Equivalence Theorem
The main structural fact is that bounded H-polytopes and V-polytopes coincide. Fourier-Motzkin elimination projects halfspace systems down, while taking the hull of vertices reverses the passage. The equivalence is non-trivial because either conversion can blow up the description size exponentially in the dimension.

Faces and the Face Lattice

Definition

$F = P \cap \{x : c^\top x = \max_P c^\top x\}$

Vertices, edges, facets

Faces of dimension $0$, $1$, and $d{-}1$.

Graded lattice

Ordered by inclusion, with $\varnothing$ and $P$ as bounds.

Printed pp. 77-124Faces and the Face Lattice
A face is the set where a valid linear functional attains its maximum over the polytope. Faces are themselves polytopes, ordered by inclusion into a graded lattice with the empty set at the bottom and the whole polytope at the top, recording every combinatorial incidence between vertices, edges, and facets.

The f-vector and Euler's Relation

f-vector

$f(P) = (f_0, f_1, \dots, f_{d-1})$

Euler-Poincare

$\sum_{i=0}^{d-1} (-1)^i f_i = 1 - (-1)^d$

3-polytopes

$f_0 - f_1 + f_2 = 2$

Printed pp. 77-124The f-vector
The f-vector counts faces by dimension, from vertices up to facets. Euler's relation ties the alternating sum to a constant, constraining which integer vectors can occur. For three-polytopes the relation reduces to vertices minus edges plus facets equals two, the classical Euler formula illustrated by the cube here.

Polarity and Duality

Polar dual

$P^{\!*} = \{y : \langle x,y\rangle \le 1\ \forall x \in P\}$

Lattice reversal

$k$-faces of $P$ become $(d{-}1{-}k)$-faces of $P^{*}$.

Vertices ↔ facets

The cube and octahedron are polar duals.

Printed pp. 77-124Polarity and Duality
The polar of a polytope containing the origin swaps the roles of vertices and facets while reversing the face lattice. The cube and the octahedron are polar duals in three dimensions, as is the simplex with itself. Polarity converts V-descriptions to H-descriptions and back very cleanly.

Simple and Simplicial Polytopes

Simplicial

Every facet is a simplex; every facet has exactly $d$ vertices.

Simple

Every vertex lies on exactly $d$ facets.

Duality bridge

$P \ \text{simplicial} \iff P^{*}\ \text{simple}$

Printed pp. 77-124Simple and Simplicial Polytopes
A simplicial polytope has every facet a simplex; a simple polytope has every vertex meeting exactly d facets. Polarity exchanges these two classes, so a theorem about simplicial polytopes immediately yields a dual statement about simple ones, effectively doubling the value of each result we prove.

Simplex, Cube, Cross-Polytope

Simplex $\Delta^d$

$f_k = \binom{d+1}{k+1}$, self-dual.

Cube $[0,1]^d$

$f_k = 2^{d-k}\binom{d}{k}$

Cross-polytope

Dual to the cube; $2d$ vertices.

Printed pp. 77-124Standard Examples
The simplex, the cube, and the cross-polytope are the canonical examples. The simplex is self-dual, while the cube and cross-polytope are polar duals. Their f-vectors follow clean binomial formulas, giving us reliable test cases against which to check every general theorem stated in this chapter.

Cyclic Polytopes and the Moment Curve

Moment curve

$\gamma(t) = (t, t^2, \dots, t^d)$

Neighborly

Every $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor$ vertices span a face.

Gale evenness

A purely combinatorial facet criterion.

Printed pp. 77-124Cyclic Polytopes
Points on the moment curve generate cyclic polytopes whose combinatorics are governed by Gale's evenness condition. They are simplicial and neighborly, meaning every small subset of vertices forms a face, which makes them extremal for the number of faces a polytope can possibly have on given vertices.

Dehn-Sommerville & the Upper Bound Theorem

Dehn-Sommerville

$h_i = h_{d-i}\quad (0 \le i \le d)$

Effect

Roughly halves the free parameters of a simplicial $f$-vector.

Upper Bound Theorem

Among $d$-polytopes with $n$ vertices, the cyclic polytope $C(n,d)$ maximizes every $f_k$.

Printed pp. 77-124Dehn-Sommerville and the Upper Bound Theorem
The Dehn-Sommerville relations are linear equations satisfied by f-vectors of simplicial polytopes, halving their degrees of freedom through the h-vector symmetry. The Upper Bound Theorem then states that cyclic polytopes maximize every face number among all polytopes with a fixed number of vertices in fixed dimension.

Steinitz's Theorem for 3-Polytopes

Theorem

$G$ is the graph of a 3-polytope $\iff$ $G$ is planar and 3-connected.

Consequence

Realizability in $\mathbb{R}^3$ becomes a pure graph property.

Higher dimensions

No such clean characterization; realizability gets hard.

Printed pp. 77-124Steinitz's Theorem
Steinitz's theorem characterizes the graphs of three-dimensional polytopes exactly as the planar three-connected graphs. This turns a geometric realizability question into pure graph theory, and it fails sharply in higher dimensions, where deciding realizability becomes genuinely hard and Gale transforms become essential tools.

Gale Transforms & Recap

Gale transform

$\{a_i\}\subset\mathbb{R}^d \ \rightsquigarrow\ \{\bar a_i\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-d-1}$

Why

Encodes affine dependences; tames high-dimensional combinatorics.

Use

Classifies polytopes with few vertices and probes realizability.

Printed pp. 77-124Gale Transforms and Recap
The Gale transform encodes a point configuration by a dual configuration capturing its affine dependences, making high-dimensional combinatorics visible in low dimension.
