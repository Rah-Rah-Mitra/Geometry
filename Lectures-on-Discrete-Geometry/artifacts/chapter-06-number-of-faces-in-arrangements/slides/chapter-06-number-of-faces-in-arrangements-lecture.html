Arrangements of Hyperplanes

Definition

A finite family $H=\{h_1,\dots,h_n\}$ of hyperplanes in $\mathbb{R}^d$.

Dissection

Each $h_i$ cuts space into two open halfspaces; faces are the pieces.

Arrangement

$\mathcal{A}(H)\subseteq \mathbb{R}^d,\quad h_i=\{x:\langle a_i,x\rangle=b_i\}$

Faces

Connected regions of constant sign pattern across all $h_i$.

Printed pp. 125-164Arrangements of Hyperplanes
An arrangement is the dissection of space produced by a finite family of hyperplanes. Each hyperplane splits everything into two open halfspaces, and intersecting all the resulting regions gives the faces. Faces range in dimension from zero, the vertices, up to the full ambient dimension, the cells.

Faces of Every Dimension

k-faces

Vertices ($k{=}0$), edges ($k{=}1$), up to cells ($k{=}d$).

Sign vectors

A face fixes which $h_i$ it lies on and the side of the rest.

Printed pp. 125-164Faces of Every Dimension
A k-face is a maximal connected piece of space whose points lie on the same chosen subset of hyperplanes and the same side of the rest. We total all faces across dimensions zero through d. This grand total is the combinatorial complexity that the chapter learns to bound sharply.

Simple Arrangements

General position

Every $d$ hyperplanes meet in one point; no $d{+}1$ share a point.

Extremal

Simple arrangements maximize the number of faces.

Vertices, simple

$f_0 = \dbinom{n}{d}$

Why it helps

Bounds proved for simple cases dominate all degenerate ones.

Printed pp. 125-164Simple Arrangements
An arrangement is simple when no d plus one hyperplanes share a point and every d of them meet in exactly one vertex. Simplicity makes the face counts extremal: simple arrangements maximize vertices, so any bound proved for them governs the general degenerate case too.

Counting in the Plane

Vertices

Each pair of lines meets once: $\binom{n}{2}$ vertices.

Euler

$V-E+F=2$ ties edges and cells to the vertex count.

Plane counts (n lines, general position)

$V=\dbinom{n}{2},\;\; E=n^2,\;\; F=\dbinom{n}{2}+n+1$

Growth

All three counts are $\Theta(n^2)$.

Printed pp. 125-164Counting in the Plane
For n lines in general position the plane is cut into vertices, edges, and cells. Choosing pairs gives the vertices, an Euler relation then fixes edges and cells. All three counts grow quadratically, confirming the plane arrangement has complexity on the order of n squared.

Total Complexity $\Theta(n^d)$

Per dimension

$k$-faces of a simple arrangement counted by binomial sums in $n$.

Grand total

Summing over $k=0,\dots,d$ gives a polynomial of degree $d$.

Total faces

$\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{d} f_k \;=\; \Theta(n^{d})$

Tight

Upper bound for all; exactly attained when simple.

Printed pp. 125-164Total Complexity
In d dimensions a simple arrangement of n hyperplanes has faces of each dimension counted by binomial sums in n. Summing over all dimensions, the total number of faces is Theta of n to the d. This polynomial blow-up is both an upper bound and, for simple families, exactly attained.

The Zone of a Hyperplane

Definition

The zone of $h$ is all faces of $\mathcal{A}$ whose closure meets $h$.

Picture

The corridor of cells that $h$ passes through, plus their boundary.

Printed pp. 125-164The Zone of a Hyperplane
The zone of a hyperplane h is the collection of all faces of the arrangement that touch h, meaning faces whose closure meets h. Visually it is the corridor of cells that h passes through together with their boundary pieces. Its size is far smaller than the whole arrangement.

The Zone Theorem

Statement

Total complexity of the zone of one hyperplane is small.

One order down

From $\Theta(n^d)$ overall to $O(n^{d-1})$ near $h$.

Zone Theorem

$\displaystyle\sum_{f\in \mathrm{zone}(h)} \#\,\text{faces} \;=\; O\!\left(n^{\,d-1}\right)$

Plane case

A line's zone has $O(n)$ faces total.

Printed pp. 125-164The Zone Theorem
The zone theorem states that the total complexity of all faces in the zone of a single hyperplane is O of n to the d minus one. Despite a cell having many faces, summed over the entire zone the count stays one order below the full arrangement. The proof uses clever charging.

Proof Idea: Induction and Charging

Remove

Delete one hyperplane; apply the bound to $n-1$ by induction.

Reinsert & charge

New zone faces are charged to the faces they split.

Charging accounting

$Z(n) \le Z(n-1) + c\,n^{\,d-2}$

Bounded charge

Left-right bookkeeping caps charges per face, giving $O(n^{d-1})$.

Printed pp. 125-164Proof Idea
The standard proof removes one hyperplane, applies the bound inductively, then reinserts it and charges the newly created zone faces to the faces they split. A careful left-right accounting ensures each face is charged a bounded number of times, yielding the linear-in-the-codimension constant.

Levels and the $k$-Level

Level of a point

Number of lines strictly below it.

k-level

Closure of all edges at level exactly $k$.

Printed pp. 125-164Levels and the k-Level
Given lines in the plane, the level of a point is the number of lines strictly below it. The k-level is the closure of all arrangement edges whose level equals k. Bounding the complexity of a single level is a famously hard open problem, far subtler than the full count.

Many Cells and Incidences

Incidences

How many point-line incidences can $m$ points and $n$ lines have?

Many cells

How many cells can be touched by many lines at once?

Szemeredi-Trotter

$I(m,n) = O\!\left(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n\right)$

Source

Both bounds descend from arrangement face counting.

Printed pp. 125-164Many Cells and Incidences
Face counts feed incidence geometry. Bounding how many cells can be rich, touching many lines, or how many point-line incidences m points and n lines admit, reduces to arrangement complexity. The Szemeredi-Trotter bound and the many-cells theorem are the headline applications of these counting tools.
