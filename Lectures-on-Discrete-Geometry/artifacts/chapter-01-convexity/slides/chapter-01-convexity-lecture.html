Convex Sets and Affine Hulls

Convex set

Closed under segments: any two points keep their whole connector inside.

Affine subspace

Closed under affine combinations; a translated linear subspace.

Affine independence

$d{+}1$ points span a $d$-flat — the dimension budget.

Convexity condition

$x,y\in C \Rightarrow tx+(1-t)y\in C,\ \ t\in[0,1]$

Affine combination

$\sum_i \lambda_i p_i,\qquad \sum_i \lambda_i = 1$

Matousek, Lectures on Discrete Geometry · printed pp. 1-16Convex Sets and Affine Hulls
A set is convex when every segment between two of its points stays inside. We contrast that with affine subspaces, the flat solution sets that are closed under all affine combinations. Affine independence of d plus one points fixes the dimension count that governs every theorem in the chapter.

The Convex Hull

Two definitions agree

Smallest convex superset $=$ all convex combinations.

Extreme points

Vertices of the hull; cannot be written as a midpoint of others.

Convex hull

$$\mathrm{conv}(X)=\Big\{\textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i x_i : \lambda_i\ge 0,\ \sum_i\lambda_i=1\Big\}$$

Intuition

The rubber band snapped tight around $X$.

Matousek, Lectures on Discrete Geometry · printed pp. 1-16The Convex Hull
The convex hull is the smallest convex set containing X, equivalently the set of all convex combinations of points of X. We picture it as the rubber band snapped around a point cloud, and we note that in the plane its boundary is a convex polygon whose vertices are extreme points.

Caratheodory's Theorem

Theorem

Every $x\in\mathrm{conv}(X)$ for $X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^d$ is a convex combination of at most $d{+}1$ points of $X$.

Why $d{+}1$?

That is the size of an affinely independent set spanning $\mathbb{R}^d$.

Proof move

Take a combination of $k>d{+}1$ points. They are affinely dependent, so a nonzero $\sum\mu_i=0$ exists.

Reduce length

$\lambda_i' = \lambda_i - t\mu_i,\quad t=\min_{\mu_i>0}\tfrac{\lambda_i}{\mu_i}$

Effect

One coefficient hits zero; repeat until $\le d{+}1$ remain.

Matousek, Lectures on Discrete Geometry · printed pp. 1-16Caratheodory's Theorem
Caratheodory bounds representation length: any point of the convex hull in R^d is already a convex combination of at most d plus one of the points. The proof reduces an overlong combination by following a direction in the kernel of an affine dependence, driving one coefficient to zero without leaving the hull.

Radon's Lemma

Lemma

Any $d{+}2$ points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ split into disjoint $A,B$ with $\mathrm{conv}(A)\cap\mathrm{conv}(B)\neq\varnothing$.

Affine dependence

$d{+}2$ points always satisfy a nontrivial relation $\sum\alpha_i p_i=0,\ \sum\alpha_i=0$.

Sort by sign

$A=\{i:\alpha_i>0\},\quad B=\{i:\alpha_i\le 0\}$

Shared point

$\displaystyle \frac{1}{S}\!\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_i p_i=\frac{1}{S}\!\sum_{i\in B}(-\alpha_i)p_i$

Normalizer

$S=\sum_{i\in A}\alpha_i>0$ makes both sides convex combinations.

Matousek, Lectures on Discrete Geometry · printed pp. 1-16Radon's Lemma
Radon's lemma states that any d plus two points in R^d can be split into two disjoint groups whose convex hulls intersect. The proof solves a homogeneous affine system, then sorts indices by the sign of the coefficients, building the shared point from the positive and negative parts of the dependence.

Helly's Theorem

Theorem

If among finitely many convex sets in $\mathbb{R}^d$ every $d{+}1$ have a common point, then all of them do.

Sharpness

$d{+}1$ is necessary: $d{+}1$ slabs can pairwise meet yet miss a global point.

Proof by induction

Drop to $n-1$ sets, pick a witness $x_i$ in each $(n-1)$-subfamily.

Radon glue

$\mathrm{conv}(A)\cap\mathrm{conv}(B)\ni z \in \bigcap_i C_i$

Finite to infinite

Compactness extends the result to infinite families of compact convex sets.

Matousek, Lectures on Discrete Geometry · printed pp. 1-16Helly's Theorem
Helly's theorem is the finite intersection principle of convex geometry: if every d plus one of finitely many convex sets in R^d share a common point, then all of them do. The inductive proof removes one set at a time and uses Radon's lemma to glue the witness points back together.

The Centerpoint Theorem

Theorem

For any $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ there is a point $c$ such that every closed halfspace containing $c$ holds at least $\tfrac{n}{d+1}$ of them.

Depth

$c$ need not be a data point; it is a robust notion of "center".

Proof sketch

For each deep halfspace take its complement's hull; these convex sets satisfy Helly's hypothesis.

Guarantee

$\forall\,\text{halfspace } H\ni c:\ |X\cap H|\ge \dfrac{n}{d+1}$

In the plane

Some point has at least a third of the data on either side of every line through it.

Matousek, Lectures on Discrete Geometry · printed pp. 1-16The Centerpoint Theorem
A centerpoint of n points is a point so deep that every closed halfplane containing it also contains at least n over d plus one of the points. The centerpoint theorem guarantees one exists for any configuration; its proof applies Helly to the family of deep halfspaces.

The four theorems form one circle of ideas: Radon proves Helly, Helly proves the centerpoint theorem, and Caratheodory controls representation length. Together with separation they supply the combinatorial toolkit reused throughout the book for selection theorems, ham-sandwich cuts, and bounds on intersection patterns of convex sets.
