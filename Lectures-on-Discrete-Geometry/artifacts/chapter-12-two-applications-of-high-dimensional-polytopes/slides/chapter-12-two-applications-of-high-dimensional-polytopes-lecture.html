Why Polytopes Prove Theorems

Vertices = objects

Discrete configurations sit at $0/1$ vertices of a polytope.

Facets = constraints

Valid inequalities cut out exactly the feasible combinatorial states.

Duality = symmetry

Geometric duals mirror combinatorial dualities like graph complementation.

Encoding

$P=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{1}_S : S \text{ feasible}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$

Payoff

One convex statement carries induction over an entire family of subobjects at once.

Printed pp. 289-310Why Polytopes Prove Theorems
A polytope encodes combinatorial data as the vertices and facets of a high-dimensional convex body. Facet inequalities become valid constraints, integral vertices become discrete objects, and a single geometric duality can settle a question that resists direct combinatorial attack across an entire family of induced subobjects simultaneously.

Perfect Graphs and the Stable Set Polytope

Perfection

$\chi(H)=\omega(H)$ for every induced subgraph $H\subseteq G$.

Stable set polytope

$\mathrm{STAB}(G)=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{1}_S : S \text{ independent}\}$

Clique relaxation

$\mathrm{QSTAB}(G)$: all $\sum_{v\in K}x_v\le 1$ over cliques $K$.

Printed pp. 289-310Perfect Graphs and the Stable Set Polytope
A graph is perfect when chromatic number equals clique number on every induced subgraph. The stable set polytope is the convex hull of indicator vectors of independent sets. Perfection is exactly the condition that clique inequalities suffice to describe this polytope completely, so STAB and QSTAB coincide.

Antiblockers and Polytope Duality

Down-closed body

A polytope $P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n_{\ge 0}$ closed under taking smaller points.

Antiblocker

$\mathrm{abl}(P)=\{y\ge 0 : \langle x,y\rangle\le 1\ \forall x\in P\}$

Involution

$\mathrm{abl}(\mathrm{abl}(P))=P$ — a clean geometric duality.

Printed pp. 289-310Antiblockers and Polytope Duality
The antiblocker of a down-closed polytope packages its facet structure into a dual body. Fulkerson's antiblocking theory pairs the clique-constrained polytope of a graph with that of its complement, so a geometric symmetry mirrors the graph-complement symmetry that the perfection property is supposed to respect.

The Weak Perfect Graph Theorem

Theorem (Lovasz, 1972)

$G \text{ perfect} \iff \overline{G} \text{ perfect}$

Polytope form

$\mathrm{STAB}(G)=\mathrm{QSTAB}(G)$ for $G$ exactly when it holds for $\overline G$.

Mechanism

$\mathrm{abl}\big(\mathrm{STAB}(G)\big)=\mathrm{STAB}(\overline G)$ swaps cliques and stable sets.

Why it was hard

The combinatorial conjecture quantified over all induced subgraphs; the polytope absorbs them into one body.

Resolved purely geometrically

Integral vertices of $\mathrm{QSTAB}$ on one side force them on the dual side.

Printed pp. 289-310The Weak Perfect Graph Theorem
Lovasz proved that a graph is perfect if and only if its complement is perfect. The polytope argument shows the clique inequalities describe the stable set polytope precisely when they do for the complement, converting the combinatorial conjecture into a verified facet description through the antiblocking involution.

Reading the Proof Geometrically

One facet per clique

Each maximal clique $K$ contributes the facet $\sum_{v\in K}x_v\le 1$.

Integral vertices

All vertices of $\mathrm{QSTAB}(G)$ are $0/1$ $\iff$ $G$ is perfect.

Induction stored

The body simultaneously certifies every induced subgraph.

Printed pp. 289-310Reading the Proof Geometrically
Each maximal clique contributes one facet of the stable set polytope, and integrality of all vertices certifies perfection. The high-dimensional polytope thus stores every induced subgraph at once, letting one convex-geometric statement carry the inductive weight that the original combinatorial argument would spread across many cases.

Second Application: Brunn-Minkowski

Brunn-Minkowski

$\mathrm{vol}(A+B)^{1/n}\ge \mathrm{vol}(A)^{1/n}+\mathrm{vol}(B)^{1/n}$

Minkowski sum

$A+B=\{a+b : a\in A,\, b\in B\}$, the swept body.

Reading

The map $t\mapsto\mathrm{vol}(\cdot)^{1/n}$ is concave under interpolation.

Printed pp. 289-310Second Application: Brunn-Minkowski
The Brunn-Minkowski inequality states that the n-th root of volume is concave under Minkowski addition of convex bodies. This single high-dimensional volume inequality is the analytic spine of the sorting application that follows, controlling how the cross-sections of an order polytope grow as we move a slicing hyperplane.

Volumes, Slabs, and Concavity

Brunn's slabs

Slice a convex body by parallel hyperplanes $x_1=t$.

Cross-section concavity

$t\mapsto \mathrm{vol}_{n-1}\big(K\cap\{x_1=t\}\big)^{1/(n-1)}$ is concave

Consequence

A unimodal volume profile we can compare across a cut.

Printed pp. 289-310Volumes, Slabs, and Concavity
Slicing a convex body by parallel hyperplanes produces cross-sectional volumes whose one-over-n-minus-one root is concave in the slice height. Brunn's slab form of the inequality turns this geometric concavity into a quantitative tool for comparing the sizes of the two halves an order polytope is cut into.

Sorting a Partial Order

Goal

Reach a total order on a poset $P$ using few comparisons.

Information bound

$\#\text{comparisons}\ge \log_2 e(P)$

Linear extensions

$e(P)$ = number of total orders consistent with $P$.

Printed pp. 289-310Sorting a Partial Order
Given a partial order, we want to reach a total order using few comparisons. The information-theoretic target is order of the logarithm of the number of linear extensions; the open question is whether a single well-chosen comparison can always roughly balance that count of consistent orders.

Linear extensions correspond to a high-dimensional order polytope, and comparing two incomparable elements splits it by a hyperplane. Brunn-Minkowski forces the existence of a pair whose two probabilities lie between roughly a fifth and four fifths, guaranteeing every comparison removes a constant fraction of the remaining extensions.

Synthesis and Takeaways

One method

Encode combinatorics as a high-dimensional polytope $P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$.

Two tools

Facet duality (antiblockers) and volume concavity (Brunn-Minkowski).

Two theorems

Weak perfect graph theorem; near-optimal poset sorting.

Recap formulas

$\mathrm{vol}(A+B)^{1/n}\ge \mathrm{vol}(A)^{1/n}+\mathrm{vol}(B)^{1/n}$

Lesson

High-dimensional convexity repays abstraction with decisive, reusable proofs.

Printed pp. 289-310Synthesis and Takeaways
Two applications, one method: encode combinatorics in a high-dimensional polytope, then exploit either facet duality or volume concavity. Antiblockers delivered the weak perfect graph theorem; Brunn-Minkowski delivered near-optimal sorting of partial orders. Geometry of many dimensions repays the abstraction with decisive, reusable proofs throughout discrete mathematics.
