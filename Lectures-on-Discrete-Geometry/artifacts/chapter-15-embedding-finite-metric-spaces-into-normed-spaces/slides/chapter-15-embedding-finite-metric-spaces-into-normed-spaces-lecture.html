Finite Metric Spaces as Data

Definition

A finite set with a distance obeying symmetry and the triangle inequality.

Running example

Graph metrics: shortest-path distance on a connected graph.

Triangle inequality

$d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$

Printed pp. 355-375Finite Metric Spaces
We fix vocabulary first. A finite metric space is just a distance matrix obeying the triangle inequality, and graph metrics on a connected graph are the running examples. Keeping the object concrete, a labelled point set with shortest-path distances, makes every later embedding question a question about preserving those numbers.

Distortion of an Embedding

Definition

$\dfrac{1}{C}\,d(x,y)\le \|f(x)-f(y)\|\le d(x,y)$

Distortion

The smallest $C\ge 1$ for which such a non-contracting, bounded map exists.

Isometry

$C=1$ preserves every distance exactly; larger $C$ means more warping.

Printed pp. 355-375Distortion of an Embedding
Distortion measures the worst multiplicative stretch and contraction an embedding inflicts. We normalize so the map never contracts, then distortion is the largest expansion ratio over all pairs. A distortion of one means an isometry; the central theorems of the chapter bound how close to one we can force it to be.

Fréchet: Isometry into $\ell_\infty$

Theorem (Fréchet)

Every $n$-point metric space embeds isometrically into $\ell_\infty^{\,n}$.

Coordinates

Fix the points $x_1,\dots,x_n$; coordinate $i$ of $f(x)$ is $d(x,x_i)$.

The map

$f(x)=\big(d(x,x_1),\dots,d(x,x_n)\big)$

Printed pp. 355-375Isometric Embedding into l-infinity
Fréchet's observation is the cleanest positive result: every n-point metric space embeds isometrically into l-infinity of dimension n. Each coordinate is the distance to one fixed point, and the triangle inequality alone forces the sup norm to reproduce the original distance exactly. Universality comes for free in the maximum norm.
Lectures on Discrete GeometryChapter 15

Why the Fréchet Map is Isometric

Upper bound

Each coordinate is $1$-Lipschitz, so $\|f(x)-f(y)\|_\infty\le d(x,y)$.

One-Lipschitz

$|d(x,x_i)-d(y,x_i)|\le d(x,y)$

Lower bound

The coordinate $i=y$ equals $d(x,y)$ exactly, so the sup is attained.

Equality

$\|f(x)-f(y)\|_\infty=\max_i|d(x,x_i)-d(y,x_i)|=d(x,y)$

Takeaway

Distortion $1$ holds with zero structural assumptions, but at the price of $n$ coordinates.

Printed pp. 355-375Why the Fréchet Map is Isometric
The proof is two lines of triangle inequality. Each coordinate is one-Lipschitz, so no pair expands beyond its true distance, while the coordinate indexed by one endpoint of a pair attains the distance exactly. The supremum over coordinates therefore equals the original distance, giving distortion one in l-infinity.

The Cost of $\ell_\infty$ Universality

Price

Fréchet uses dimension $n$: too high for Euclidean geometry or fast algorithms.

Real goal

Embed into low dimension or into $\ell_2$ with small distortion.

Trade-off

Dimension versus distortion drives the rest of the chapter.

Printed pp. 355-375The Cost of l-infinity
Isometry in l-infinity is bought with dimension n, which is far too large for Euclidean geometry or for algorithms. The real question becomes embedding into low-dimensional or Hilbert-type spaces with small distortion. This trade between dimension and distortion is the engine driving the rest of the chapter.

Bourgain's Theorem

Theorem (Bourgain)

Every $n$-point metric space embeds into $\ell_2$ with distortion $O(\log n)$.

Universal

No assumption on the metric is required at all.

Same for $\ell_p$

The bound $O(\log n)$ holds for every $\ell_p$, $1\le p\le\infty$.

Tight

Expanders show $\Omega(\log n)$ is unavoidable in general.

Printed pp. 355-375Bourgain's Theorem
Bourgain's theorem is the headline upper bound: every n-point metric space embeds into Euclidean space, and into any l-p, with distortion O(log n). It is universal, requiring no structural assumption on the space, and it is asymptotically optimal, which the expander lower bound later confirms cannot be improved in general.

Random Subsets as Coordinates

Generalize Fréchet

Replace distance-to-a-point with distance to a random subset $A\subseteq X$.

Distance to a set

$d(x,A)=\min_{a\in A} d(x,a)$

Many scales

Sample sets of geometrically growing sizes $1,2,4,\dots,n$.

Why random

Randomization spreads each distance evenly across the scales.

Printed pp. 355-375Random Subsets as Coordinates
The construction generalizes Fréchet: instead of distance to a single point, each coordinate is the distance to a randomly chosen subset of points. Random sets of geometrically growing sizes are sampled, and the distance-to-set functions become the embedding coordinates. Randomization is what spreads distances across scales evenly.

The Coordinate Construction

Scales

$|A|\in\{1,2,4,\dots,n\},\quad O(\log n)\text{ samples each}$

Coordinate

For each sampled set $A$, use the value $d(x,A)$ as one coordinate of $f(x)$.

No expansion

$d(\cdot,A)$ is $1$-Lipschitz, so the map never stretches any pair.

Printed pp. 355-375The Coordinate Construction
Concretely we sample sets at sizes one, two, four, up to n, repeating O(log n) times at each scale. The coordinate for a set A is the distance from x to the nearest point of A. Each such coordinate is one-Lipschitz, so the map never expands; the work lies in proving it does not contract too much.

Why It Does Not Contract Too Much

Separation at a scale

For each pair, some scale of random set separates them with constant probability.

Per-scale gain

$\mathbb{E}\,|d(x,A)-d(y,A)|\gtrsim \dfrac{d(x,y)}{\log n}$ at the right scale

Sum the scales

Adding contributions over $O(\log n)$ scales lower-bounds the embedded distance.

Conclusion

$\|f(x)-f(y)\|_2\ \gtrsim\ \dfrac{d(x,y)}{\log n}$

Distortion

Combined with the non-expansion bound, distortion is $O(\log n)$.

Printed pp. 355-375Why It Does Not Contract Too Much
The hard direction shows that for any pair, some scale of random set separates them with constant probability, contributing a definite amount to the embedded distance. Summing the guaranteed contributions across the log n scales yields a lower bound on the embedded distance proportional to the true distance over log n.

Expanders Force Distortion $\Omega(\log n)$

Hard instance

A constant-degree expander: diameter $\Theta(\log n)$, most pairs near the diameter.

Lower bound

$c_2(\text{expander})=\Omega(\log n)$

Mechanism

A Poincaré-type inequality bars $\ell_2$ from cheaply separating so many far pairs.

Conclusion

Bourgain's $O(\log n)$ is tight; no better universal bound exists.

Printed pp. 355-375Expanders Force Large Distortion
Expanders are the extremal hard instances. Their graph metric has diameter about log n while most pairs sit near that diameter, yet Euclidean geometry cannot absorb so many mutually far points cheaply. A Poincaré-type inequality forces distortion of order log n, matching Bourgain exactly and proving his bound tight.

Synthesis: Distortion vs Dimension

Fréchet

Free isometry into $\ell_\infty$, but at dimension $n$.

Bourgain

Universal $O(\log n)$ distortion into $\ell_2$ via random subsets.

Expanders

$\Omega(\log n)$ lower bound: the upper bound is best possible.

Closed loop

$1\ \xrightarrow{\ \ell_\infty,\ \dim n\ }\ \text{isometry},\quad c_2(X)=\Theta(\log n)$

Outlook

These three results frame all later refinements of distortion and dimension trade-offs.

Printed pp. 355-375Synthesis and Outlook
The chapter closes a clean loop: l-infinity gives free isometry at dimension n, Bourgain gives O(log n) distortion into Hilbert space, and expanders show that O(log n) is the best universal bound possible. Distortion versus dimension is the recurring trade, and these three results frame all later refinements.
