How often can points and lines touch?

Bound the number of point-line incidences.

The tool

Counting Incidences

Definition

An incidence is a pair $(p,\ell)$ with point $p$ on line $\ell$.

The count

$I(P,L)$ measures the whole point-line relation.

Incidence count

$I(P,L)=\big|\{(p,\ell): p\in\ell\}\big|$

Why it matters

Distance and pattern problems reduce to bounding $I$.

Printed pp. 41-76Counting Incidences
An incidence is just a flag, a point lying on a line. The incidence count I of m points and n lines is the size of this relation. The whole subject is bounding this single integer, because so many distance and pattern questions reduce to counting incidences between geometric objects.

Easy Bounds

One constraint

Two distinct points share at most one common line.

Double count

Cauchy-Schwarz on incident pairs.

Naive bound

$I(P,L)=O\!\left(m\sqrt{n}+n\right)$

Not tight

Correct shape, wrong exponents; the truth is smaller.

Printed pp. 41-76Easy Bounds
Two lines meet in at most one point, so no two points share two common lines. A Cauchy-Schwarz double count on this constraint already yields an incidence bound of order m times root n plus n. It is correct in spirit but far from tight, which motivates the deeper theorem.

The Szemeredi-Trotter Theorem

Theorem

$I(P,L)=O\!\left(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n\right)$

$m$ points, $n$ lines

The bound is tight up to the constant.

Sharp

A grid construction matches the exponents.

Central result

Drives every application in the chapter.

Printed pp. 41-76Szemeredi-Trotter
The Szemeredi-Trotter theorem states the true maximum: incidences are of order m to the two-thirds times n to the two-thirds, plus m plus n. This bound is tight, matched by a grid construction, and it is the central result of the chapter and the engine for everything downstream.

Tightness: The Grid

Construction

A $\sqrt{m}\times\sqrt{m}$ integer grid of points.

Lines

Lines of small slope each hit many grid points.

Printed pp. 41-76Tightness: The Grid
A square root-m by root-m integer grid with lines of small rational slope realizes the extremal count. Checking that the two-thirds exponents are achieved convinces us the theorem cannot be improved. The grid is the canonical witness that the upper bound is best possible up to constants.

The Crossing Number

Definition

$\mathrm{cr}(G)$ is the minimum edge crossings over all plane drawings.

Planar

$\mathrm{cr}(G)=0$ and $e\le 3n-6$.

Printed pp. 41-76Crossing Number
The crossing number of a graph is the fewest pairwise edge crossings over all drawings in the plane. Planar graphs have crossing number zero and obey Euler's bound of at most three n minus six edges. Forcing more edges forces crossings, and quantifying that pressure is the key idea.

The Crossing Number Inequality

Theorem ($e\ge 4n$)

$\mathrm{cr}(G)\ \ge\ \dfrac{e^3}{64\,n^2}$

Hypothesis

At least four times as many edges as vertices.

Proof idea

Keep each vertex with probability $p$; apply $e\le 3n$ to the survivor.

Optimize

Choose $p=4n/e$ to maximize the cubic bound.

Printed pp. 41-76Crossing Number Inequality
When a graph has at least four times as many edges as vertices, its crossing number is at least e cubed over sixty-four n squared. The proof deletes random vertices, applies the linear Euler bound to the survivor, and optimizes the sampling probability. This cubic lower bound is the whole trick.

Proof via Crossings

The graph

Vertices are points; edges join consecutive points on each line.

Edges

$e = I - n$ when every line carries a point.

Squeeze

$\dfrac{(I-n)^3}{64m^2}\le \mathrm{cr}(G)\le \binom{n}{2}$

Solve

Rearranging gives $I=O(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n)$.

Printed pp. 41-76Proof of Szemeredi-Trotter
Build a graph whose vertices are the points and whose edges join consecutive points along each line. Edge count is incidences minus lines. The crossings are bounded by pairs of lines, so the crossing inequality squeezes incidences from above and delivers the two-thirds exponents almost mechanically.

Application: Unit Distances

Question

How many pairs of $n$ points are at distance exactly $1$?

Reduction

Replace points by unit circles; pairs become point-circle incidences.

Printed pp. 41-76Unit Distances
How many pairs of n points can be exactly distance one apart. Replacing each point by a unit circle turns unit-distance pairs into point-circle incidences. Two circles meet twice, mimicking the two-line rule, so the Szemeredi-Trotter machinery yields an n to the four-thirds upper bound.

Application: Distinct Distances

Unit bound

At most $O(n^{4/3})$ unit-distance pairs.

Dual count

Few repeats force many distinct values.

Lower bound

$\#\{\text{distinct distances}\}=\Omega\!\left(\sqrt{n}\right)$

Outlook

Guth-Katz later push this to near-linear $\tilde\Omega(n)$.

Printed pp. 41-76Distinct Distances
Dually, n points must determine many distinct distances. A counting argument against the unit-distance bound forces at least order root n distinct distances, far from the near-linear truth later proved by Guth and Katz. The gap shows how incidence bounds both enable and limit distance geometry.

Incidences with Curves

Generalize

Replace lines by circles or bounded-degree curves.

Condition

Any two curves meet in $\le k$ points.

Curve incidences

$I(P,\Gamma)=O\!\left(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n\right)$

Caveat

Higher multiplicity $k$ weakens the exponents.

Printed pp. 41-76Incidences with Curves
The same scheme generalizes to circles and bounded-degree curves. When any two curves meet in at most a constant number of points, a crossing-number drawing again applies and produces Szemeredi-Trotter-type bounds, though the exponents weaken with the multiplicity of intersections allowed between curves.

Recap and Outlook

What we proved

The tight $m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n$ incidence bound.

How

Translate geometry into a graph drawing; count crossings.

Applications

Unit distances, distinct distances, curve incidences.

The method

A combinatorial invariant settles a geometric extremal problem.

Printed pp. 41-76Recap and Outlook
We counted incidences, proved the tight Szemeredi-Trotter bound through the crossing-number inequality, and harvested unit-distance, distinct-distance, and curve applications. The takeaway is a method: translate a geometric extremal question into a graph drawing and let the crossing invariant do the counting.
