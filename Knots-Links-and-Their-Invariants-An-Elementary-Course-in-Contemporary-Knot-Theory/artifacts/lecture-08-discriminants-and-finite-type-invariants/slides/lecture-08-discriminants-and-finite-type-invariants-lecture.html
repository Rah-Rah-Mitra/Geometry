The Space of Knots

Embeddings

All smooth embeddings of the circle form a huge space.

Paths

Generic paths between embeddings are isotopies.

Bad maps

Immersions with double points fail to embed.

Printed pp. 69-76The Space of Knots
Think of all smooth embeddings of the circle as an infinite-dimensional space. Generic paths in it are isotopies, but special maps that fail to be embeddings, namely immersions with double points, form a codimension-one subset. Crossing this subset corresponds to changing a crossing in a diagram, the fundamental local move.

The Discriminant

Definition

The set of singular knots with a transverse double point.

Chambers

It cuts the space into one region per knot type.

Strata

Walls meet along higher singularities.

Control

Their combinatorics governs how invariants jump.

Printed pp. 69-76The Discriminant
Define the discriminant as the set of singular knots, those with at least one transverse double point. It separates the space of knots into chambers, one for each knot type. Walls of the discriminant meet along higher singularities, and the combinatorics of these strata controls how invariants can jump from chamber to chamber.

Singular Knots and Differences

Difference rule

Value at a double point is positive minus negative resolution.

Discrete derivative

It measures change across the wall.

Rule

$v(K_{\times})=v(K_{+})-v(K_{-})$

Iterate

Repeating it counts many double points.

Printed pp. 69-76Singular Knots and Differences
Extend any knot invariant to singular knots by the difference rule: the value at a double point is the value of the positive resolution minus the negative resolution. This finite difference across the discriminant wall is the discrete derivative that measures how the invariant changes as a crossing is switched.

Toward Finite Type

Definition

Order $n$ means it vanishes beyond $n$ double points.

Meaning

Polynomial of degree $n$ in crossing changes.

Vanishing

$v(K)=0\ \text{if}\ \#\,\text{double points}>n$

Examples

Conway and Jones coefficients fit this scheme.

Printed pp. 69-76Toward Finite Type
Call an invariant of finite type of order n if its extension vanishes on every singular knot with more than n double points. This says the invariant is polynomial of degree n in the crossing changes. Coefficients of the Conway and Jones polynomials, suitably expanded, supply the first examples.
