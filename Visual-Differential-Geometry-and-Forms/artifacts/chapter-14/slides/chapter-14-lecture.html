The Curvature of a Spike

How can curvature live at a single vertex of a cone or polyhedron?

The local surprise

A cone is flat away from its tip. Small neighborhoods not touching the vertex can be unrolled into the plane without stretching.

The global clue

A loop enclosing the tip does not behave like a loop in the plane: the angular account is short.

Chapter Question

Where did the missing curvature go?

The guiding model is a cone or polyhedral spike: away from the vertex the surface is locally flat, but a loop around the vertex returns with a missing angle.

The Angular Ledger

Vertex Defect

$\delta = 2\pi - \theta_{\mathrm{around}}$

Interpretation

The defect is the total curvature assigned to the vertex. It is an integral quantity, not a finite pointwise value of $K$.

What to inspect

If the surface angle around the tip is less than $2\pi$, the spike carries positive curvature.

$\delta$ measures concentrated curvatureThe Angular Ledger
The cone replaces smooth curvature density with an angular ledger. Around an ordinary planar point the incident angle is two pi. Around a spike the available angle is smaller, and the shortfall is the total Gaussian curvature stored at the vertex. This is the discrete version of integrating K over a tiny neighborhood.

Unroll the Cone: An Intrinsic Test

Cut

Slice along one generator from the tip to the base.

Flatten

The cone opens into a planar sector because no stretching is needed away from the vertex.

Sector Account

sector angle $= \theta_{\mathrm{around}}$
defect $=2\pi-\theta_{\mathrm{around}}$

The cone is flat except at the vertexUnrolling
Unrolling the cone is the cleanest intrinsic test because it avoids looking from outside. Cut along a generator and flatten the surface. The sides fit in the plane without stretching, but the circular neighborhood opens into a sector whose missing wedge is exactly the defect. The vertex remembers the cut even after every other point becomes flat.

Curvature as a Point Measure

Smooth Surface

$\displaystyle \int_D K\,dA$

Spike Surface

$\displaystyle \int_D K\,dA = \begin{cases} \delta, & v\in D,\\ 0, & v\notin D. \end{cases}$

Measure viewpoint

The curvature is concentrated at $v$ like a point mass with weight $\delta$.

Concentrated curvature replaces pointwise densityPoint measure
Smooth Gaussian curvature spreads through area; spike curvature is concentrated like a point mass. We should not say the cone has an ordinary finite value of K at the tip. Instead its integral over any small disk containing the tip is the defect, while disks missing the tip integrate to zero.

Polyhedral Vertex Formula

Discrete Gaussian Curvature

$\displaystyle K(v)=2\pi-\sum_{f\ni v}\alpha_f(v)$

Surface-only data

Each $\alpha_f(v)$ is a face angle measured inside the surface. No normal vectors are needed for this computation.

Sign

Less than $2\pi$ gives positive curvature; more than $2\pi$ gives negative curvature.

Polyhedral curvature = angle defectVertex formula
For a polyhedral surface the same bookkeeping is local and finite. Add the face angles that meet at one vertex, subtract the sum from two pi, and obtain the vertex curvature. Positive defect looks like a peak; negative defect looks like excess angle or a saddle-like vertex. The formula is entirely intrinsic because it uses face angles measured on the surface.

Spherical Image of the Normal Fan

Gauss map idea

Smooth curvature equals signed area swept out by unit normals.

Polyhedral version

Each flat face has one normal. Around a vertex, adjacent normals form a spherical polygon.

Agreement

area of spherical image $=$ angular defect

Extrinsic normals recover the same numberSpherical image
The extrinsic view follows the normals. Around a smooth patch, the Gauss map sweeps out area on the unit sphere; for a polyhedral vertex, each face contributes one constant normal and the normals trace a spherical polygon. The area of that spherical polygon equals the same defect, so the normal picture agrees with the angle-sum ledger.

Their equality is the polyhedral shadow of the Theorema Egregium. Curvature is revealed by embedding, but its value is already encoded in the intrinsic metric.

The Smoothing Limit

Round the tip

Replace the singular vertex with a small smooth cap. Now $K$ is an ordinary function on the cap.

Shrink the cap

The curvature density rises, the area decreases, and the integral stabilizes.

Stable Quantity

$\displaystyle \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{D_\varepsilon}K_\varepsilon\,dA=\delta$

Concentration is a limit of smooth curvatureSmoothing limit
A spike can be rounded into a smooth cap, and then the curvature density becomes ordinary again. As the cap shrinks, the pointwise curvature grows while the area supporting it shrinks. The important invariant is the integral: the total curvature tends to the same angular defect rather than disappearing or exploding.

Worked Defect Computation

Five triangles

$\sum\alpha=5\cdot\pi/3$

$K(v)=\pi/3$

Positive spike: the vertex is short by one sixth-turn.

Six triangles

$\sum\alpha=6\cdot\pi/3$

$K(v)=0$

Flat tiling: the local angle account exactly balances.

Seven triangles

$\sum\alpha=7\cdot\pi/3$

$K(v)=-\pi/3$

Negative vertex: the surface has angular excess.

Exercise-ready calculationWorked example
A quick computation makes the unit of measurement concrete. If five equilateral triangle corners meet at a vertex, the angle sum is five pi over three, so the defect is pi over three. If six meet, the defect is zero and the surface is locally flat like a hexagonal tiling. If seven meet, the defect is negative.

Recap: What the Spike Teaches

1. Curvature can concentrate

A cone is flat except at the tip, but the total curvature at that tip is nonzero.

2. Angle defect is intrinsic

The number $2\pi-\sum\alpha_f$ is computed from surface angles.

3. Normals agree

The spherical image of the normal fan has the same area as the defect.
