The Principal Curvatures of a Surface

How every direction of bending collapses into two numbers

The question

At one point a surface bends differently in every direction. Can we organize all of it?

The answer

Two orthogonal extremes, $\kappa_1$ and $\kappa_2$, generate every normal curvature by Euler's formula.

This chapter, printed pages 109 through 114, asks how every direction of bending at one surface point can be organized. We slice the surface with vertical planes, read off a curvature for each slice, and discover that two orthogonal directions carry the extreme values from which all others follow.

Normal Sections

Construction

Erect the unit normal $\mathbf{N}$ at point $p$. Any plane through $\mathbf{N}$ slices the surface in a plane curve.

Normal curvature

The signed curvature of that slice at $p$ is the normal curvature $\kappa_n(\theta)$ for its direction.

Definition

$\kappa_n(\theta) = \mathrm{II}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{t}) = \mathbf{t}^\top S\, \mathbf{t}$

pp. 109-114Normal Sections
Pick a point and erect the surface normal there. Any plane containing that normal cuts the surface in a plane curve called a normal section. The signed curvature of that curve at the point is the normal curvature for its direction, and it changes smoothly as the cutting plane rotates.

The Sign of Normal Curvature

Convention

Bending toward $\mathbf{N}$ is positive; bending away is negative. The orientation of $\mathbf{N}$ fixes the sign once and for all.

Saddles change sign

On a saddle some sections curve up and some curve down, so $\kappa_n$ must pass through zero as the plane sweeps.

pp. 109-114Sign of Normal Curvature
We orient normal curvature against the chosen unit normal. A section bending toward the normal counts as positive, one bending away as negative. On a saddle some directions curve up while others curve down, so the normal curvature must change sign as the cutting plane sweeps around the point.

Sweep the plane

Rotate the cutting plane through every direction $\theta$ in the tangent plane.

One max, one min

The curvatures order into a single smooth peak and a single smooth trough.

Claim

$\kappa_n(\theta)$ is governed by just two numbers.

Sweeping the cutting plane through every direction produces a fan of normal sections sharing one tangent point. Principal Curvatures and Directions

Definition

$\kappa_1 = \max_\theta \kappa_n$ and $\kappa_2 = \min_\theta \kappa_n$ are the principal curvatures.

Principal directions

The directions $\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2$ achieving the extremes are the principal directions.

Euler's theorem

$\mathbf{e}_1 \perp \mathbf{e}_2$ — the extremes are orthogonal.

pp. 109-114Principal Curvatures
The largest and smallest normal curvatures at a point are the principal curvatures kappa one and kappa two. The directions in which these extremes occur are the principal directions. Euler proved these two privileged directions are always orthogonal, so they frame the tangent plane with a natural perpendicular coordinate cross.

Euler's Formula

The blending law

An oblique section at angle $\theta$ from $\mathbf{e}_1$ has a curvature that is a weighted mix of the two extremes.

Euler's formula

$$\kappa_n(\theta) = \kappa_1\cos^2\theta + \kappa_2\sin^2\theta$$

Weights sum to one

$\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, so $\kappa_n$ stays trapped between $\kappa_2$ and $\kappa_1$.

pp. 109-114Euler's Formula
Euler's formula gives the normal curvature of an oblique section as kappa one cosine squared theta plus kappa two sine squared theta, where theta is measured from the first principal direction. Every directional curvature is thus a weighted blend of just the two extremes, with the weights summing to one.

Why Euler's Formula Holds

Diagonal second form

In principal coordinates the shape operator is diagonal, so $\mathrm{II}$ has no cross term.

Local height

$$z \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\kappa_1 x^2 + \kappa_2 y^2\big)$$

Plug in a unit tangent

Set $x=\cos\theta,\,y=\sin\theta$; the quadratic returns Euler's expression at once.

pp. 109-114Why the Formula Holds
In principal coordinates the second fundamental form is diagonal, so the height of the surface above its tangent plane is a pure sum of squared coordinates with coefficients kappa one and kappa two. Plugging a unit tangent at angle theta into this quadratic and doubling recovers Euler's cosine-sine expression directly.

Polar picture

Plot $|\kappa_n(\theta)|$ as a radius in direction $\theta$. The whole bending continuum becomes one closed curve.

Read the extremes

Longest radius is $\kappa_1$, shortest is $\kappa_2$, and the symmetry axes are the principal directions.

Plotting normal curvature against direction traces a smooth closed rose. Its two perpendicular axes of symmetry are exactly the principal directions, and its longest and shortest radii read off kappa one and kappa two. The whole continuum of bending is compressed into this single two-lobed picture.

Mean and Gaussian Curvature

Two combinations

$$H = \tfrac{1}{2}(\kappa_1+\kappa_2),\qquad K = \kappa_1\kappa_2$$

Mean curvature $H$

The average bending; it depends on how the surface is embedded in space.

Gaussian curvature $K$

The product; Gauss showed it is intrinsic — invisible to bending without stretching.

pp. 109-114Mean and Gaussian Curvature
From the two principals we build two famous combinations. Their average is the mean curvature H, sensitive to how the surface sits in space, while their product is the Gaussian curvature K. Gauss later proved K is intrinsic, surviving any bending that does not stretch the surface fabric.

The Sign of $K$ Classifies the Point

$K>0$ elliptic

Same signs: a dome or bowl, surface stays on one side of the tangent plane.

$K<0$ hyperbolic

Opposite signs: a saddle, surface crosses through its tangent plane.

$K=0$ parabolic

One principal vanishes: a cylinder-like ridge, flat in one direction.

pp. 109-114Sign of K and Local Shape
The sign of the Gaussian product classifies the point. Both principals agreeing gives positive K and a dome or bowl; opposite signs give negative K and a saddle; one principal vanishing gives zero K and a cylinder-like ridge that bends only one way while staying flat along the other.

Lab: Surface of Revolution

Symmetry picks the axes

Meridians and parallels are the principal directions — no computation of extremes needed.

Two principal curvatures

$\kappa_{\text{mer}}$ from the profile, $\kappa_{\text{par}}$ from the circle's section

Then assemble

$K=\kappa_{\text{mer}}\kappa_{\text{par}}$ and $H=\tfrac12(\kappa_{\text{mer}}+\kappa_{\text{par}})$ vary with height.

pp. 109-114Surface of Revolution Lab
On a surface of revolution the principal directions align with meridians and parallels by symmetry. The meridian supplies one principal curvature directly, while the circular parallel supplies the other through its normal section. Computing both lets us read mean and Gaussian curvature at every height along the profile.

Recap and Outlook

What we built

  • Normal sections give a curvature per direction.
  • Two orthogonal extremes: $\kappa_1,\kappa_2$.
  • Euler blends them for every $\theta$.

Packaged invariants

Mean $H$ for shape in space, Gaussian $K=\kappa_1\kappa_2$ for intrinsic geometry.
