Gauss's Theorema Egregium

Why is Gaussian curvature intrinsic when normals first define it?

A surface can bend in space while preserving every local ruler measurement.

The tension

Gaussian curvature is introduced through how the surface sits in space.

The surprise

The same number is determined by the metric alone.

Open by naming the surprise. Gaussian curvature was first introduced through the normal direction and the way a surface sits in three dimensional space, so it looks extrinsic. Gauss's theorem says the same number can be recovered from the surface's own metric data. The lecture question is therefore not whether curvature is useful, but why an outside-looking definition is secretly measurable from inside the surface.

The Suspicious Definition

The usual construction of $K$ seems to need an outside observer

Gaussian curvature

$$K = k_1 k_2$$

$k_1$ and $k_2$ are the principal normal curvatures.

Why suspicious?

Principal curvatures are found by slicing the surface with normal planes and reading how those space curves bend.

The theorem's claim

After multiplying the two normal curvatures, all dependence on the chosen embedding disappears.

printed pp. 138-142; PDF pp. 167-171Extrinsic Start
Before stating the theorem, remind the room why there is something to prove. Principal curvatures are found by watching normal sections bend in ambient space, and their product gives Gaussian curvature. That construction uses normals, osculating curves, and an outside viewer. The theorem will insist that after all of that extrinsic machinery has run, the final product depends only on the first fundamental form.

Bending Without Stretching

A plane and a cylinder are locally the same metric geometry

Local isometry

$$\Phi(u,v)=(\cos u,\sin u,v), \qquad ds^2=du^2+dv^2$$

What changes

The surface normal and embedding in space change dramatically.

What does not

The metric remains Euclidean locally, so $K=0$ on both the plane and cylinder.

printed pp. 138-142; PDF pp. 167-171Plane to Cylinder
Use the plane-to-cylinder example as the tactile model. A sheet rolled into a cylinder changes its normal directions and its visible embedding, but a rectangular grid drawn on the sheet keeps the same edge lengths and angles. Because the metric is unchanged, Theorema Egregium predicts the Gaussian curvature stays zero before and after the bending.

The Theorem

Gaussian curvature is preserved by local isometry

Theorema Egregium

If two surface patches are locally isometric, then corresponding points have the same Gaussian curvature.

$$\Phi^\*g_N=g_M \quad\Longrightarrow\quad K_M = K_N\circ\Phi$$

Equivalent reading

$K$ can be computed from the first fundamental form and its derivatives alone.

Why remarkable?

The formula $K=k_1k_2$ begins with normal curvature in space, yet the final value is a metric invariant.

printed pp. 138-142; PDF pp. 167-171Theorema Egregium
State the theorem cleanly and slowly. If two surface patches are locally isometric, then their Gaussian curvatures agree at corresponding points. Equivalently, Gaussian curvature is determined by the first fundamental form and its derivatives. The word remarkable matters here because the definition seems to require the second fundamental form, while the theorem says the metric already contains the answer.

How the Proof Can Possibly Work

The proof replaces normal bending by connection curvature

1. Metric

Start with $g_{ij}$ and the inverse matrix $g^{ij}$.

$$ds^2=g_{ij}dx^i dx^j$$

2. Connection

Metric compatibility determines the Christoffel symbols.

$$\Gamma^k_{ij}=\tfrac12 g^{k\ell}(\partial_i g_{j\ell}+\partial_j g_{i\ell}-\partial_\ell g_{ij})$$

3. Curvature

The connection's failure to commute yields a scalar in dimension two.

$$R_{1212} = K\,\det(g)$$

Proof Roadmap

Show the scalar obtained from the metric connection agrees with the extrinsic product $k_1k_2$. The ambient normal vanishes from the final computation.

printed pp. 138-142; PDF pp. 167-171Proof Mechanism
Do not run a full symbolic proof on a slide. Instead give the roadmap: write the Levi-Civita connection from the metric, compute the curvature of that connection, and show the resulting scalar is the same Gaussian curvature obtained from the shape operator. This shows how extrinsic bending data gets eliminated and replaced by metric bookkeeping.

Bending Versus Stretching

Only stretching changes the intrinsic data

Bending

Changes the embedding and normals, but preserves all small lengths and angles.

Stretching

Changes metric coefficients, so curvature computed from the metric may change.

Diagnostic

$$g \text{ unchanged } \Rightarrow K \text{ unchanged}$$

Consequences for Maps

Curvature is an obstruction to perfect flattening

Plane to cylinder

Possible locally: both have $K=0$, so the metric can be preserved by rolling.

Sphere to plane

Impossible without distortion: a sphere has $K>0$, while the plane has $K=0$.

Mapmaker's lesson

Any flat map of the Earth must distort distances, angles, areas, or some combination of them.

printed pp. 138-142; PDF pp. 167-171Mapping Consequences
Now translate the theorem into a practical obstruction. A map that preserves all small lengths and angles must preserve Gaussian curvature. A plane and cylinder can be locally isometric because both have zero Gaussian curvature. A sphere cannot be flattened onto a plane without distortion because positive curvature cannot be carried to zero curvature by an isometry.

Takeaways

The theorem upgrades curvature from a picture to an invariant

1. Extrinsic start

$K=k_1k_2$ is introduced through normal curvatures in ambient space.

2. Intrinsic result

$K$ is determined by the first fundamental form, so local isometries preserve it.

3. Geometric consequence

Bending without stretching preserves curvature; stretching can change it.

Chapter answer

$$\text{same metric} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{same } K$$

Curvature is not merely how a surface looks in space. It is encoded in the rules for measuring on the surface itself.
