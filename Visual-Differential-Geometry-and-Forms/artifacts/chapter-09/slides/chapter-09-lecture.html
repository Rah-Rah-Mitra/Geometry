Curves in 3-Space

The moving frame, curvature $\kappa$, and torsion $\tau$

One extra freedom

A space curve may bend within a plane and also twist out of it.

Two invariants

Curvature $\kappa$ for bending, torsion $\tau$ for twisting.

One frame

The Frenet triple $(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N},\mathbf{B})$ records both at once.

pp. 106-108Curves in 3-Space
This short act, printed pages 106 through 108, asks a single question: what new information appears once a curve is free to leave a plane? Plane curves carried one scalar, curvature. In space we will need a second scalar, torsion, and a moving frame to register both invariants together.

From Plane Curves to Space Curves

Plane curve

Confined to a page. Bending is the only freedom, so one scalar $\kappa$ describes it.

Space curve

Free to lift off the page. A second freedom appears: how it twists away from any plane.

The new question

What invariant measures that lift, and how do we read it off the curve?

pp. 106-108From Plane Curves to Space Curves
In the plane a curve bends but cannot escape its page, so one number per point suffices. A space curve can additionally lift out of any plane we draw it against. We motivate the chapter by contrasting a circle that stays flat with a spring that climbs, asking what extra invariant the climb encodes.

Unit-Speed Parameterization

Strip out speed

Reparameterize by arc length $s$ so the curve is traced at unit speed.

Intrinsic clock

Arc length is geometry; the original parameter is just a label.

Arc length

$s(t)=\displaystyle\int_{t_0}^{t}\lVert\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(u)\rVert\,du$

Unit tangent

$\mathbf{T}(s)=\dfrac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{ds},\qquad \lVert\mathbf{T}\rVert=1$

pp. 106-108Unit-Speed Parameterization
Before measuring bending we strip away how fast the curve is traced. Reparameterizing by arc length makes the velocity a unit tangent, so every later derivative reports pure geometry rather than the bookkeeping of speed. We stress that arc length is intrinsic while the parameter we started with is merely a label.

The Unit Tangent and Curvature

Tangent swing

$\mathbf{T}'(s)$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{T}$ because $\mathbf{T}$ has constant length.

Curvature

$\kappa=\lVert\mathbf{T}'(s)\rVert\ \ge 0$

Osculating circle

The best-fitting round arc has radius $\rho=1/\kappa$.

pp. 106-108The Unit Tangent and Curvature
Differentiating the unit tangent gives a vector orthogonal to it whose length is the curvature kappa. Kappa measures how sharply the tangent direction swings per unit length. We connect this to the osculating circle of radius one over kappa, the round arc that best hugs the curve at that point.

The Frenet Frame

Tangent $\mathbf{T}$

Points along the direction of motion.

Normal $\mathbf{N}$

$\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{T}'/\kappa$, points toward the center of bending.

Binormal $\mathbf{B}$

$\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{T}\times\mathbf{N}$, completes a right-handed triple.

pp. 106-108The Frenet Frame
At each point we attach three orthonormal vectors: the tangent T pointing along motion, the principal normal N pointing toward the center of bending, and the binormal B completing a right-handed triple. This moving frame travels with the particle and turns the study of a curve into the study of how its frame rotates.

The Osculating Plane

Spanned by $\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N}$

The flat sheet that hugs the curve to second order.

Normal is $\mathbf{B}$

The binormal is the unit perpendicular to this plane.

Plane curve test

If $\mathbf{B}$ never changes, the curve stays in one plane.

pp. 106-108The Osculating Plane
The tangent and normal span the osculating plane, the flat sheet that locally contains the curve to second order. The binormal is its unit perpendicular. For a plane curve this sheet never moves, so the binormal is constant; any change in the binormal is the signature that the curve is leaving the plane.

Torsion: Twisting Out of the Plane

Tipping binormal

Torsion is the rate at which $\mathbf{B}$ rotates about the tangent.

Torsion

$\mathbf{B}'(s)=-\tau\,\mathbf{N}$

Bending vs twisting

$\kappa$ acts inside the plane; $\tau$ lifts the plane out of itself.

pp. 106-108Torsion: Twisting Out of the Plane
Torsion tau measures how fast the osculating plane rotates about the tangent as we advance along the curve. Equivalently it is the rate at which the binormal tips. Curvature records bending within a plane; torsion records the curve corkscrewing out of that plane into the third dimension.

The Frenet-Serret Equations

Frame derivatives

$\begin{aligned}\mathbf{T}'&=\kappa\,\mathbf{N}\\ \mathbf{N}'&=-\kappa\,\mathbf{T}+\tau\,\mathbf{B}\\ \mathbf{B}'&=-\tau\,\mathbf{N}\end{aligned}$

Skew-symmetric

The coefficient matrix is antisymmetric, so the frame stays orthonormal.

Matrix form

$\dfrac{d}{ds}\!\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{T}\\\mathbf{N}\\\mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\kappa&0\\-\kappa&0&\tau\\0&-\tau&0\end{pmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{T}\\\mathbf{N}\\\mathbf{B}\end{pmatrix}$

pp. 106-108The Frenet-Serret Equations
The derivatives of T, N, and B with respect to arc length are governed by a single skew-symmetric matrix built from kappa and tau. T turns toward N, B turns toward N, and N feeds both back. Skew-symmetry guarantees the frame stays orthonormal, simply rotating rigidly as it advances.

Reading the Frame as Rotation

Darboux vector

The skew matrix is an angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ spinning the frame.

Angular velocity

$\boldsymbol{\omega}=\tau\,\mathbf{T}+\kappa\,\mathbf{B}$

Decomposed

$\kappa$ is the spin about $\mathbf{B}$; $\tau$ is the spin about $\mathbf{T}$.

pp. 106-108Reading the Frame as Rotation
The skew-symmetric coefficient matrix is the angular velocity of the frame, the Darboux vector. Its component along the binormal is curvature and its component along the tangent is torsion. So the whole motion of the frame is one instantaneous rotation, decomposed into bending and twisting parts.

The Helix: Constant $\kappa$ and $\tau$

Model curve

A circular helix has constant $\kappa$ and constant $\tau$ everywhere.

Helix of radius $a$, pitch $2\pi b$

$\kappa=\dfrac{a}{a^2+b^2},\quad \tau=\dfrac{b}{a^2+b^2}$

Limits

$b\!\to\!0$ gives a circle ($\tau=0$); $a\!\to\!0$ gives a straight line.

pp. 106-108The Helix: Constant Curvature and Torsion
A circular helix has constant curvature and constant torsion, and conversely those two constants determine a helix up to rigid motion. It is the model space curve, the analogue of the circle for plane curves. We give the explicit radius and pitch formulas in terms of kappa and tau.

The Fundamental Theorem of Space Curves

Theorem

Given $\kappa(s)>0$ and $\tau(s)$, there is a curve with those invariants, unique up to a rigid motion.

Complete invariants

$\kappa$ and $\tau$ encode everything about the curve's shape.

Why

Frenet-Serret is a linear ODE; initial frame fixes the rest.

pp. 106-108The Fundamental Theorem of Space Curves
Prescribing curvature and torsion as functions of arc length determines a curve uniquely up to a rigid motion of space. Kappa and tau are therefore a complete set of bending-and-twisting invariants. We frame this as the payoff of the chapter: two scalars fully encode the shape of any space curve.

Recap and the Straw Model

Frame

$(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N},\mathbf{B})$ rides the curve as a rigid orthonormal triple.

$\kappa$ bends

Curvature turns the curve inside its osculating plane.

$\tau$ twists

Torsion rotates that plane about the tangent.

Ahead

The same frame language carries us into surfaces next.

pp. 106-108Recap and the Straw Model
We close with the physical straw-and-wire picture: thread a bent wire through a straw and watch the frame ride along it. Recapping, kappa bends the curve inside the osculating plane and tau twists that plane about the tangent, and together they replay the entire curve. This sets up surfaces in the chapters ahead.
