Restated: The First Fundamental Form

Intrinsic data

Three coefficients $E,F,G$ encode all length and angle measurement.

Coordinate speeds

$E,G$ are squared speeds of the coordinate curves; $F$ is their inner product.

Line element

$$ds^2 = E\,du^2 + 2F\,du\,dv + G\,dv^2$$

Why intrinsic

An inhabitant of the surface measures with $E,F,G$ alone, never leaving it.

pp. 83-93The First Fundamental Form
Every surface measurement starts from the metric. We recall how the first fundamental form encodes lengths, angles, and areas through three coefficient functions, and we stress that these coefficients are intrinsic data: they let an inhabitant of the surface do all geometry without ever leaving it or seeing the ambient embedding.

Worked Problem A — Metric of the Sphere

Exercise

Parameterize the unit sphere by colatitude $\theta$ and longitude $\phi$ and read off its first fundamental form.

Setup

$\mathbf{x}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\,\cos\theta)$

Result

$$ds^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\!\theta\, d\phi^2$$

Read the answer

$E=1$, $F=0$, $G=\sin^2\theta$: meridians and parallels are orthogonal, and the metric varies with latitude.

pp. 83-93Worked Problem A: Sphere Metric
Our first worked exercise parameterizes the round sphere and reads off its metric from the speed of coordinate curves. We compute the coefficients directly, confirm that meridians and parallels meet at right angles, and note the shrinking parallels that make the metric vary with latitude rather than being flat.

Restated: Conformal Maps

Angle-preserving

A map is conformal when its metric is a positive scalar times the flat one.

Conformal condition

$$ds^2 = \Lambda(u,v)^2\,(du^2 + dv^2)$$

Coefficient test

$F=0$ and $E=G$: the off-diagonal term vanishes and the diagonal terms agree.

Geometric meaning

$\Lambda$ may stretch lengths, but it rescales every direction equally, so angles are untouched.

pp. 83-93Conformal Maps
A conformal map preserves angles by scaling the metric by a single positive factor. We restate this condition, explain why the off-diagonal coefficient must vanish and the diagonal ones must agree, and emphasize that conformality is exactly the statement that infinitesimal circles map to infinitesimal circles, never to ellipses.

Worked Problem B — Stereographic Projection

Exercise

Project the sphere from the north pole onto the equatorial plane and find the conformal factor.

Pulled-back metric

$$ds^2 = \frac{4\,(dx^2+dy^2)}{(1+x^2+y^2)^2}$$

Conclusion

The factor is $\Lambda=\tfrac{2}{1+r^2}$, the same in every direction, so the projection is conformal.

Why it matters

One angle-faithful chart of the sphere; this is the model used for the Poincare disc later.

pp. 83-93Worked Problem B: Stereographic Projection
Stereographic projection is the canonical conformal chart of the sphere. We sketch the projection from the north pole, derive its conformal factor as a function of distance from the origin, and verify the angle-preserving property by watching the coordinate grid stay orthogonal everywhere in the plane image.

Restated: The Pseudosphere

Tractrix of revolution

Spin the tractrix about its asymptote to obtain a surface of constant negative curvature.

Strip metric

$$ds^2 = du^2 + e^{-2u}\,dv^2$$

Hyperbolic in disguise

A change of variable turns this into the half-plane metric of the hyperbolic plane.

Saddle everywhere

Every point looks like a saddle; this is the visual signature of negative curvature.

pp. 83-93The Pseudosphere
The pseudosphere is the tractrix surface of revolution, the constant negative curvature counterpart of the sphere. We recall its profile curve, write its metric in the strip coordinates that reveal hyperbolic structure, and stress that it realizes a piece of the hyperbolic plane as a genuine surface sitting in ordinary space.

Worked Problem C — Confirming $K=-1$

Exercise

Using the half-plane form $ds^2 = \tfrac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2}$, compute the Gaussian curvature.

Conformal-metric formula

$$K = -\frac{1}{\Lambda^2}\,\Delta(\ln\Lambda)$$

Carry it out

$\Lambda=\tfrac1y,\ \ln\Lambda=-\ln y \;\Rightarrow\; K=-1$

Sanity check

A clean negative constant matches the saddle geometry: triangles close with less than $\pi$.

pp. 83-93Worked Problem C: Curvature of the Pseudosphere
Here we confirm that the pseudosphere has constant curvature minus one. Using the half-plane form of its metric we apply the curvature formula for a conformally flat metric, carry out the logarithmic derivatives, and obtain a clean negative constant, matching the saddle-like geometry visible in the surface.

Restated: Two Hyperbolic Models

Upper half-plane

$$ds^2 = \frac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2},\quad y>0$$

Poincare disc

$$ds^2 = \frac{4\,(dx^2+dy^2)}{(1-x^2-y^2)^2}$$

Geodesics

Vertical lines and boundary-orthogonal arcs; diameters and arcs in the disc.

Same geometry

Both are conformal, both have $K=-1$; the disc is bounded, the half-plane is open above.

pp. 83-93Two Models of the Hyperbolic Plane
Hyperbolic geometry has two standard pictures: the upper half-plane and the Poincare disc. We restate both metrics, identify their geodesics as vertical lines and circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles, and prepare to move objects between the two models using a single conformal transformation.

Restated: Hyperbolic Isometries

Mobius transformation

$$z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d},\quad ad-bc=1$$

Isometries

Real-coefficient Mobius maps preserve the half-plane metric and its boundary.

Conformal and geodesic-preserving

They keep angles and carry geodesics to geodesics.

Three types

Elliptic (rotation), parabolic (one boundary fixed point), hyperbolic (translation along a geodesic).

pp. 83-93Hyperbolic Isometries
The isometries of the hyperbolic plane are exactly the Mobius transformations that preserve the boundary. We restate the Mobius form, recall that these maps act conformally and send geodesics to geodesics, and classify them as elliptic, parabolic, or hyperbolic according to their fixed-point behavior on the boundary circle or line.

Worked Problem D — Cayley Transform

Exercise

Use the Cayley map to send the half-plane to the disc and track a geodesic.

The map

$$w = \frac{z-i}{z+i}$$

Trace the image

The imaginary axis $\to$ a diameter; a boundary semicircle $\to$ a boundary-orthogonal arc.

Why an isometry

The conformal factors transform so the line element is unchanged: the map is a true isometry.

pp. 83-93Worked Problem D: A Mobius Geodesic Transfer
We carry a half-plane geodesic into the disc with the Cayley transform. Tracking three boundary points through the map, we show that a vertical line becomes a diameter and a semicircle becomes a boundary-orthogonal arc, confirming that the transformation is an isometry between the two hyperbolic models.

Restated: Lorentz and Spacetime

Minkowski line element

$$ds^2 = dx^2 - dt^2$$

Sign flip

The minus sign is everything: it carves the plane into timelike, null, and spacelike directions.

Hyperbolic again

Constant proper time $t^2-x^2=1$ is a hyperbola whose induced geometry is hyperbolic.

Closing Act II

The indefinite metric reframes everything: hyperbolic geometry lives inside Minkowski space.

pp. 83-93Lorentz and Spacetime Metrics
Act II closes with the indefinite metric of spacetime. We restate the Minkowski line element with its crucial sign flip, contrast its light-cone structure with the positive-definite surface metrics, and note that hyperbolic geometry reappears as the geometry of the unit timelike hyperboloid of constant proper time.
