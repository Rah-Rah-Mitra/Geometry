Geodesics and Geodesic Curvature

Splitting a curve's bending into normal and sideways turning

The question

How much of a curve's bending is forced by the surface, and how much is the curve veering inside it?

The answer

Resolve the curvature vector into a normal part and a geodesic part. Geodesics are where the second vanishes.

This chapter asks a single sharp question: when a curve lives on a surface, how much of its bending is forced by the surface and how much is the curve veering inside it. A Curve on a Surface: The Moving Frame

Three axes at every point

Parameterize by arc length $s$. At each point of the curve build an orthonormal triple riding along it.

The Darboux frame

$\mathbf{T}=\dfrac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds},\quad \mathbf{N}\perp\text{surface},\quad \mathbf{U}=\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{T}$

Why this triple

$\mathbf{T}$ points along the path, $\mathbf{N}$ off the surface, and $\mathbf{U}$ sideways within the surface. Every measurement resolves against these.

pp. 115-129A Curve on a Surface
We anchor everything to the moving frame riding along the curve: the unit tangent, the surface normal, and the in-surface side vector that completes the orthonormal triple. Every later decomposition is just resolving the acceleration against these three axes, so we spend a slide making the frame concrete before measuring anything.

Splitting the Curvature Vector

Acceleration is curvature

For an arc-length curve, $\ddot{\mathbf{r}}=\kappa\,\mathbf{n}$ is the curvature vector. It has no component along $\mathbf{T}$.

Decomposition

$\ddot{\mathbf{r}}=\underbrace{\kappa_n\,\mathbf{N}}_{\text{normal}}+\underbrace{\kappa_g\,\mathbf{U}}_{\text{geodesic}}$

Pythagoras of curvature

$\kappa^2=\kappa_n^{\,2}+\kappa_g^{\,2}$

pp. 115-129Splitting the Curvature Vector
The acceleration of an arc-length curve is its curvature vector. Project it onto the surface normal to get normal curvature, and onto the in-surface side vector to get geodesic curvature. The Pythagorean identity binding the two pieces is the algebraic heart of the chapter, so I dwell on why the cross term vanishes.

Normal Curvature and Meusnier's Theorem

Direction, not curve

$\kappa_n$ depends only on the tangent direction. Any two curves through a point sharing a tangent share the same normal curvature.

From the second fundamental form

$\kappa_n=\mathrm{II}(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{T})=\kappa\cos\theta$

Meusnier

If the curve's osculating plane tilts by angle $\theta$ from the normal, its radius shrinks: $R=R_n\cos\theta$. Centers lie on a circle.

pp. 115-129Normal Curvature and Meusnier
Normal curvature depends only on the tangent direction, not on the particular curve carrying it, because it is read off the second fundamental form. Meusnier's theorem packages this: every curve sharing a tangent direction has its curvature center lying on a circle, with the normal section the largest. I sketch the tilted-circle picture carefully.

Geodesic Curvature: Sideways Turning

The intrinsic part

$\kappa_g$ measures how fast $\mathbf{T}$ swings sideways within the surface, ignoring the forced bending into space.

As a turning rate

$\kappa_g=\dfrac{d\varphi}{ds}$

Bending without stretching

$\kappa_g$ is unchanged when the surface is flexed isometrically. An inhabitant could measure it with rulers alone.

pp. 115-129Geodesic Curvature as Sideways Turning
Geodesic curvature is the intrinsic part: it measures how fast the tangent swings sideways within the surface, ignoring the unavoidable bending into space. Crucially it survives bending the surface without stretching, so an inhabitant of the surface could measure it with rulers alone. That intrinsic character is what makes it the right notion of straightness.

Geodesics: The Straightest Curves

Definition

$\kappa_g\equiv 0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \ddot{\mathbf{r}}\parallel \mathbf{N}$

As straight as allowed

No sideways turning at all. Acceleration points purely off the surface, so within the surface the curve never bends.

Local, not always global

Great circles are sphere geodesics; the short arc minimizes, the long arc is still geodesic but not shortest.

pp. 115-129Geodesics: The Straightest Curves
A geodesic is defined by geodesic curvature identically zero: no sideways turning at all. Such a curve is as straight as the surface permits, its acceleration pointing purely along the normal. I connect this to the great-circle examples and stress that geodesics are local, not always globally shortest, paths.

Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Measurement

Two observers, one answer

An outside observer watches $\ddot{\mathbf{r}}$ bend in space and dots it with $\mathbf{U}$. An inside observer uses only the metric.

Extrinsic reading

$\kappa_g=\ddot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{U}=\ddot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot(\mathbf{N}\times\mathbf{T})$

They must agree

The metric already encodes side-turning, so the intrinsic formula reproduces the extrinsic dot product exactly.

pp. 115-129Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Measurement
Two observers can compute geodesic curvature: an outside one watching the curve bend in space and resolving along the side vector, and an inside one using only the metric. They must agree, and that agreement is a small theorem. I walk the class through why the metric already encodes the side-turning rate.

The Sticky-Tape Construction

Tape that will not wrinkle

Press a strip of tape flat along the curve, hugging the surface. A geodesic is the path where it lies without buckling sideways.

Unroll to test

Develop (unroll) the surface onto a plane. Geodesics become honest straight lines; non-geodesics curve.

Development preserves

$\kappa_g^{\text{surface}}=\kappa^{\text{plane}}_{\text{after unrolling}}$

pp. 115-129The Sticky-Tape Construction
Lay a strip of tape flat along the curve, hugging the surface without wrinkling. A geodesic is exactly the path along which the tape lies without buckling to either side. Unrolling the surface onto a plane straightens geodesics into actual straight lines, which is the most tactile test of vanishing geodesic curvature.

Geodesics on Surfaces of Revolution

Natural coordinates

Spin a profile curve about an axis. Meridians run top to bottom; parallels are horizontal circles of radius $r$.

The key variables

$r=\text{distance to axis},\quad \psi=\angle(\text{curve},\,\text{meridian})$

Meridians are geodesics

Each meridian is a geodesic by symmetry; parallels generally are not. We seek the conserved quantity for the rest.

pp. 115-129Surfaces of Revolution
Now we specialise to surfaces of revolution, where meridians and parallels give natural coordinates. The distance from the axis is the radius function, and the angle a geodesic makes with the meridians becomes the key variable. This setting is rich enough to produce a genuine conserved quantity, which the next slide extracts.

Clairaut's Relation

Theorem (Clairaut)

$$r\,\sin\psi = \text{const along any geodesic}$$

A conservation law

Rotational symmetry yields a conserved quantity, exactly as a cyclic coordinate does in mechanics. The constant is $h=r\sin\psi$.

Since $\sin\psi\le 1$, a geodesic cannot pass where $r

pp. 115-129Clairaut's Relation
Clairaut's relation states that along any geodesic on a surface of revolution the product of the axial radius and the sine of the angle to the meridian stays constant. I derive it as a conservation law from rotational symmetry, the surface analogue of conserved angular momentum, and read off where the geodesic must turn back.

Kepler, Angular Momentum, and Clairaut

Same symmetry, same law

Rotational symmetry of the surface plays the role a central force plays for an orbit. Both give a conserved "angular" quantity.

Kepler ↔ Clairaut

$r^2\dot\phi=L \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; r\sin\psi=h$

Bound geodesics bounce

Like a bound orbit oscillating between perihelion and aphelion, a geodesic oscillates between two barrier radii.

pp. 115-129Kepler, Angular Momentum, and Clairaut
Clairaut's invariant is Kepler's second law in disguise. The rotational symmetry of the surface plays the role the central force plays for orbits, and the swept-rate constancy becomes the constancy of radius times sine of the angle. I use this analogy to predict barrier radii and the bouncing behaviour of bound geodesics.

Synthesis and Takeaways

The split

$\kappa^2=\kappa_n^2+\kappa_g^2$. Normal curvature is extrinsic and direction-fixed; geodesic curvature is intrinsic.

Geodesics

$\kappa_g\equiv 0$: the straightest curves. Tested by sticky tape and by unrolling to straight lines.

Clairaut

On surfaces of revolution, $r\sin\psi$ is conserved — Kepler's law for geodesics, with barrier radii.

pp. 115-129Synthesis and Takeaways
We collect the thread: curvature splits into normal and geodesic parts, geodesics are the zero-geodesic-curvature curves, intrinsic and extrinsic readings agree, sticky tape and unrolling test straightness, and Clairaut's relation governs surfaces of revolution. I close by previewing how geodesic curvature feeds the Gauss-Bonnet accounting ahead.
