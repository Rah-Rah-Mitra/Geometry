Gaussian Curvature

Measuring the bending of a surface from the inside out

The question

How can a flat creature confined to a surface detect its curvature using only intrinsic distances and angles?

The answer

Curvature $K$ shows up as small defects: triangles whose angles do not sum to $\pi$, circles shorter than $2\pi r$.

Three signatures

Positive on the sphere, zero on plane and cylinder, negative on a saddle.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Gaussian Curvature
This chapter spans printed pages seventeen through twenty-three and asks how a flat creature living inside a surface could measure its bending without ever stepping outside. The answer is Gaussian curvature, detected entirely from intrinsic defects in tiny triangles and circles. We open with that question and the smallest experiments that resolve it.

Extrinsic Bending vs. Intrinsic Curvature

Extrinsic

How the surface sits in space — what an outside observer sees. Bending a sheet changes this freely.

Intrinsic

What inhabitants measure with rulers inside the surface: lengths, angles, areas. Curvature lives here.

Theorema Egregium

$K$ is preserved by every isometry

A bend that stretches no distance cannot change $K$.

VDGF · pp. 17-23The Intrinsic Viewpoint
We distinguish extrinsic bending, which an outside observer sees, from intrinsic curvature, which the inhabitants can detect using only distances measured within the surface. Gauss's remarkable theorem promises that one number survives every bend that preserves lengths, so a cylinder reads as flat while a sphere does not. That number is what we hunt.

Geodesics: The Straightest Paths

Definition

A geodesic is a locally length-minimizing curve — the path an ant walks turning neither left nor right.

On the sphere

Geodesics are great circles: equators and meridians, never the smaller circles of latitude.

Why they matter

Triangles whose sides are geodesics are the rulers we use to detect curvature.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Geodesics
Before measuring defects we need straight lines on a curved surface. A geodesic is the locally length-minimizing path, the route an ant walks without turning left or right. On the sphere geodesics are great circles; on the plane they are ordinary lines. Geodesic triangles built from these arcs are the rulers of intrinsic geometry.

Angular Excess of a Geodesic Triangle

Definition · angular excess

$$E = (\alpha + \beta + \gamma) - \pi$$

Reading the sign

  • $E>0$: angles overflow $\pi$ — sphere-like
  • $E=0$: angles sum to $\pi$ — flat
  • $E<0$: angles fall short — saddle-like

Famous case

A spherical triangle with three right angles has $E = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ — it covers an octant.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Angular Excess
In the plane the three angles of a triangle sum to exactly half a turn. On a curved surface a geodesic triangle violates this: the sum exceeds pi on a sphere and falls short on a saddle. We name the difference the angular excess E, and it is positive, zero, or negative according to the sign of the curvature beneath it.

The Defining Relation: $E \approx K\,A$

Local Gauss law

$$E \;=\; \iint_{\triangle} K\, dA \;\approx\; K \cdot A$$

Curvature as a density

$K$ is excess per unit area. Shrinking the triangle isolates $K$ at one point:

Pointwise definition

$$K(p) \;=\; \lim_{A\to 0}\frac{E}{A}$$

VDGF · pp. 17-23E = K A
Gauss's local law states that the angular excess of a small geodesic triangle equals the curvature times the enclosed area. Dividing excess by area and shrinking the triangle isolates K at a single point. This makes curvature a density of excess per unit area, the central definition the rest of the chapter unpacks and tests.

Positive Curvature: The Sphere

Constant curvature

$$K = \frac{1}{R^2} \;>\; 0$$

Bulging triangles

Geodesic sides bow outward, every angle widens, and the angle sum exceeds $\pi$.

Scale check

A larger radius $R$ curves more gently — $K$ falls off like $1/R^2$, so big spheres look nearly flat locally.

VDGF · pp. 17-23The Sphere
On a sphere of radius R the curvature is one over R squared, constant and positive everywhere. Geodesic triangles bulge outward, their angles overflowing pi, and a triangle with three right angles really does close up over an octant. Larger spheres curve more gently, exactly as the inverse-square radius dependence predicts for the excess we measure.

Zero Curvature: Plane and Cylinder

Flat verdict

$$K \equiv 0, \qquad E = 0, \qquad C = 2\pi r$$

The surprise

A cylinder is intrinsically flat. Rolling stretches nothing, so every triangle closes at exactly $\pi$.

Lesson

Flatness is intrinsic. You cannot tell a cylinder from a plane by experiments done inside the surface.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Plane and Cylinder
The plane has zero curvature, and so, surprisingly, does the cylinder. Rolling a flat sheet into a tube bends it in space but stretches no distances, so every intrinsic experiment returns the Euclidean answer. Triangles close at exactly pi and small circles have circumference two pi r. Flatness is intrinsic, not a matter of looking straight.

Negative Curvature: The Saddle

Opposite bending

$$K \;=\; \kappa_1\kappa_2 \;<\; 0$$

Pinched triangles

Sides cave inward, angles narrow, the sum drops below $\pi$, and the excess $E$ is negative.

Geodesics diverge

Nearby straight paths spread apart faster than in the plane. The pseudosphere has constant $K<0$.

VDGF · pp. 17-23The Saddle
At a saddle point the surface curves up in one direction and down in the orthogonal one, giving negative Gaussian curvature. Geodesic triangles pinch inward so their angle sum falls below pi, the excess is negative, and nearby geodesics spread apart faster than in the plane. The pseudosphere realises constant negative curvature throughout.

Circles and Disks as Curvature Detectors

Circumference defect

$$C(r) = 2\pi r\left(1 - \frac{K r^2}{6} + \cdots\right)$$

Area defect

$$A(r) = \pi r^2\left(1 - \frac{K r^2}{12} + \cdots\right)$$

How to read it

$K>0$ makes circles shorter than $2\pi r$; $K<0$ makes them longer. Solve for $K$ from the shortfall.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Circle Detectors
A geodesic circle of intrinsic radius r need not have Euclidean circumference. Expanding to third order, the circumference is two pi r reduced by a term proportional to K r cubed, and the disk area shrinks by a term in K r to the fourth. Measuring how much a small circle falls short of two pi r reads off the curvature directly.

The Shrinking-Triangle Curvature Meter

Procedure

Lay down ever smaller geodesic triangles at $p$ and record the ratio $E/A$ for each.

Convergence

$$\frac{E}{A}\Big|_{\text{small}} \;\longrightarrow\; K(p)$$

Why a limit

Any single triangle reports only an average curvature. The limit pins the value at the point.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Curvature Meter
To pin K at a point we let a geodesic triangle shrink toward it and watch the ratio of excess to area. Each individual triangle gives only an average, but the ratio stabilizes to a definite limit as the triangle collapses. That stable limit is the pointwise Gaussian curvature, and the convergence is the practical content of the defining relation.

Local Gauss–Bonnet: Excess Adds Up

Additivity

Subdivide a region into geodesic triangles. Each shared interior edge contributes to two triangles with opposite orientation and cancels.

From local to integral

$$\sum_i E_i \;=\; \iint_{\Omega} K\, dA$$

The bridge

Pointwise curvature density integrates to total curvature — the seed of the global theorem.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Local Gauss-Bonnet
Excess is additive: tile a region with small geodesic triangles and the interior edges cancel in pairs, so the total excess equals the sum of the pieces. In the limit this becomes the integral of K over the region. Local Gauss-Bonnet thus turns the pointwise density into total curvature, the bridge from local defects to global theorems.

Synthesis: The Curvature Ledger

$K>0$ — sphere

Angle surplus, $E>0$, circles short of $2\pi r$, geodesics converge.

$K=0$ — plane / cylinder

Euclidean answers: $E=0$, $C=2\pi r$, parallel geodesics stay parallel.

$K<0$ — saddle

Angle deficit, $E<0$, circles longer than $2\pi r$, geodesics diverge.

Why it matters

$K$ is the isometry invariant; integrating its density opens the global Gauss–Bonnet theorem.

VDGF · pp. 17-23Synthesis
We close by collecting the three signatures into one ledger: positive curvature gives angle surplus and deficient circles, zero gives Euclidean answers, negative gives angle deficit and excess circumference. Curvature is the invariant that survives every isometry, measurable from the inside, and integrating its density prepares the global Gauss-Bonnet theorem to come.
