Warm-Up: Ultimate Equality for the Cube

Problem

Differentiate $f(x)=x^3$ by Newton's ultimate-equality argument, keeping every discarded term visible.

The chord

$$\frac{(x+h)^3-x^3}{h}=3x^2+3xh+h^2$$

The limit

As $h\to0$ the terms $3xh$ and $h^2$ become ultimately negligible, so $f'(x)=3x^2$.

pp. 24-28Warm-Up: Ultimate Equality for the Cube
We start with Newton's ultimate-equality idea applied to the cube. The secant ratio is a finite chord that we shrink until the discarded terms vanish in the limit. Keeping the bookkeeping visible shows precisely which pieces survive and why the answer equals three x squared.

Angular Excess, Made Precise

Definition

For a triangle bounded by geodesics, the excess is the angle sum minus the flat value $\pi$.

Excess

$$E=(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)-\pi$$

Reading the sign

$E>0$ on a sphere, $E=0$ on the plane, $E<0$ on a saddle.

pp. 24-28Angular Excess, Made Precise
Here we make angular excess precise. A geodesic triangle has interior angles whose sum exceeds pi on a positively curved surface. The excess is the deviation from the flat sum, and the local Gauss-Bonnet statement equates it to the total curvature captured inside the triangle's boundary.

Worked Problem: The Spherical Octant

Problem

Three great-circle arcs meet at right angles, bounding one octant of the sphere. Find the angle sum and excess.

Compute

$$\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\;\Rightarrow\;E=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}-\pi=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$$

Cross-check

One octant is $\tfrac18$ of the sphere's $4\pi$ total curvature, namely $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ — the excess matches.

pp. 24-28Worked Problem: The Spherical Octant
Our flagship computation is the triple-right-angled triangle on a sphere. Three mutually perpendicular great-circle arcs bound one octant. Each corner is a right angle, so the angle sum is three halves pi and the excess is exactly pi over two, matching one eighth of the total sphere.

Excess Equals Area Times Curvature

General law

On a sphere of radius $R$ the excess of any geodesic triangle equals its area over $R^2$.

Spherical excess

$$E=\frac{A}{R^2}=K\,A,\qquad K=\frac{1}{R^2}$$

Octant check

Area $A=\tfrac18(4\pi R^2)=\tfrac{\pi R^2}{2}$, so $E=A/R^2=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$. Consistent.

pp. 24-28Excess Equals Area Times Curvature
We generalize the octant result to any spherical triangle. The excess equals the enclosed area divided by the radius squared, which is the area times the constant curvature. Checking the octant against this formula confirms the bookkeeping and ties angle defect directly to measurable area.

Sorting Surfaces by Curvature Sign

Sphere — $K>0$

Triangles bulge, geodesics reconverge, excess is positive.

Plane / cylinder — $K=0$

Angle sums stay at $\pi$; the surface rolls flat without stretching.

Saddle — $K<0$

Triangles pinch inward, geodesics diverge, excess is negative.

pp. 24-28Sorting Surfaces by Curvature Sign
Next we sort three model surfaces by curvature sign. The sphere closes triangles with positive excess, the plane and the rolled cylinder keep angle sums at pi, and the saddle spreads triangles into angle deficit. Sign, not magnitude, decides which non-Euclidean world we inhabit.

Worked Problem: The Cylinder Is Intrinsically Flat

Problem

A cylinder looks curved. Show that a surface dweller measuring triangles finds $K=0$.

Key move

Cut and unroll the cylinder onto a flat sheet. No distance or angle changes — the map is an isometry.

Conclusion

$$K_{\text{cyl}}=0,\qquad E=\iint K\,dA=0$$

pp. 24-28Worked Problem: The Cylinder Is Intrinsically Flat
This exercise confronts the common error that a cylinder is curved. Intrinsically it is flat: unrolling it onto paper preserves every distance and angle, so its Gaussian curvature is zero. The lesson is that bending in space is extrinsic and invisible to a surface dweller measuring triangles.

Worked Problem: Pythagorean Triples on the Circle

Problem

Construct Pythagorean triples geometrically from rational points on the unit circle.

Sweep a rational slope $t$

$$\left(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\;\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\right)$$

Clear denominators

With $t=\tfrac{m}{n}$ this gives $(n^2-m^2,\,2mn,\,n^2+m^2)$ — a triple such as $3,4,5$.

pp. 24-28Worked Problem: Pythagorean Triples on the Circle
We revisit Euclidean geometry through Pythagoras. Sweeping a line of rational slope from a fixed point on the unit circle produces a second rational point, and clearing denominators yields a Pythagorean triple. This makes the algebra of right triangles a geometric construction the reader can rerun.

Measuring Curvature From the Inside

Intrinsic recipe

Lay a small geodesic triangle, sum its angles, subtract $\pi$, divide by area.

Pointwise curvature

$$K(p)=\lim_{A\to0}\frac{(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)-\pi}{A}$$

No embedding needed

A surface dweller measures $K$ from angles and areas alone, never leaving the surface.

pp. 24-28Measuring Curvature From the Inside
Now we invert the excess law to measure curvature intrinsically. Lay down a small geodesic triangle, sum its angles, subtract pi, and divide by the area. As the triangle shrinks the ratio converges to the Gaussian curvature at the point, needing no embedding in space.

Worked Problem: A Tessellation Excess Ledger

Problem

Tile a sphere with geodesic triangles and add up every face's excess. What total appears?

Sum over faces

$$\sum_{\text{faces}} E_i=\iint_{S^2} K\,dA=4\pi$$

Why $4\pi$

Total curvature equals $2\pi\chi$ with $\chi(S^2)=2$ — Gauss-Bonnet as an additive ledger.

pp. 24-28Worked Problem: A Tessellation Excess Ledger
We scale up to a tessellation, summing the excess of every spherical face. Each face contributes its own angle defect, and the column total recovers four pi, the integral of curvature over the whole sphere. This is Gauss-Bonnet appearing as a simple additive ledger.
