Across printed pages 51 to 64 the chapter asks how an abstract plane of constant negative curvature can be made tangible. Beltrami answered with a real surface, the pseudosphere, on which lengths and angles obey hyperbolic rules, turning a logical possibility into something we can draw and measure.

The Tractrix: A Curve of Constant Tangent

The dragged weight

Pull a stone on a taut string while the free end walks straight along an axis.

Defining property

The tangent segment between the curve and the axis always has the same length $a$.

Parametrization

$x = a\!\left(\ln\tan\tfrac{t}{2} + \cos t\right),\; y = a\sin t$

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64The Tractrix
The tractrix is the path of a weight dragged on a taut string whose free end walks the axis. Its defining feature is that the tangent segment cut off by the axis has constant length. That single property fixes the curve and seeds the whole construction of the pseudosphere later in this chapter.

Building the Pseudosphere

Surface of revolution

Spin the tractrix about its asymptote; the swept trumpet is the pseudosphere.

Opposite of the sphere

The sphere bulges out with positive curvature; this surface saddles inward.

Why it matters

It is the first concrete surface realizing the hyperbolic plane locally.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64Building the Pseudosphere
Revolving the tractrix about its asymptote sweeps out the pseudosphere. Where a sphere bulges outward with equal positive curvature everywhere, this trumpet curls the two principal directions in opposite senses, producing constant negative Gaussian curvature. It is the southern twin of the sphere and the first honest model of hyperbolic geometry.

Constant Negative Curvature

Principal curvatures

The meridian and the parallel bend in opposite senses at every point.

Gauss curvature

$K = \kappa_1\kappa_2 = -\dfrac{1}{a^2}$

Set $a=1$

Then $K=-1$ everywhere: the intrinsic constant we track through every model.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64Constant Negative Curvature
On the pseudosphere the meridian bends one way while the parallel bends the other, so the product of principal curvatures is negative and constant. Setting the radius to one gives Gaussian curvature exactly minus one. This is the intrinsic signature the chapter tracks across every model that follows.

Conformal Coordinates Unroll the Surface

One scaling factor

Pick coordinates that stretch the surface by the same amount in all directions.

Conformal metric

$ds^2 = \dfrac{dx^2 + dy^2}{y^2}$

Angles survive

A common factor preserves angles; only lengths are rescaled by $1/y$.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64Conformal Coordinates
Choosing coordinates that scale the surface uniformly at each point turns the pseudosphere metric into a flat metric multiplied by a single conformal factor. Because the factor is one over the height squared, angles are preserved while lengths are rescaled, and the whole surface unrolls onto a strip of the upper half plane.

The Beltrami-Poincare Half-Plane

The arena

All points with $y>0$; the real axis $y=0$ is the boundary at infinity.

Metric

$ds = \dfrac{\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}}{y}$

Boundary is far

As $y\to 0$ a tiny step costs unbounded hyperbolic length, so $y=0$ is unreachable.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64The Beltrami-Poincare Half-Plane
The upper half plane carries the metric in which Euclidean lengths are divided by the height above the boundary. Near the real axis a tiny Euclidean step costs an enormous hyperbolic distance, so the boundary sits infinitely far away. This single rescaling rule encodes all of hyperbolic trigonometry.

Equal Hyperbolic Discs, Unequal Pictures

Same radius

Two discs of equal hyperbolic radius look very different in Euclidean size.

Distance along a vertical

$d\big((x,y_1),(x,y_2)\big) = \left|\ln\dfrac{y_2}{y_1}\right|$

Read it off

Distance counts ratios of heights, not differences, so the boundary recedes forever.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64Hyperbolic Distance and Discs
Two figures that look wildly different in Euclidean size can have the same hyperbolic radius once we divide by height. A disc drawn high in the half plane is hyperbolically the same as a tiny disc near the axis. The model stretches the eye while the intrinsic measurement stays faithful.

Geodesics: Semicircles and Vertical Rays

Two shapes

Hyperbolic straight lines are vertical rays and semicircles centred on the axis.

Right-angle meeting

Each geodesic hits the boundary $y=0$ orthogonally.

Optical reading (Snell)

$\dfrac{\sin\theta}{v(y)} = \text{const},\quad v(y)\propto y$

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64Geodesics as Semicircles
Shortest paths in the half plane are vertical rays and semicircles centred on the real axis, each meeting the boundary at a right angle. Light in a medium whose speed grows with height would bend along exactly these arcs, so Snell's law gives an optical reading of hyperbolic straight lines.

The Angle of Parallelism

The limiting ray

From a point off a line, one ray just fails to ever meet it: the parallel ray.

Lobachevsky formula

$\Pi(d) = 2\arctan\!\big(e^{-d}\big)$

Less than a right angle

The angle $\Pi(d)$ shrinks toward $0$ as the perpendicular distance $d$ grows.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64The Angle of Parallelism
Drop a perpendicular of hyperbolic length from a point to a line. The limiting ray that just fails to meet the line makes an angle strictly less than a right angle, shrinking as the distance grows. This angle of parallelism is the quantitative heart of how parallels fail in hyperbolic space.

The Beltrami-Poincare Disc

Compress to a disc

A Mobius map carries the half-plane onto the open unit disc.

Disc metric

$ds = \dfrac{2\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}}{1-(x^2+y^2)}$

Geodesics

Diameters and arcs that strike the boundary circle at right angles.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64The Beltrami-Poincare Disc
A Mobius map carries the half plane onto the open unit disc, where the metric divides Euclidean length by one minus the squared radius. Geodesics become diameters and circular arcs that hit the boundary circle orthogonally, and the whole infinite plane is compressed into a finite, beautifully symmetric picture.

Two Models, One Geometry

Half-plane

Boundary is a line; great for horocycles and boundary computations.

Disc

Boundary is a circle; great for rotational symmetry and tilings.

The bridge map

$w = \dfrac{z-i}{z+i}$

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64Comparing the Two Models
Half plane and disc are the same hyperbolic geometry in different clothing, joined by a conformal Mobius map that preserves angles and distances. Choose the half plane for boundary computations and horocycles, choose the disc for rotational symmetry and tilings. Each model lights up a different feature of one surface.

Synthesis and Outlook

What we built

Tractrix → pseudosphere → conformal chart → half-plane → disc.

The invariant

Constant curvature $K=-1$ is what every model preserves.

Visual Differential Geometry and Forms · pp. 51-64Synthesis and Outlook
The pseudosphere made hyperbolic geometry measurable, conformal coordinates exported it to the plane, and the two Poincare models gave clean arenas for computation. Constant negative curvature is the single invariant linking them all. Next we let curvature vary from point to point and build the full machinery of Riemannian metrics.
