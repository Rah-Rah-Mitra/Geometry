Mapping Surfaces: The Metric

The first fundamental form: the data that lets a flat page remember a curved surface

The question

What must a map carry so that lengths, angles, and curvature can be recovered?

The answer

The metric $ds^2 = E\,du^2 + 2F\,du\,dv + G\,dv^2$, encoding intrinsic distance.

The price

No flat chart of a sphere keeps both lengths and angles. We study the distortion.

Coordinates Are Labels; Length Is Geometry

A chart is a parametrization

A surface point is $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$. The pair $(u,v)$ is a name, not a distance.

The trap

Equal steps $du$ in the chart can mean very different physical lengths at different points.

What we need

A rule converting a coordinate displacement $(du,dv)$ into a measured length $ds$.

Coordinate tangents

$\mathbf{r}_u = \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial u}, \quad \mathbf{r}_v = \dfrac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial v}$

Displacement

$d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_u\,du + \mathbf{r}_v\,dv$

From here

Squaring $d\mathbf{r}$ produces exactly three local coefficients. That square is the metric.

Printed pp. 31-50The Chapter Question
A coordinate grid is only a set of labels. The geometric content lives in how those labels convert into measured length. We motivate the metric by drawing one coordinate cell and asking how its true side lengths and corner angle depend on position, which already forces three local coefficients on us.

The First Fundamental Form

The line element

$ds^2 = E\,du^2 + 2F\,du\,dv + G\,dv^2$

The coefficients

$E = \mathbf{r}_u\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}_u,\;\; F = \mathbf{r}_u\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}_v,\;\; G = \mathbf{r}_v\!\cdot\!\mathbf{r}_v$

Intrinsic

$ds^2$ depends only on dot products of tangents, so it is felt from inside the surface.

Printed pp. 31-50The Line Element
The first fundamental form packages all intrinsic measurement into one quadratic expression. Given a coordinate displacement, it returns the squared physical length. E, F, and G are dot products of the coordinate tangent vectors, so they vary from point to point and encode the entire shape of the surface as felt from inside.

What Each Coefficient Means

$E$ — speed along $u$

$|\mathbf{r}_u| = \sqrt{E}$ is the physical length of a unit step in $u$ while $v$ is held fixed.

$G$ — speed along $v$

$|\mathbf{r}_v| = \sqrt{G}$ is the length of a unit step in $v$ while $u$ is held fixed.

$F$ — tilt of the grid

$F = \sqrt{EG}\,\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the coordinate curves.

Orthogonal chart

$F = 0 \iff \mathbf{r}_u \perp \mathbf{r}_v$

Printed pp. 31-50Reading E, F, G
Each coefficient has a concrete job. E and G are squared speeds along the two coordinate curves, while F measures their non-orthogonality. When F vanishes the grid is locally rectangular. We tie every coefficient to a side or angle of the small coordinate parallelogram so the formula stops being abstract bookkeeping.

Length, Angle, Area From E, F, G

Arc length

$L = \displaystyle\int \sqrt{E\,\dot u^2 + 2F\,\dot u\dot v + G\,\dot v^2}\,dt$

Angle between curves

$\cos\theta = \dfrac{F}{\sqrt{EG}}$

Area element

$dA = \sqrt{EG - F^2}\,\,du\,dv$

The payoff

All intrinsic geometry of the surface is computable from three functions of position, without ever leaving the chart.

Curvature preview

Even the Gaussian curvature $K$ can be written from $E, F, G$ and their derivatives alone (Gauss's Theorema Egregium).

Why it matters

A map that records $E, F, G$ at every point loses nothing about distance, angle, or area.

Printed pp. 31-50Measuring with the Metric
Once E, F, and G are known, arc length, angle, and area follow by integration without ever leaving the chart. We show the arc-length integral along a curve, the angle formula between coordinate directions, and the area element. This is the payoff: all intrinsic geometry is computable from three functions of position.

Seeing Distortion: Tissot's Indicatrix

The idea

Send a tiny circle of unit directions through the metric. It comes out an ellipse.

The axes

The semi-axes are the principal stretch factors of the map at that point.

The reading

Round ellipse = faithful local shape. Elongated ellipse = anisotropic stretch.

Printed pp. 31-50Tissot's Indicatrix
To see distortion at a glance we map a tiny circle of directions through the metric and watch it become an ellipse. The ellipse axes are the principal stretch factors. A round indicatrix means faithful local shape; an elongated one exposes anisotropy. This single picture summarizes how a map deforms the neighborhood of each point.

Conformal Maps: Angles Survive

Conformal metric

$ds^2 = \Lambda(u,v)^2\,(du^2 + dv^2)$

Equal scaling

Every direction is stretched by the same factor $\Lambda$, so angles between curves are untouched.

Round indicatrix

Conformal $\iff E = G,\; F = 0$. Tissot's ellipse stays a circle of radius $\Lambda$.

The cost

Areas scale as $\Lambda^2$, so a conformal map of a sphere cannot keep areas honest.

Printed pp. 31-50Conformal Maps
A conformal map preserves angles by scaling every direction equally, so the metric reduces to a single position-dependent factor times the flat form. Tissot's indicatrix stays a circle, only its radius changes. Conformality is the strongest faithfulness a flat map of a curved surface can hope for, and it costs us honest areas.

Stereographic Projection of the Sphere

Construction

From the north pole $N$, draw a ray through a sphere point $P$ to where it pierces the equatorial plane.

Conformal scale

$ds^2 = \dfrac{4}{(1+r^2)^2}\,(dx^2 + dy^2)$

Behavior

The scale factor blows up as $P \to N$; the north pole goes to infinity.

Key fact

The projection is conformal everywhere except, by limit, at the pole itself.

Printed pp. 31-50Stereographic Projection
Projecting the sphere from its north pole onto the tangent plane gives the cleanest conformal chart. We derive the projection geometrically by similar triangles, read off the conformal scale factor, and note that it blows up near the pole. The construction makes the angle-preserving property visible rather than merely asserted.

Stereographic Projection Preserves Circles

The miracle

Every circle on the sphere maps to a circle or a straight line in the plane.

Why a circle

A sphere circle is the sphere cut by a plane; its projection is a conic the conformal scaling forces to close.

The line case

A circle through the pole $N$ projects to a straight line — a circle of infinite radius.

Printed pp. 31-50Circles Map to Circles
The signature miracle of stereographic projection is that every circle on the sphere maps to a circle or a straight line in the plane. We sketch why a circle, as the intersection of the sphere with a plane, projects to a conic that the conformal scaling forces to close up into a round circle, with great circles through the pole becoming lines.

Mercator: Conformality on a Cylinder

Goal

Wrap the sphere onto a cylinder so compass bearings (rhumb lines) become straight lines.

Conformal spacing

$y(\phi) = \displaystyle\int_0^{\phi} \sec\phi'\,d\phi' = \ln\!\big|\sec\phi + \tan\phi\big|$

The bargain returns

Angles are honest, but vertical stretching makes polar areas inflate without bound.

Printed pp. 31-50The Mercator Map
Mercator stretches the stereographic idea into a cylinder so that rhumb lines become straight. Demanding conformality on a cylindrical chart fixes the vertical spacing as an integral of the secant of latitude. The same conformal bargain returns: angles and compass bearings are honest, but areas near the poles inflate without bound.

Why Every Flat Map Must Lie

Theorema Egregium (preview)

$K_{\text{sphere}} = \tfrac{1}{R^2} \neq 0 = K_{\text{plane}}$

The obstruction

Gaussian curvature is intrinsic. A length-preserving (isometric) map would have to match $K$ — impossible here.

So choose

Keep angles (conformal: Mercator, stereographic) or keep areas (equal-area) — never both.

Printed pp. 31-50The Inevitable Distortion
Because the sphere has curvature and the plane has none, no chart can preserve both lengths and angles at once. Theorema Egregium is the deep reason, previewed here through the metric. Every world map therefore lies in some controlled way, and choosing a projection means choosing which quantity you are willing to sacrifice.

Recap: The Metric Is the Cargo

1. The metric

$ds^2 = E\,du^2 + 2F\,du\,dv + G\,dv^2$ is the intrinsic data a faithful map must transport.

2. It computes everything

Length, angle ($\cos\theta = F/\sqrt{EG}$), and area ($\sqrt{EG-F^2}$) all follow from three functions.

3. Conformal trade

Equal scaling $\Lambda^2(du^2+dv^2)$ keeps angles but sacrifices area.

4. Stereographic

Realizes that trade on the sphere; circles stay circles, the pole flies to infinity.

One-line takeaway

$\text{geometry} = \text{metric},\;\; \text{not the embedding}$

Looking ahead

Theorema Egregium will be proved from $E, F, G$ alone, turning curvature into a purely intrinsic measurement.

The metric, not the embedding, is what a faithful map must transport.
