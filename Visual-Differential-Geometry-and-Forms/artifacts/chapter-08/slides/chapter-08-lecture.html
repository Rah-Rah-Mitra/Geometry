What Should Curvature Measure?

Three demands fix the definition before any formula

Lines are flat

A straight line must register zero bending everywhere.

Tighter is more

A small circle should score higher than a wide circle.

Speed blind

The number is geometric: it ignores how fast we trace the path.

Dimensional clue

$$[\kappa] = \frac{1}{\text{length}}$$

Curvature is reciprocal length, so a radius $R$ must appear.

Target

Build one number $\kappa$ that satisfies all three demands at once.

pp. 97-105What Should Curvature Measure?
Before any formula we agree on what we want. A straight line should have curvature zero, a tight circle more curvature than a wide one, and the number should not care how fast a particle traces the path. These three demands already force curvature to be one over a length, the geometry of the local bend.

Curvature as a Steering Force

Needham's physical picture: bend a unit-speed point

Force = curvature

$$\mathbf{F} = \kappa\,\mathbf{n}, \qquad |\mathbf{F}| = \kappa$$

Why it fits

Straight motion needs no force, so $\kappa = 0$. A tight turn demands a hard inward push, so $\kappa$ is large.

pp. 97-105Curvature as a Steering Force
Needham's first picture is physical. A point of unit mass moving at unit speed feels a force perpendicular to its motion that bends the trajectory. The amount of inward force required to hold the point on the curve is exactly the curvature. Straight motion needs no force, so its curvature vanishes, matching intuition.

The Osculating Circle

Three points through a circle, then let them merge

Construction

Three nearby points determine a unique circle. Slide the outer two toward the middle point $P$.

Limiting circle

$$\text{circle}(P_-,P,P_+)\;\longrightarrow\;\text{osculating circle at }P$$

Contact

The limit touches the curve to second order: same tangent and same bending.

pp. 97-105The Osculating Circle
Take three nearby points on the curve and pass a circle through them. As the outer two slide into the middle one, this circle settles onto a single limiting circle that kisses the curve. Its radius R is the radius of curvature, and the curvature is its reciprocal. The circle sits on the concave side.

Curvature Equals One Over R

The fundamental formula of plane-curve curvature

Calibration circle

A circle of radius $R$ bends the same way at every point, so its curvature is constant.

Match the curve

The osculating circle bends exactly like the curve at $P$, so they share the same $\kappa$.

Definition

$$\kappa \;=\; \frac{1}{R}$$

$R$ is the radius of the osculating circle; large $R$ means gentle bending.

pp. 97-105Curvature Equals One Over R
For a circle of radius R the definition must give a constant, since the bend is the same everywhere. The osculating circle of any curve is the circle whose bending matches the curve at that instant, so the curve and circle share tangent and second order contact. Hence the fundamental formula kappa equals one over R.

Tangent Turning Along the Arc

Curvature is the turning rate of the tangent

Turning rate

$$\kappa \;=\; \frac{d\phi}{ds}$$

Reading it

A sharp bend swings the tangent a lot over a short arc, so $d\phi/ds$ is large there.

Consistency

On a circle of radius $R$, one full lap turns the tangent by $2\pi$ over length $2\pi R$, giving $\kappa = 1/R$.

pp. 97-105Tangent Turning Along the Arc
Walk along the curve and watch the tangent direction. Its angle phi changes as you advance. Curvature is the rate at which that angle turns per unit length travelled, the derivative of phi with respect to arc length s. A sharp bend turns the tangent quickly over a short arc, so kappa is large there.

Arc Length and the Unit Tangent

Move at unit speed so the tangent can only rotate

Arc-length chart

Reparametrize by $s$ so $|\mathbf{r}'(s)| = 1$. The point advances one unit of length per unit of $s$.

Unit tangent

$$\mathbf{T}(s) = \mathbf{r}'(s), \qquad |\mathbf{T}| = 1$$

Frenet relation

$$\mathbf{T}'(s) = \kappa\,\mathbf{n}, \qquad \mathbf{T}'\perp\mathbf{T}$$

pp. 97-105Arc Length and the Unit Tangent
Reparametrize by arc length so the point moves at unit speed. Then the tangent vector T has constant length one and can only rotate, never stretch. Its derivative with respect to s is perpendicular to T and points toward the centre of the osculating circle. The length of that derivative is the curvature.

Newton's Vanishing Triangle

Curvature from an infinitesimal similar triangle

The triangle

Over a tiny arc $\delta s$ the tangent swings by $\delta\phi$. Two radii to the centre subtend the same angle.

Arc subtends angle

$$\delta s \approx R\,\delta\phi \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \frac{\delta\phi}{\delta s}\to\frac{1}{R}$$

Limit survives

As the arc shrinks, the surviving ratio is exactly $\kappa = d\phi/ds = 1/R$.

pp. 97-105Newton's Vanishing Triangle
Newton derived curvature from a tiny triangle. Over an arc of length delta-s the tangent swings by a small angle delta-phi. The chord, the change in tangent, and the radius form similar triangles that collapse as the arc shrinks. In the limit the surviving ratio delta-phi over delta-s is precisely the curvature, one over R.

Reading the Limit Carefully

Which pieces vanish, and which survive

First order matters

Chord and arc agree to first order in $\delta s$; their difference is higher order and is discarded.

The kept ratio

Both $\delta\phi$ and $\delta s$ are first order, so their quotient has a finite, nonzero limit.

Order bookkeeping

$$\text{arc} - \text{chord} = O(\delta s^{3}), \qquad \frac{\delta\phi}{\delta s} = O(1)$$

Lesson

Every infinitesimal curvature argument is an exercise in tracking orders: keep the leading terms, drop the rest.

pp. 97-105Reading the Limit Carefully
The triangle argument only works because the discarded pieces vanish faster than the kept ones. The chord and the arc agree to first order, and the angle equals the arc over the radius for a circle. Tracking which quantities are first order and which are higher order is the heart of every infinitesimal curvature computation.

Signed Curvature and Orientation

Give the bend a direction in the plane

Sign convention

Counterclockwise turning is positive, clockwise negative, relative to the oriented tangent.

Signed and unsigned

$$\kappa = \frac{d\phi}{ds}, \qquad |\kappa| = \frac{1}{R}$$

Inflection

Where the curve switches sides the osculating circle flips and $\kappa$ passes through zero.

pp. 97-105Signed Curvature and Orientation
In the plane we can give curvature a sign by choosing a sense of turning. Counterclockwise bending is positive, clockwise negative, and an inflection where the curve switches sides has curvature zero. The unsigned magnitude is one over R, while the sign records which way the osculating circle lies relative to the oriented tangent.

Worked Example: The Tractrix

Turn the definition into a measurement

Setup

The tractrix is traced by a weight dragged on a taut string of length $a$ whose free end slides along the axis.

Defining property

$$\text{tangent length to axis} = a\ \text{(constant)}$$

Payoff

Applying $\kappa = d\phi/ds$ to this curve yields an explicit curvature we can verify numerically in the notebook lab.

pp. 97-105Worked Example: The Tractrix
The tractrix is the path of a weight dragged on a taut string of fixed length whose end moves along a line. Its defining property is that the tangent segment from the curve to the axis always has the same length. Computing its curvature turns the abstract definition into a concrete measurement we can check numerically.

Summary: Three Faces of Curvature

One number, measured three ways

Force

The inward push on a unit-speed point: $|\mathbf{F}| = \kappa$.

Osculating circle

The reciprocal of the best-fit radius: $\kappa = 1/R$.

Tangent turning

The turning rate of the tangent: $\kappa = d\phi/ds$.

They agree

$$|\mathbf{F}| \;=\; \frac{1}{R} \;=\; \frac{d\phi}{ds} \;=\; \kappa$$

Looking ahead

This single bend number is the seed of curvature on surfaces, where two principal directions each carry their own $\kappa$.

pp. 97-105Summary: Three Faces of Curvature
We met one quantity wearing three masks: the steering force on a unit-speed point, the reciprocal radius of the osculating circle, and the turning rate of the tangent. They agree because each measures the same local bend. This single number kappa is the foundation for the curvature of surfaces studied next.
