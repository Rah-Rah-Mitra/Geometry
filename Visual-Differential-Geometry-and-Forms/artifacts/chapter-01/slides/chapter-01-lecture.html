What changes when straightness is intrinsic rather than Euclidean?

Flat plane

Euclid and the Fifth Postulate

Four that feel obvious

Draw a segment; extend it; circle of any center and radius; all right angles are equal.

One that feels like a theorem

The fifth postulate is longer and less self-evident, so geometers spent two millennia trying to prove it from the other four.

Euclid's fifth (original form)

If a line crossing two lines makes interior angles on one side summing to less than $180^\circ$, the two lines meet on that side.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Euclid and the Fifth Postulate
Euclid's first four postulates feel inevitable, but the fifth, the parallel postulate, always read like a theorem waiting to be proved. For two thousand years geometers tried to derive it and failed. That stubborn failure, not any success, is what eventually cracked open spherical and hyperbolic geometry as honest alternatives.

Playfair's Restatement: The Dial We Will Turn

Playfair's axiom

Through a point $P$ not on a line $\ell$, there is exactly $\mathbf{1}$ line through $P$ that never meets $\ell$.

Turn the count

0 parallels → spherical geometry.
1 parallel → Euclidean geometry.
$\infty$ parallels → hyperbolic geometry.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45The Parallel Postulate Restated
Playfair's clean restatement says exactly one line through an outside point misses a given line. Phrasing it this way isolates the single assumption we can vary. Keep it and you stay Euclidean; allow zero such lines and you get the sphere; allow infinitely many and you fall into the hyperbolic plane.

Geodesics: Redefining "Straight" Intrinsically

Two equivalent meanings

A geodesic is locally shortest between nearby points, and equally the path that never turns left or right as you walk it.

Locally shortest

$\displaystyle L[\gamma]=\int_a^b \lVert \gamma'(t)\rVert\,dt \;\to\; \text{minimum}$

On the sphere

The straightest paths are great circles, circles whose plane passes through the centre.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Geodesics: Straightest Paths
On a curved surface the word straight has to be redefined intrinsically. A geodesic is the path an ant walks without ever turning left or right, and equivalently the locally shortest route between nearby points. Great circles play this role on the sphere, so they are the true straight lines of spherical geometry.

Spherical Geometry: Zero Parallels

Every pair meets

Any two distinct great circles intersect in two antipodal points. There is no way to draw a great circle that avoids another.

Consequence

Number of lines through $P$ missing $\ell$ $= 0$.

Why flights look curved

The shortest route hugs a great circle, so it bows away from the straight-on-the-map latitude.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45The Sphere: No Parallels
Any two distinct great circles must intersect, in fact at two antipodal points, so the sphere admits no parallel lines at all. This is the geometry of zero parallels. The familiar lines of latitude are not geodesics, which is exactly why airplanes fly curved-looking routes that are actually the straightest available.

Spherical Triangles Bulge: Angle Sum Exceeds $180^\circ$

A triple-right triangle

Put one vertex at the north pole and two on the equator a quarter-turn apart. Each corner is a right angle, so the sum is $270^\circ$.

Angular excess

$E = (\alpha+\beta+\gamma)-\pi \;>\; 0$

Reading the picture

The corners flare outward because the surface curves away beneath the triangle: positive curvature.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Spherical Triangles Bulge Outward
Build a triangle from three great-circle arcs and its angles overflow. A triangle with a vertex at the pole and two on the equator already shows three right angles summing to two hundred seventy degrees. The corners push outward because the surface itself curves away beneath them, a visible fingerprint of positive curvature.

Harriot's Theorem: Excess Equals Area

Harriot / Girard (unit sphere)

$\text{Area}(\triangle) = (\alpha+\beta+\gamma) - \pi = E$

Lune bookkeeping

A lune of angle $\theta$ has area $2\theta$. Three lunes from the triangle's corners cover the hemisphere with the triangle counted thrice; subtracting the surplus leaves $2(\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi)$ for a triangle pair.

Big idea

Excess is curvature integrated over the enclosed region, not a measurement error.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Angular Excess Measures Area
Harriot's beautiful result turns geometry into bookkeeping: cut the sphere into lunes, overlay the triangle, and the leftover angle, the excess beyond a flat triangle, equals the triangle's area on the unit sphere. Excess is not an error to discard. It is the curvature integrated over the region the triangle encloses.

The Hyperbolic Plane: Infinitely Many Parallels

Too much surface

Near every point a saddle has more area than a flat disk of the same radius, so geodesics splay apart instead of meeting.

Consequence

Lines through $P$ missing $\ell$ $= \infty$ (two are limiting / asymptotic).

Familiar models

A Pringle crisp, a lettuce edge, or the Poincaré disk all realise this geometry.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45The Hyperbolic Plane: Many Parallels
The hyperbolic plane reverses the sphere. Through a point off a line run infinitely many non-intersecting lines, two of them limiting and asymptotic. Visualized as a saddle or a crinkly lettuce leaf, there is too much surface near every point, so geodesics splay apart instead of converging the way they do on a sphere.

Hyperbolic Triangles Pinch: Angle Sum Below $180^\circ$

Angular defect

The angles fall short of two right angles, and a bigger triangle loses more. An ideal triangle, with vertices at infinity, has all three angles equal to $0^\circ$.

Defect and area

$\text{Area}(\triangle) = \pi - (\alpha+\beta+\gamma) = -E \;>\; 0$

Mirror of the sphere

The saddle pulls surface away, so corners pinch inward instead of bulging out.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Hyperbolic Triangles Pinch Inward
In hyperbolic geometry triangle angles fall short of two right angles, and the larger the triangle the larger the deficit. An ideal triangle with vertices at infinity has all three angles equal to zero. The corners pinch inward because the saddle pulls surface away, the mirror image of the sphere's outward bulge.

One Dial: Excess, Zero, Defect

Signed angular excess

$E = (\alpha+\beta+\gamma) - \pi \;=\; \iint_{\triangle} K \, dA$

Same instrument, three readings

$E>0$ on the sphere, $E=0$ in the plane, $E<0$ in the hyperbolic plane. The sign of the leftover angle is the sign of the curvature.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Excess and Defect Side by Side
Lining the three worlds up shows one continuous dial. Angle sum greater than a straight pair signals positive curvature, exactly a straight pair signals flatness, and less signals negative curvature. The deviation, signed, is the angular excess. Curvature is not exotic; it is the leftover angle a careful triangle hands you.

Intrinsic versus Extrinsic Bending

Extrinsic

How the surface sits in space. Rolling paper into a cylinder changes this dramatically.

Intrinsic

What an inhabitant can measure without leaving the surface: distances, angles, geodesics, and angular excess.

Key fact

Bending a flat sheet preserves all surface distances, so its triangles still sum to $180^\circ$.

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Intrinsic versus Extrinsic
Bend a sheet of paper into a cylinder and you have changed its embedding in space but not a single distance measured along the surface, so its triangles still sum to a straight pair. Intrinsic geometry is what an inhabitant can detect without leaving the surface, and angular excess is its loudest signal.

Curvature: The Thread Through Every Picture

What we built

Varied the parallel count, redefined straight as geodesic, and used the triangle angle sum as a curvature meter.

What it measures

The Gaussian curvature $K$ is $>0$ on the sphere, $0$ on the plane, $<0$ on the saddle, equalling excess per unit area.

Where it leads

Geodesics, parallel transport, and differential forms in later chapters all grow from this single seed.

The unifying statement

$\displaystyle K = \lim_{A\to 0}\frac{E}{A} = \lim_{A\to 0}\frac{(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)-\pi}{A}$

pp. 3-16 · PDF 32-45Curvature as the Common Thread
The chapter closes by naming what all three pictures share. A single number, the Gaussian curvature, is positive on the sphere, zero on the plane, and negative on the saddle, and it equals the angular excess per unit area. Everything later in the book, geodesics, transport, and forms, grows from this seed.
