Paths before groups

Endpoint-Fixed Homotopy

The endpoints are constraints, not decorations: they remain pinned for every homotopy time.

Definition
Path Homotopy Relative Endpoints

A homotopy \(F(s,t)\) connects paths \(f\) and \(g\) while keeping \(F(0,t)=x_0\) and \(F(1,t)=x_1\) for all \(t\).

Notebook Check

  • Maximum endpoint error: \(6.1 \times 10^{-17}\).
  • The product loop starts and ends at the basepoint.
  • The join point is the basepoint, so \(f*g\) is defined in \(\pi_1(X,x_0)\).
Interactive artifact path-homotopy-and-product.html
Use the endpoint pins as the first invariant the class should protect. Homotopy is allowed to move the middle of the path, but the endpoints stay fixed for every time value. Then tie the basepoint join to the loop product: the product is a loop only because the first loop ends exactly where the second one starts.
Loop classes

The Group Law

Concatenation descends from loops to endpoint-fixed homotopy classes.

Group Operation
Loop Product On Classes

\([f][g]=[f*g]\)

Identity is the constant loop at \(x_0\); inverse is the same loop with parameter reversed.

Common Failure

The product is not a free-floating operation on unbased loops. It uses a chosen basepoint and a pinned join.

Algebraic Target

The group \(\pi_1(X,x_0)\) stores endpoint-fixed homotopy classes of loops based at \(x_0\).

Treat the group law as a construction that survives deformation. Concatenation gives multiplication, the constant loop gives the identity, and reversing time gives inverses. The important caution is that products live at a chosen basepoint, so the operation is not just a rule for arbitrary closed curves floating in the space.
Transport

Basepoints Matter

A path between basepoints changes representatives by conjugation.

Basepoint Change

\(\beta_h([\alpha])=[h*\alpha*h^{-1}]\)

If \(h\) runs from \(x_0\) to \(x_1\), a loop based at \(x_1\) becomes a loop based at \(x_0\) by traveling out, looping, and returning.

Check From The Notebook

  • Conjugation homomorphism: true.
  • Transport product agrees with product of transports.
  • Unbased loop data naturally remembers conjugacy classes.
This is the place to slow down on basepoints. Changing basepoints by a path h transports loop classes by conjugation, which explains why unbased loop information loses a chosen representative and keeps only conjugacy. The notebook check verifies that this transport respects products, so the formula is not merely a mnemonic.
First computation

The Circle

Lifting a loop to the real line turns invisible winding into an endpoint displacement.

Circle Theorem

\(\pi_1(S^1,x_0)\cong \mathbb{Z}\)

The integer is the lifted endpoint after starting the lift at \(0\).

Lift Checks

  • Declared windings: \(-1,0,1,2,3\).
  • Each lifted endpoint is its winding integer.
  • Every sample projects back to the basepoint on \(S^1\).
Interactive artifact circle-lift-winding.html
For the circle, emphasize unwrapping. A loop on S1 returns to the same visible point, but its lift to the real line can end at a different integer. That endpoint is the winding number, and the artifact checks windings from minus one through three, all projecting back to the same basepoint downstairs.
Functoriality

Maps Induce Homomorphisms

A basepoint-preserving map sends loops to loops and products to products.

Functorial Rule

\((g\circ f)_*=g_*\circ f_*, \qquad (id_X)_*=id\)

Composition of continuous maps becomes composition of induced group homomorphisms.

Three Consequences

  • Degree maps on \(S^1\) multiply degrees.
  • \(\pi_1(X\times Y)\) splits into a product.
  • A retraction makes the inclusion-induced map injective.
degree composition true torus pair (2,-4) retraction injective
Maps of spaces become maps of groups by sending each loop through the continuous map. The diagram artifact packages three moves students will reuse constantly: functorial composition, product splitting, and the retraction argument for injectivity. Use the torus pair and circle degree checks as quick computational witnesses.

Assembly theorem

van Kampen Statement

Local fundamental groups assemble by a free product, then overlaps impose normal relations.

van Kampen Recipe

\(\pi_1(X)\cong (\pi_1(U)*\pi_1(V))/N\)

The normal subgroup \(N\) is generated by the differences between the same overlap loop seen in two cover pieces.

Hypotheses Made Visible

  • The cover nerve is connected.
  • The triple intersection is present in the notebook model.
  • The sample relator is \(i_{AB}(\omega)i_{BA}(\omega)^{-1}\).
wedge of two circles: free rank 2 overlap identifications
State van Kampen as assembly with a quotient. The free product records local loops in the cover pieces, while the normal subgroup forces overlap loops to agree after entering the whole space. The connected nerve and triple-intersection check are there to keep the hypotheses visible instead of hidden in prose.
Proof roadmap

van Kampen Proof Moves

The proof is a controlled translation from a global loop to local words and overlap relators.

Proof Moves
  • Subdivide the interval so each segment lands in a cover set.
  • Replace each segment by local loop data based at chosen connectors.
  • Show different choices differ by conjugates of overlap relators.

Why Normal Closure?

Overlap relations must remain killable after they are moved around the group by arbitrary paths.

The proof moves are the theorem in action: subdivide a loop so each small part sits in one cover set, replace those parts by local loop data, then compare alternative decompositions by overlap relations. Stress why normal closure appears: a relation may be carried around by paths before it is killed.
Relations from cells

Attaching 2-Cells

The boundary word of an attached disk becomes nullhomotopic in the new space.

2-Cell Rule

If \(X\) is formed by attaching a 2-cell along a loop \(w\), then \(\pi_1(X)\) quotients by the normal closure of \(w\).

Abelianization Sanity Checks

  • Orientable genus 2: free rank \(4\).
  • Nonorientable genus 3: free rank \(2\) plus torsion \(\mathbb{Z}/2\).
  • Surface words include \([a,b]\), \([a_1,b_1][a_2,b_2]\), and \(a_1^2a_2^2a_3^2\).
A 2-cell is best understood as a relation-maker. Its boundary word becomes nullhomotopic after the disk is attached, so the fundamental group is quotiented by the normal closure of that word. The abelianization checks provide sanity tests: genus two orientable rank four, and nonorientable genus three carrying Z over 2 torsion.
Presentations in action

Torus-Knot Presentations

A geometric decomposition becomes two generators with one relation.

Presentation

\(\pi_1(S^3\setminus K_{m,n})=\langle a,b\mid a^m=b^n\rangle\)

For coprime \(m,n\), the notebook examples \(K_{2,3}\) and \(K_{3,4}\) abelianize to free rank \(1\).

Sanity Check

Abelianization collapses the noncommutative presentation to a rank-one abelian group for the coprime examples. This does not identify the full knot group, but it catches the expected first-homology size.

Interactive artifact torus-knot-presentation.html
Use the torus-knot slide as a compact presentation example. The geometry supplies two natural generators, and van Kampen supplies the single relation a to the m equals b to the n. Abelianization is not the full group, but the rank-one check is a useful quick test for the coprime examples.
Local sheets

Covering Spaces

A cover looks locally like copies of the base, while global sheet motion stores algebra.

Finite Graph Cover

For \(S^1\vee S^1\), a finite cover is encoded by two permutations of a fiber: one for the \(a\)-loop and one for the \(b\)-loop.

Inspection Targets

  • Local neighborhoods lift to separate sheets.
  • Connected cover means the generated action has one orbit.
  • The gallery includes connected 2-, 3-, and 4-sheet examples.
2 sheets: group order 2 3 sheets: group order 6 4 sheets: V4 regular cover
Covering spaces turn local sameness into global bookkeeping. In graph covers of a wedge of two circles, the fiber over the basepoint is a set of sheets, and each generator lifts as a permutation of those sheets. The gallery shows that connectedness can be read as a single orbit.
Loops acting on fibers

Monodromy

Lifting a based loop permutes the sheets lying over the basepoint.

Action On The Fiber

\(\rho:\pi_1(X,x_0)\to Sym(p^{-1}(x_0))\)

The orbit of one sheet under this action is its connected component in the cover.

Regular Versus Nonnormal

  • Regular cover: monodromy order \(4\), deck centralizer order \(4\).
  • Nonnormal cover: monodromy order \(6\), deck centralizer order \(1\).
  • Both examples are connected because each action is transitive.
Monodromy is the action version of lifting. A loop at the basepoint moves a chosen sheet to another sheet, giving a permutation of the fiber. When the generated action is transitive, the cover is connected. Compare the regular four-sheet example with the nonnormal three-sheet example to separate transitivity from regularity.
Covers as subgroups

Classification

Connected covers correspond to subgroups of the fundamental group, with arrows reversed.

Classification Principle

For a suitable connected, locally path-connected, semilocally simply connected space, based connected covers correspond to subgroups \(H\leq \pi_1(X,x_0)\).

Arrow Reversal

A larger cover sits above a smaller cover when its subgroup is smaller. The universal cover corresponds to \(H=\{e\}\).

5 nodes 6 arrows DAG check true
Classification reverses the arrows students may expect. Subgroups of the fundamental group classify connected covers, with smaller subgroups giving larger covers. A covering map downward corresponds to subgroup inclusion upward. The lattice artifact makes this reversal concrete and records a directed acyclic organization with the universal cover at the top.
Symmetries over the base

Deck Transformations

Regularity is the difference between all fibers looking symmetrically alike and only locally alike.

Deck Group Formula

\(Deck(\widetilde X_H/X)\cong N(H)/H\)

A connected cover is regular exactly when \(H\) is normal in \(\pi_1(X,x_0)\).

Notebook Contrast

  • Regular example: deck order \(4\).
  • Nonnormal example: deck order \(1\).
  • The regular deck elements act transitively on the four sheets.
Deck transformations are the symmetries of the cover that sit over the identity map downstairs. For the cover associated to a subgroup H, the deck group is the normalizer quotient N of H over H. Normal subgroups give regular covers, and the checks contrast deck order four with the nonnormal order-one case.
Maximal trees

Graphs Are Free

Collapse a maximal tree and keep one generator for each remaining edge.

Graph Rank

\(rank\,\pi_1(\Gamma)=E-V+1\)

For the notebook graph, \(E=9\), \(V=6\), so the rank is \(4\).

Why The Tree Vanishes

A maximal tree is contractible and contains all vertices. Collapsing it preserves the loop information while turning each chord into a visible loop generator.

tree edges 5 chord edges 4 tree acyclic
Graphs are the most visible free groups in the chapter. Choose a maximal tree, collapse it, and every chord becomes one free generator. The numerical check E minus V plus one equals four gives students a fast way to test the picture while remembering that the basis depends on the tree.
Universal covers as structure

K(G,1) And Bass-Serre

When higher homotopy disappears, group splittings become visible in universal covering trees.

K(G,1)

A \(K(G,1)\) has \(\pi_1\cong G\) and \(\pi_n=0\) for \(n>1\). Its universal cover is contractible.

Bass-Serre Bridge

Graphs of groups glue classifying spaces along injective edge maps. The universal cover arranges lifted pieces in a tree-like pattern.

truncation is a tree 19 vertices 18 edges bipartite by vertex type
The K(G,1) and Bass-Serre slide points beyond the chapter without leaving its logic. A K(G,1) has all higher homotopy suppressed, so the fundamental group becomes the main homotopy invariant. Bass-Serre theory then organizes group splittings by universal covers that look like trees; the artifact is a finite tree window.
