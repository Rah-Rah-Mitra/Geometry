Complex Geometry: An Introduction · Chapter 01

Local Theory of Holomorphic Functions

Printed pages 1-50; PDF pages 12-61. A coordinate-level lecture through several variables, analytic germs, Hermitian linear algebra, and the local \( \bar\partial \) calculus behind Kähler charts.

Open the chapter as a sequence of local machines rather than a list of isolated facts. First the source develops holomorphic functions of several variables, then converts them into germs and local rings. The second section supplies Hermitian linear algebra, and the final section turns that linear algebra into differential forms, dbar exactness, and the local Kahler model.

Holomorphicity Is Componentwise, But Not Merely Formal

Definition 1.1.1
For \(U\subset \mathbb C^n\), a \(C^1\) function \(f:U\to\mathbb C\) is holomorphic when every coordinate satisfies the one-variable Cauchy-Riemann equation.
$$\bar\partial f=\sum_{i=1}^n {\partial f\over\partial \bar z_i}\,d\bar z_i=0,$$ $$ {\partial\over\partial z_i}={1\over2}\left({\partial\over\partial x_i}-i{\partial\over\partial y_i}\right). $$

The chapter later gives this notation an actual differential-form meaning in Section 1.3.

Use this slide to make the notation honest. In one variable, the Cauchy-Riemann equations say that the real derivative is complex linear. In several variables the same test is imposed in each coordinate direction, and the expression dbar f equals zero is only a shorthand until the differential-form language arrives in Section 1.3.

Product Cauchy Formula: One Circle Per Variable

Proposition 1.1.2
Separate holomorphicity on a polydisc gives an iterated Cauchy integral, hence local power series.
$$f(z)={1\over(2\pi i)^n}\int_{\partial P} {f(\zeta)\,d\zeta_1\cdots d\zeta_n\over (\zeta_1-z_1)\cdots(\zeta_n-z_n)}.$$
  • Proof is repeated one-variable Cauchy.
  • Uniform convergence allows coefficients to be read from the integral.
  • Integral representations preserve holomorphic dependence on parameters.
The key visual is that a polydisc has a distinguished product boundary, not a round sphere. Repeated one-variable Cauchy integration gives the displayed kernel with one denominator for each coordinate. This is the engine behind local power series and behind later parameter arguments where an integral of a holomorphic integrand remains holomorphic.

Hartogs: In Dimension Two Or More, Holes Are Fragile

Proposition 1.1.4
For \(n>1\), a holomorphic function on a polydisc shell \(B_s(0)\setminus B_{s'}(0)\) extends uniquely across the missing smaller polydisc.

Contrast With One Variable

A punctured disc can carry a pole or an essential singularity. Hartogs says the multi-variable shell forces enough boundary information to fill the interior.

Laurent coefficients in \(z_1\) become holomorphic functions of \(w=(z_2,\ldots,z_n)\); the negative part vanishes by maximum-principle control on the side wall.
Do not present Hartogs as a minor extension theorem. It is one of the first genuinely several-variable surprises. The proof in the source expands in one coordinate and lets the remaining coordinates control Laurent coefficients. Because the singular set cannot hide inside a compact hole, the negative Laurent part is forced to disappear.

Weierstrass Preparation Turns Zeros Into A Polynomial

Proposition 1.1.6
If \(f(0)=0\) and the \(z_1\)-axis sees a nonzero one-variable function, then after shrinking
$$ f(z_1,w)= \left(z_1^d+a_1(w)z_1^{d-1}+\cdots+a_d(w)\right)h(z_1,w), $$ $$ a_j(0)=0,\qquad h(0)\ne 0. $$

The polynomial factor is unique. It isolates the zero geometry; the unit \(h\) is locally harmless.

Frame Weierstrass preparation as a normal form for hypersurface germs. The zeros in the chosen variable may branch and collide, so the roots themselves need not be single-valued functions. But the elementary symmetric functions are holomorphic, giving one monic polynomial in z one. Everything left over is a unit and carries no local zero set.

Extension, Inverse, And Implicit Maps Share One Local Pattern

Riemann Extension

Bounded holomorphic data on \(U\setminus Z(f)\) extends across the thin analytic set after reducing to one-variable slices.

Inverse Map

If \(F:U\to V\) has \(\det JF(z_0)\ne 0\), then near \(z_0\) it has a holomorphic inverse.

Implicit Graph

A full-rank block of the complex Jacobian lets a fiber \(F=F(z_0)\) be written as a holomorphic graph.

$$J(F)(z)=\left({\partial F_i\over\partial z_j}(z)\right),\qquad d_{\mathbb R}F\hbox{ is complex linear.}$$

The real inverse and implicit function theorems supply smooth maps; complex linearity upgrades them to holomorphic maps.

This slide ties together three results that can otherwise feel unrelated. Extension arguments use Weierstrass preparation and Cauchy integrals to cross thin sets. The inverse and implicit function theorems begin as real differential topology, then the complex Jacobian condition forces the resulting inverse or graph map to satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

Analytic Germs Make Geometry And Ideals Talk Locally

Definitions 1.1.21-1.1.24
An analytic germ is locally a common zero set \(Z(f_1,\ldots,f_k)\). The ideal \(I(X)\) consists of germs vanishing on \(X\).
$$Z(I)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I(X),\qquad X\hbox{ irreducible}\Longleftrightarrow I(X)\hbox{ prime}.$$
Proposition 1.1.29
Local Nullstellensatz: \(I(Z(I))=\sqrt I\) inside \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb C^n,0}\).

The finite-projection theorem in the source supplies the dimension intuition: an irreducible germ is locally finite over a coordinate space of dimension \(d\).

The source now makes rigorous what earlier slides used informally: zero sets of germs are themselves local objects. Emphasize the reversal of inclusion between ideals and zero sets. The Nullstellensatz says this reversal loses exactly nilpotent power information. Irreducibility becomes primality, so local analytic geometry can be studied with commutative algebra.

Meromorphic Functions Remember Divisors; Schwarz Controls Order

Definitions 1.1.33-1.1.34
Locally a meromorphic function is a quotient \(m=g/h\) of holomorphic germs, with \(g,h\) relatively prime. Its zero and pole sets are analytic.
Proposition 1.1.36
A Schwarz-type estimate converts vanishing order \(k\) at the origin into a \(k\)-th order radial bound on smaller polydiscs.
$$m={g\over h},\qquad Z(m)=Z(g),\qquad P(m)=Z(h),$$ $$ |f(z)|\le M\left({\|z\|_\infty\over s}\right)^k\quad\hbox{as a model estimate.} $$
Meromorphic functions are introduced locally because the quotient description is stable in the stalk. Once numerator and denominator are relatively prime, zeros and poles are well-defined analytic sets. The Schwarz lemma at the end of Section 1.1 gives a different kind of local control: vanishing order imposes quantitative decay along complex lines inside a polydisc.

Almost Complex Structures Are Linear Algebra Before Geometry

Definition 1.2.1
An almost complex structure on a real vector space \(V\) is an endomorphism \(I\) with \(I^2=-\operatorname{id}\).
$$V_{\mathbb C}=V^{1,0}\oplus V^{0,1},\qquad \overline{V^{1,0}}=V^{0,1}.$$
Definition 1.2.7
Exterior powers split into bidegrees \( \Lambda^{p,q}V^*=\Lambda^p(V^{1,0})^*\otimes\Lambda^q(V^{0,1})^* \).
The second section deliberately pauses the analytic story and builds the linear algebra that every tangent space will carry. The main message is that a real endomorphism squaring to minus the identity gives a complex vector space structure. After complexification, the plus and minus i eigenspaces create the bidegree notation used for forms.

A Compatible Metric Packages Into A Fundamental Form

Definitions 1.2.11 and 1.2.13
A metric \(g\) is compatible with \(I\) if \(g(Iv,Iw)=g(v,w)\). Its fundamental form is \(\omega(v,w)=g(Iv,w)\).
$$h(v,w)=g(v,w)-i\,\omega(v,w),\qquad h\hbox{ is Hermitian.}$$ $$\omega_0={i\over2}\sum_j dz_j\wedge d\bar z_j.$$

This is the local linear model behind Hermitian and Kähler geometry: metric data becomes a real \((1,1)\)-form.

Here the algebra becomes geometric again. A compatible metric treats the complex rotation I as an isometry. The fundamental form records the oriented area pairing produced by applying I to one input. Combining the symmetric metric and the skew form gives a Hermitian form, and in standard coordinates this becomes the familiar i over two sum.

Lefschetz Operators Create An \( \mathfrak{sl}_2 \) Ladder

Definitions 1.2.23 and 1.2.24
The Lefschetz operator is \(L(\alpha)=\omega\wedge\alpha\). Its adjoint is \(\Lambda\), and \(H=[L,\Lambda]\).
$$[H,L]=2L,\qquad [H,\Lambda]=-2\Lambda,\qquad [L,\Lambda]=H.$$ $$H|_{\Lambda^k V^*}=(k-n)\operatorname{id}.$$

This finite-dimensional \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\)-representation is the algebraic skeleton of Lefschetz decomposition.

The operator L is simple: wedge with the fundamental form. The depth comes from its adjoint Lambda and the commutator H. Together they satisfy the sl two relations on the exterior algebra. This gives a representation-theoretic way to organize all forms by degree and explains why the middle degree is the reflecting wall.

Primitive Forms Are The Building Blocks; Hodge-Riemann Gives The Sign

Definition 1.2.29
A form \(\alpha\in\Lambda^k V^*\) is primitive when \(\Lambda\alpha=0\). Denote the primitive subspace by \(P^k\).
Proposition 1.2.30 and Corollary 1.2.36
Every form decomposes into Lefschetz powers of primitive forms, and the Hodge-Riemann pairing has a controlled signature on primitive \((p,q)\)-pieces.
$$\Lambda^k V^*=\bigoplus_{j\ge0} L^jP^{k-2j},\qquad Q(\alpha,\beta)=\int \alpha\wedge L^{n-k}\beta.$$
Primitive forms are the lowest-weight vectors for the Lefschetz sl two action. Once a primitive form is chosen, repeated wedging with omega builds an entire string. The Hodge star interacts with these strings in a precise way, and the Hodge-Riemann relation says that after the conventional complex sign correction, primitive pieces have definite sign.

Differential Forms On \( \mathbb C^n \) Inherit The Bidegree Split

Definitions 1.3.1 and 1.3.3
For \(U\subset\mathbb C^n\), the tangent spaces carry the standard complex structure, so complexified forms split into bundles \(\Lambda^{p,q}U\).
$$T_{\mathbb C}U=T^{1,0}U\oplus T^{0,1}U,\qquad A^k_{\mathbb C}(U)=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}A^{p,q}(U).$$

This is where the earlier linear algebra becomes a calculus on open subsets of complex affine space. Each tangent space has its standard almost complex structure, so forms split into p q types. The projection operators are local and compatible with restriction, which is why they can later be glued on complex manifolds.

The Exterior Derivative Splits, And \( \bar\partial \) Is Locally Exact

Definition 1.3.5
Define \(\partial=\Pi_{p+1,q}d\) and \(\bar\partial=\Pi_{p,q+1}d\) on \(A^{p,q}(U)\).
$$d=\partial+\bar\partial,\qquad \partial^2=0,\quad \bar\partial^2=0,\quad \partial\bar\partial+\bar\partial\partial=0.$$
Propositions 1.3.7-1.3.9
On polydiscs, every \(\bar\partial\)-closed \((p,q)\)-form with \(q>0\) is locally \(\bar\partial\)-exact.
The exterior derivative has only two type components because d changes total degree by one. The d squared identity then splits into the Dolbeault identities. The central analytic result is the dbar Poincare lemma: on a polydisc, closed forms of positive anti-holomorphic degree are dbar of something locally, first in one variable and then by induction.

$$\omega={i\over2}\sum h_{ij}(z)\,dz_i\wedge d\bar z_j,\qquad (h_{ij})=I+O(|z|^2).$$ $$\omega_0={i\over2}\partial\bar\partial |z|^2.$$
