Cohomology As Local-To-Global Failure

A chapter map from local gluing problems to computable invariants for projective schemes and families.

Chapter Goal
Cohomology turns a failure of local data to glue into groups that can be computed, paired, pushed forward, and tracked in families.
  • Start from sections on opens and ask whether they come from a global section.
  • Replace a chosen cover by derived functors so the answer is intrinsic.
  • Use vanishing, exact sequences, duality, and base change to do geometry with the answer.
Source orientation: Chapter III, printed pages 201-292; PDF pages 216-307.Notebook: chapter-03-cohomology/03-cohomology.ipynb
Open by framing cohomology as a diagnostic, not as a mysterious new object. The learner already knows local regular functions and sheaves from earlier chapters; now the question is whether compatible local choices can be made global. The chapter starts with this gluing failure, then builds a derived-functor machine so the obstruction does not depend on a preferred cover. Emphasize that the later topics, including projective tables, duality, direct images, flatness, formal functions, and semicontinuity, are all ways of making that diagnostic useful.
Translation Guide

Read \(H^i\) As A Graded Failure Meter

Degree Zero
\(H^0(X,\mathcal F)=\Gamma(X,\mathcal F)\)
The sections that are already global.
First Obstruction
\(H^1\)
Local sections can match pairwise yet fail to be one global section.
Higher Degrees
\(H^i(X,\mathcal F)=R^i\Gamma(X,\mathcal F)\)
Derived functors stabilize the bookkeeping beyond a single cover.
Vocabulary to keep visible: sections, obstruction, derived functor.Slide 2 / 18
Use this slide as the audience's dictionary for the entire lecture. H0 is not new notation for something exotic; it is the global section functor Gamma. H1 is the first place where compatible local behavior can fail to globalize. The higher groups continue that pattern after the theory is passed through derived functors. Stress the equality H^i equals R^i Gamma because it explains why exact sequences of sheaves produce long exact sequences of cohomology groups.
Derived Functors

A Resolution Pipeline Feeds \(R^iF\)

Mechanism
A left exact functor preserves kernels but not necessarily cokernels. Its right derived functors measure the missing exactness.
\(0\to A\to I^0\to I^1\to \cdots\quad\leadsto\quad R^iF(A)\)
  • For sheaves, take \(F=\Gamma(X,-)\).
  • For modules, this same pattern produces Ext.
  • Exact sequences become long exact sequences after deriving.
Source sections: Derived Functors and Cohomology of Sheaves.Slide 3 / 18
The key pedagogical move is to treat derived functors as a pipeline. Start with an object A, replace it by a resolution whose terms are good for the functor F, apply F termwise, and then take cohomology of the resulting complex. For the chapter, the main example is F equals global sections. This explains why cohomology groups are attached to sheaves but behave functorially enough to sit inside exact sequences, dualities, and pushforwards.
Computability

Acyclic Objects Make Computation Possible

Vanishing Test
\(\mathcal A\ \text{acyclic}\quad\Rightarrow\quad H^i(X,\mathcal A)=0\ \text{for}\ i>0\)
Why This Matters
A resolution by acyclic objects can compute cohomology without building an injective resolution explicitly.
  • Injective objects are theoretically available.
  • Flasque or acyclic objects are often easier to recognize.
  • Cech complexes become valid under the right cover hypotheses.
Acyclicity is the bridge from definition to calculation.Slide 4 / 18
This slide is the computational relief valve. Derived functors are defined with resolutions, but injective resolutions are usually not the object a geometer wants to write down in a calculation. Acyclic objects make the same groups accessible through friendlier complexes. The lecture should underline the implication: if the higher cohomology of the pieces vanishes, then the remaining complex faithfully computes the target. This prepares the audience for Cech calculations and for affine vanishing.
Cech Cochains

Cech Cochains On A Three-Open Cover

Notebook Artifact

A 1-cochain assigns data to pairwise overlaps; the cocycle test reads the triple overlap.

Cochain Levels
For a cover \(\{U_i\}\), a 0-cochain is data on each \(U_i\); a 1-cochain is data on each \(U_i\cap U_j\); a 2-cochain is data on triple overlaps.
\(C^0 \xrightarrow{\delta} C^1 \xrightarrow{\delta} C^2\)
  • Local sections become vertices.
  • Pairwise differences become edges.
  • Triple-overlap residuals detect a failed cocycle.

Use the graph to slow the abstraction down. The vertices are not decoration; they represent local choices on opens. The edges represent overlap data, where two local choices are compared. The triple overlap is where the alternating sum is tested. This gives a concrete reason for the complex C0 to C1 to C2, and it lets the instructor say that cohomology is bookkeeping for gluing rather than a separate geometric layer floating above the scheme.

Affine Vanishing

Affine Schemes: Higher Cohomology Vanishes

Theorem
\(X=\operatorname{Spec}A,\ \mathcal F\ \text{quasi-coherent}\quad\Rightarrow\quad H^i(X,\mathcal F)=0\ \text{for}\ i>0.\)
Interpretation
On a noetherian affine scheme, quasi-coherent sheaves are controlled by modules of global sections; the local-to-global obstruction disappears in positive degree.
Source section: Cohomology of a Noetherian Affine Scheme.Slide 7 / 18
This is the chapter's first major vanishing result and should be presented as a computational baseline. Affine schemes are where quasi-coherent sheaves behave like modules. For this class of sheaves, the global section functor sees enough that higher cohomology vanishes. Later projective and family computations are measured against this: non-affineness is exactly where positive cohomology can become geometrically meaningful.

Ext Groups

Ext Groups Are Cohomology Of Hom

Derived Hom
\(\operatorname{Ext}^i(F,G)=R^i\operatorname{Hom}(F,G)\)
Two Levels
Global Ext groups are derived functors of global Hom; sheaf Ext keeps the calculation local before taking global sections.
  • Ext^0 is Hom.
  • Higher Ext records extension and obstruction data.
  • Duality statements naturally use Ext-style language.
Source section: Ext Groups and Sheaves.Slide 11 / 18
This slide transfers the derived-functor pattern from Gamma to Hom. Ext^0 is Hom, and higher Ext groups measure the derived failure of Hom to be exact. Make the global versus sheaf distinction explicitly: global Ext returns groups, while sheaf Ext is computed locally as a sheaf before any global sections are taken. This distinction helps prevent confusion when Serre duality appears, because duality often moves between cohomology groups, Hom-like pairings, and canonical twists.
Serre Duality

Serre Duality Pairs Opposite Degrees

Smooth Projective Form
\(H^i(X,\mathcal F)\ \times\ H^{n-i}(X,\mathcal F^\vee\otimes\omega_X)\to k\)
Meaning
A group in degree \(i\) is paired with a canonical-twisted dual group in complementary degree \(n-i\).
  • Duality changes degree.
  • The canonical sheaf \(\omega_X\) supplies the twist.
  • Vanishing in one region can imply vanishing or dimensions in another.
Source section: The Serre Duality Theorem.Slide 12 / 18
This is the conceptual turning point from calculation to symmetry. The formula says that cohomology in degree i pairs with cohomology in complementary degree n minus i after dualizing the sheaf and twisting by the canonical sheaf. The pairing diagram should be read horizontally: each blue group has a violet partner. For locally free sheaves on a smooth projective variety, this is the cleanest form for lecture purposes. Mention that more general statements use Ext, which is why the previous slide matters.
Higher Direct Images

\(R^i f_*\mathcal F\) Packages Fiberwise Cohomology

Relative Cohomology
\(f:X\to Y,\qquad R^i f_*\mathcal F\)
A sheaf on \(Y\) that records the \(i\)-th cohomology of \(\mathcal F\) along the fibers, under suitable hypotheses.
  • When \(Y\) is a point, this recovers \(H^i(X,\mathcal F)\).
  • When \(Y\) varies, it turns cohomology into data on the base.
  • Base change asks when this packaging commutes with pulling to a fiber.
Source section: Higher Direct Images of Sheaves.Slide 13 / 18
Higher direct images are the relative version of cohomology. Instead of collapsing X all the way to a point, the map f collapses each fiber toward the base Y and records the resulting cohomology as sheaves on Y. This is the right language for families because it lets the base remember how the cohomology of fibers is changing. The slide should connect directly to the next two topics: flatness controls numerical behavior, and base change asks whether the packaged sheaf actually gives the fiber cohomology after restriction.
Flat Morphisms

Flat Families Control Numerical Data

Guiding Principle
Flatness prevents unpredictable numerical jumps: in projective families, it is tied to a constant Hilbert polynomial.
\(\text{flat over }S\quad\Rightarrow\quad P_{\mathcal F_s}(m)\ \text{is locally constant}\)
  • The fibers may look different.
  • The polynomial data remains controlled.
  • Without flatness, dimensions can change for reasons unrelated to geometry one meant to study.
Source section: Flat Morphisms.Slide 14 / 18
Flatness is often introduced algebraically, but in this lecture it should be tied to what it buys geometrically. A flat family has controlled fiberwise numerical data; in projective settings this is expressed through local constancy of the Hilbert polynomial. Say carefully that flatness does not mean every fiber is isomorphic or visually identical. It means the family is not secretly gaining or losing embedded numerical mass in ways that would make cohomological and Hilbert-function arguments unreliable.
Smooth Morphisms

Smooth Means Flat With Regular Geometric Fibers

Smooth Morphism
A smooth morphism is a flat morphism whose geometric fibers are regular, with the expected relative dimension behavior.
  • Smooth fibers vary without developing singularities.
  • Flatness controls the family numerically.
  • A singular special fiber can break smoothness even when nearby fibers look regular.
Source section: Smooth Morphisms.Slide 15 / 18
Use this slide to separate smoothness from the softer notion of a visually continuous family. Smoothness includes flatness, but it also requires regular geometric fibers. The lower row shows the kind of special fiber that should make students suspicious: a marked singular point where regularity fails. This distinction is important because smooth families are the setting where many cohomological tools behave especially well, while singular degeneration is where jumps and correction terms often enter.
Formal Functions

Formal Functions Compare Completion With A Tower Of Thickenings

Formal Functions
\(\widehat{R^i f_*\mathcal F}\ \cong\ \varprojlim_n H^i(X_n,\mathcal F_n)\)
Meaning
The completed higher direct image can be read from cohomology on infinitesimal neighborhoods of the fiber.
  • Thicken the fiber.
  • Compute cohomology on each thickening.
  • Pass to an inverse limit.
Source section: The Theorem on Formal Functions.Slide 16 / 18
Formal functions are a bridge between global derived pushforwards and infinitesimal neighborhoods of a fiber. The tower is the object to point at: X1, X2, X3, and so on are successive thickenings, each carrying a restricted sheaf. The theorem says that completing the higher direct image at the base point agrees with the inverse limit of these cohomology groups. This is a powerful local-to-formal principle and sets up why completion can recover cohomological information near a special fiber.

Four Habits For Later Chapters

Cech

Turn local gluing into a complex and inspect the residual.

Table

Use projective-space formulas and Euler characteristics as anchors.

Exact Chain

Propagate known groups and respect the connecting homomorphism.

Family Jump

Expect upper jumps, then ask whether base change is exact enough.

Chapter III supplies the local-to-global toolkit used by curves, surfaces, and moduli arguments.Slide 18 / 18
End by naming the four habits students should carry forward. First, draw a Cech complex when gluing feels abstract. Second, keep a small table such as O(d) on P1 nearby as a calibration example. Third, read long exact sequences as dependency chains rather than as static formulas. Fourth, in families, look for jumps and ask what flatness, formal functions, semicontinuity, and base change actually guarantee. These habits are exactly what later chapters use when curves and surfaces become the main objects.
