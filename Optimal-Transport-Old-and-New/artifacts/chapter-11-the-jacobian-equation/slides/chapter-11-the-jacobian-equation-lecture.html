The Jacobian Equation

From mass conservation to a determinant identity

The single identity

If $T_\#\mu=\nu$ with densities $f$ and $g$, then $f(x)=g(T(x))\,|\det DT(x)|$.

Why it matters

It is the bridge from transport maps to PDE and to regularity theory.

This chapter

Derive it, specialize to Monge-Ampere, and handle non-smooth maps.

Printed pp. 273-280The Jacobian Equation
Welcome to the chapter that turns a transport map into a differential equation. Mass conservation, written infinitesimally, forces the source density to equal the target density at the image point times the absolute Jacobian determinant. That single identity is the bridge from couplings to partial differential equations and, ultimately, to regularity theory.

Push-Forward and Mass Conservation

Push-forward

$T_\#\mu=\nu$ means $\mu\big(T^{-1}(B)\big)=\nu(B)$ for every measurable $B$.

In densities

$d\mu=f\,dx$ and $d\nu=g\,dy$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Goal

Make this set-level balance into a pointwise relation between $f$ and $g$.

Mass balance, integral form

$$\int_{T^{-1}(B)} f(x)\,dx \;=\; \int_{B} g(y)\,dy$$

Test functions

Equivalently $\displaystyle\int \zeta(T(x))\,f(x)\,dx=\int \zeta(y)\,g(y)\,dy$ for all bounded $\zeta$.

Printed pp. 273-280Push-Forward and Mass Conservation
Recall the push-forward condition. A map T transports mu onto nu when the mass of every target set equals the mass of its preimage. We rewrite this integral identity through a change of variables, which is exactly where the Jacobian determinant of T enters and where the chapter's central equation will be born.

Change of Variables

Classical formula

For $T$ injective and $C^1$ with $\det DT\neq 0$, set $y=T(x)$, $dy=|\det DT(x)|\,dx$.

Substitute

Push the target integral back to $x$-space along the map.

Match integrands

Two integrals agreeing on every $B$ forces their integrands to agree a.e.

Substitution

$$\int_B g(y)\,dy=\int_{T^{-1}(B)} g(T(x))\,|\det DT(x)|\,dx$$

Therefore

$$f(x)=g(T(x))\,|\det DT(x)| \quad (\text{a.e.})$$

Printed pp. 273-280Change of Variables
The classical change-of-variables formula is the engine here. For a smooth injective map with positive Jacobian, integrating against the target density and substituting y equals T of x produces a factor of the Jacobian determinant. Demanding that this reproduces the source density pointwise gives the Jacobian equation directly.

The Jacobian Equation

Statement

$$f(x)=g\big(T(x)\big)\,\big|\det DT(x)\big|$$

Reading

Source density = target density at the image, weighted by local volume change.

Absolute value

Mass is unsigned; only the magnitude $|\det DT|$ of the volume scaling appears.

Almost everywhere

Required only $\mu$-a.e.; null sets and folds are allowed to misbehave.

Nondegeneracy

Where $g(T(x))>0$, the determinant cannot vanish — no mass is crushed to zero volume.

Printed pp. 273-280The Jacobian Equation
Here is the heart of the chapter, stated cleanly. The source density at x equals the target density at the image times the absolute value of the determinant of the differential of T. Emphasise that the determinant carries an absolute value because mass is unsigned, and that the identity is required only mu-almost-everywhere.

Local Mass Balance

The Jacobian equation read geometrically

Balance the cells

$$\underbrace{f(x)\,dx}_{\text{mass in}}=\underbrace{g(T(x))\,dy}_{\text{mass out}},\quad dy=|\det DT|\,dx$$

Cancel $dx$

Dividing the volume element gives back $f=g(T)\,|\det DT|$ — the same identity, now intuitive.

Printed pp. 273-280Local Mass Balance
Walk the audience through the picture. A tiny volume element around x is mapped to a parallelepiped around T of x whose volume is scaled by the absolute Jacobian determinant. Equal mass in both cells forces density times volume to balance, which is the Jacobian equation read geometrically rather than analytically.

Quadratic Cost: $T=\nabla\psi$

Brenier's map

For cost $|x-y|^2$, the optimal $T$ is the gradient of a convex potential $\psi$.

Differential

$DT=D(\nabla\psi)=D^2\psi$, the Hessian of a convex function.

Positivity

Convexity gives $D^2\psi\succeq 0$, so $\det D^2\psi\geq 0$ — no absolute value needed.

Substitute into the Jacobian equation

$$f(x)=g\big(\nabla\psi(x)\big)\,\det D^2\psi(x)$$

What changed

The map is now encoded by a single scalar potential; its Hessian determinant is the unknown.

Printed pp. 273-280Quadratic Cost: T = grad psi
For the quadratic cost, Brenier's theorem says the optimal map is the gradient of a convex potential psi. Substitute T equal to grad psi into the Jacobian equation. The differential of the map becomes the Hessian of psi, so its determinant is the Monge-Ampere operator acting on a convex function.

The Monge-Ampere Equation

Monge-Ampere form

$$\det D^2\psi(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g\big(\nabla\psi(x)\big)}$$

Type

Fully nonlinear, second order; the right side couples to the map through $\nabla\psi$.

Printed pp. 273-280The Monge-Ampere Equation
This slide states the specialization explicitly. The determinant of the Hessian of psi equals the source density divided by the target density evaluated at grad psi. This fully nonlinear second-order equation, of Monge-Ampere type, is the analytic form the transport problem takes for quadratic cost.

Approximate Differentiability

The trouble

Optimal maps may be only Borel; the classical $DT$ need not exist everywhere.

The fix

$T$ is approximately differentiable at $x$ if a linear map $L$ approximates it in measure-density.

Approximate Jacobian

$|\det \tilde{D}T|$ replaces $|\det DT|$ in the identity.

Density-sense limit

$$\operatorname*{ap\,lim}_{z\to x}\frac{T(z)-T(x)-L(z-x)}{|z-x|}=0$$

Brenier maps

As gradients of convex functions, they are differentiable Lebesgue-a.e. — strong enough for the equation.

Printed pp. 273-280Approximate Differentiability
Optimal maps need not be smooth, so the classical Jacobian may not exist everywhere. The fix is approximate differentiability: at almost every point the map admits a linear approximation in the measure-theoretic density sense. Brenier maps, being gradients of convex functions, are differentiable Lebesgue-almost-everywhere by Aleksandrov's theorem.

Aleksandrov Second Derivatives

Aleksandrov's theorem

A convex $\psi$ has a second-order Taylor expansion at Lebesgue-a.e. point.

Pointwise expansion

$$\psi(x{+}h)=\psi(x)+\nabla\psi(x)\!\cdot\! h+\tfrac12 h^\top D^2_A\psi(x)\,h+o(|h|^2)$$

Aleksandrov Hessian

$D^2_A\psi$ is this a.e.-defined symmetric matrix; it enters $\det D^2\psi$.

Not the distributional one

Distinguish $\det D^2_A\psi$ from the Monge-Ampere measure, which can carry singular mass.

Consequence

The Jacobian equation uses the pointwise Aleksandrov determinant, not a distribution.

Printed pp. 273-280Aleksandrov Second Derivatives
Aleksandrov's theorem guarantees that a convex function has a second-order Taylor expansion almost everywhere, defining the Aleksandrov Hessian. This pointwise Hessian, rather than any distributional object, is the determinant that appears in the Jacobian equation. Distinguish it carefully from the larger distributional Monge-Ampere measure.

Where the Identity Holds

Hypotheses

$\mu,\nu$ absolutely continuous with densities $f,g$; $T$ the optimal (Brenier) map.

Conclusion

The Jacobian equation holds $\mu$-a.e. with the approximate / Aleksandrov determinant.

The working identity

$$f(x)=g\big(T(x)\big)\,\big|\det \tilde{D}T(x)\big| \quad \mu\text{-a.e.}$$

Injectivity a.e.

Monotonicity makes $\nabla\psi$ injective off a null set, legitimizing the substitution.

Caveat

"Almost everywhere" is unavoidable: folds, kinks, and density zeros sit on null sets.

Printed pp. 273-280Where the Identity Holds
Collect the technical caveats. The Jacobian equation holds mu-almost-everywhere using the approximate Jacobian, provided the densities are honest and the map is the optimal one. Stress that almost-everywhere is the natural and unavoidable register here, and that injectivity up to null sets is what makes the substitution legitimate.

The Bridge to PDE

Coupling to determinant

$f=g(T)\,|\det DT|$ recasts a measure problem as a determinant equation.

To Monge-Ampere

For quadratic cost, $\det D^2\psi = f/g(\nabla\psi)$ — the analytic gateway.

Onward

Feeds Caffarelli-style regularity and later curvature/volume-distortion bounds.

Printed pp. 273-280The Bridge to PDE
Close by naming the payoff. The Jacobian equation converts a measure-coupling problem into a determinant equation, and for quadratic cost into Monge-Ampere. That equation is the gateway to Caffarelli's and later regularity theory, and it reappears whenever volume distortion of optimal maps must be quantified, as in curvature bounds.
