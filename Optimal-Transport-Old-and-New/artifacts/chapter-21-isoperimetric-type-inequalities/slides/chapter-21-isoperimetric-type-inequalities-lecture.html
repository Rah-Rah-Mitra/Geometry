The Classical Isoperimetric Problem

Euclidean statement

Among regions of fixed volume, the ball minimizes boundary measure.

Why it matters

It bounds the boundary-to-volume ratio from below, a purely geometric constraint.

Sharp inequality

$$\operatorname{Per}(A) \;\ge\; n\,\omega_n^{1/n}\,|A|^{(n-1)/n}$$

Equality holds exactly when $A$ is a ball.

Transported version

Replace the ball by the model region of a curved space and the same logic survives.

Printed pp. 545-566The Classical Isoperimetric Problem
Among all regions of a given volume, which one minimizes boundary measure? In Euclidean space the ball wins, and the inequality controls the boundary-to-volume ratio. We restate it through the isoperimetric profile so that the same question can be transported to any metric-measure space with a curvature floor.

The Isoperimetric Profile

Definition

For each volume fraction $v$, the smallest possible perimeter of a region of that size.

Reading it

A higher profile means the space separates volume more cheaply.

Printed pp. 545-566The Isoperimetric Profile
The isoperimetric profile records, for each fraction of total volume, the smallest possible perimeter of a region of that size. Reading the profile is the whole game: a higher profile means the space separates volume more cheaply, and comparison theorems bound it from below by a model profile.

Curvature-Dimension $\mathrm{CD}(K,N)$

Two parameters

$K$ is a lower curvature bound; $N$ is an upper dimension bound.

Synthetic meaning

Entropy is displacement convex along Wasserstein geodesics.

Convexity along $(\mu_t)$

$$\mathrm{Ent}(\mu_t) \le (1-t)\,\mathrm{Ent}(\mu_0)+t\,\mathrm{Ent}(\mu_1)-\tfrac{K}{2}t(1-t)W_2^2$$

Payoff

No smooth structure needed, so it survives on singular spaces.

Printed pp. 545-566Curvature-Dimension CD(K,N)
The curvature-dimension condition packages a lower Ricci bound K and an upper dimension bound N into displacement convexity of entropy along Wasserstein geodesics. This synthetic formulation needs no smooth structure, so it travels to singular spaces while still forcing the geometric concentration that isoperimetry detects.

The Levy-Gromov Inequality

Hypothesis

$\mathrm{Ric}\ge K>0$ and dimension $N$ on a closed manifold.

Conclusion

Every region's boundary is at least that of a spherical cap of equal volume fraction.

Profile comparison

$$\frac{\operatorname{Per}(A)}{\mathrm{vol}(M)} \;\ge\; \frac{\operatorname{Per}(\tilde A)}{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}^N)},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{vol}(A)}{\mathrm{vol}(M)}=\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\tilde A)}{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}^N)}$$

Reading

Curvature alone, not the detailed shape, sets the floor on perimeter.

Printed pp. 545-566Levy-Gromov Inequality
Levy-Gromov states that on a manifold with Ricci at least K and dimension N, any region's boundary measure is at least that of a spherical cap enclosing the same volume fraction in the model sphere. Curvature alone, not the detailed shape, sets the floor on perimeter.

Jacobian and Volume Distortion

Distortion variable

Track the Jacobian determinant $\mathcal{J}(t)$ of the interpolating map.

Bochner control

Ricci curvature governs the concavity of $\mathcal{J}^{1/N}$.

Curvature-dimension ODE

$$\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\,\mathcal{J}(t)^{1/N} \;\le\; -\frac{K}{N}\,\mathcal{J}(t)^{1/N}$$

Consequence

A region cannot shrink its boundary faster than the model allows.

Printed pp. 545-566Jacobian and Volume Distortion
Along the transport interpolation the Jacobian determinant obeys a concavity governed by Ricci curvature through the Bochner formula. Lower curvature bounds the determinant's growth, so a region cannot shrink its boundary faster than the model allows. This pointwise inequality is the analytic engine of the comparison.

Functional Form and Deficit

Softened twin

Gaussian and exponential concentration are functional isoperimetry.

Stability

A near-optimal region must resemble the ball up to small error.

Isoperimetric deficit

$$\delta(A)=\frac{\operatorname{Per}(A)}{\operatorname{Per}(\tilde A)}-1\;\ge\;0$$

The deficit vanishes exactly at the model region.

Quantitative

Small deficit controls the symmetric difference from a ball.

Isoperimetry has a softened functional twin: Gaussian and exponential concentration, and a sharp deficit measuring distance from the extremal ball. The deficit vanishes exactly at the model region, and stability theorems control how a near-optimal region must resemble that ball up to small error.
