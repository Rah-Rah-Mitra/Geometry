Monge's Problem (1781)

The picture

Move a pile of earth (deblais) into a target embankment (remblais).

The unknown

A map $T$ sending each location $x$ to a destination $T(x)$.

The rule

Mass is conserved: $T$ must push $\mu$ forward onto $\nu$.

Monge cost

$\displaystyle \inf_{T_\#\mu=\nu}\ \int c\big(x,T(x)\big)\,d\mu(x)$

Constraint

$T_\#\mu=\nu$: the image measure of $\mu$ under $T$ equals $\nu$.

Printed pp. 1-4Monge's Problem
Stress the physical origin: Monge in 1781 asked how to move a pile of earth into an embankment with least total work. The unknown is a map sending each grain to a destination while preserving mass. Emphasize that the constraint, not just the cost, is what makes the problem hard.

Why Monge Is Hard

No splitting

A map sends each point to one image; mass at $x$ cannot be divided.

Existence can fail

One source atom feeding two targets admits no map at all.

Nonlinear

The cost depends nonlinearly on $T$; the admissible set is not convex.

Printed pp. 1-4Why Monge Is Hard
Make the difficulty concrete. A deterministic map cannot split mass, so it may simply fail to exist when a single source atom must feed several targets. Point out that the cost is highly nonlinear in the map and the admissible set is awkward, which stalled the problem for over a century.

Kantorovich's Relaxation (1942)

Plans, not maps

Allow mass at $x$ to be split among many destinations.

Couplings

A plan $\pi$ is a measure on $X\times Y$ with marginals $\mu,\nu$.

Convex

The feasible set is convex; a minimizer exists under mild conditions.

Kantorovich problem

$\displaystyle \inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\ \int c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)$

Generalizes Monge

Every map $T$ gives a plan $\pi=(\mathrm{id},T)_\#\mu$.

Printed pp. 1-4Kantorovich's Relaxation
Introduce the decisive idea: replace maps by transport plans, that is couplings of the two measures. Now mass may split, the feasible set is convex, and a minimizer always exists under mild conditions. Note that every Monge map induces a plan, so Kantorovich genuinely enlarges Monge.

The Cost Functional

Local cost

$c(x,y)$ is the price of moving one unit from $x$ to $y$.

Quadratic cost

$c(x,y)=\tfrac12\,|x-y|^2$ is the one that opens geometry.

The cost decides

It fixes the structure of maps and the metric to come.

Total cost of a plan

$\displaystyle \mathcal{C}(\pi)=\int_{X\times Y} c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)$

Marginal constraints

$\displaystyle \pi(A\times Y)=\mu(A),\quad \pi(X\times B)=\nu(B)$

Printed pp. 1-4The Cost Functional
Write the objective explicitly and read it aloud. The total cost integrates the local cost against the plan, and the quadratic cost is the one that links transport to geometry. Mention that the choice of cost determines everything downstream, from the structure of optimal maps to the metric that appears later.

The Dual Viewpoint

Prices

Replace transport by a pair of price functions $\varphi,\psi$.

Constraint

Admissible prices obey $\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le c(x,y)$.

Potentials

The optimal $\varphi$ is a Kantorovich potential; its gradient rebuilds $T$.

Strong duality

$\displaystyle \min_{\pi}\int c\,d\pi \;=\; \max_{\varphi\oplus\psi\le c}\ \int\varphi\,d\mu+\int\psi\,d\nu$

Why it matters

Nearly every existence proof in the book runs through this identity.

Printed pp. 1-4Kantorovich Duality
Present duality as the workhorse of the theory. Maximizing prices over the c-conjugacy constraint matches the minimum transport cost. The optimal potentials are the Kantorovich potentials, and their gradient will reconstruct the optimal map. Tell students that almost every existence proof in the book passes through this duality.

The Old: Structure of Optimal Maps

Brenier's theorem

For quadratic cost the optimal map is $T=\nabla\phi$ with $\phi$ convex.

Cyclical monotonicity

The geometric fingerprint of an optimal support.

Part One

Existence, the Monge problem, regularity, and smoothness.

Brenier map

$\displaystyle T(x)=\nabla\phi(x),\qquad \phi \text{ convex}$

Cyclical monotonicity

$\displaystyle \sum_i |x_i-y_i|^2 \le \sum_i |x_i-y_{\sigma(i)}|^2$

Printed pp. 1-4The Old: Structure of Optimal Maps
Summarize the classical achievements. Under quadratic cost the optimal plan concentrates on the graph of a map that is the gradient of a convex function, Brenier's theorem. Cyclical monotonicity is the geometric fingerprint of optimality. These results, with regularity and the Monge problem, form Part One of the book.

The Wasserstein Distance

A metric on measures

The optimal quadratic cost yields a genuine distance.

Geometry appears

$(\mathcal{P}_2(X),W_2)$ is a metric space we can do geometry on.

What it unlocks

Geodesics, curvature, and gradient flows of distributions.

Wasserstein distance

$\displaystyle W_2(\mu,\nu)=\Big(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int |x-y|^2\,d\pi\Big)^{1/2}$

Bridge to Part Two

This is the object that turns transport into geometry.

Printed pp. 1-4Wasserstein Distance
Define the metric that makes transport geometric. The square root of the optimal quadratic cost is the Wasserstein distance, turning the space of probability measures into a metric space. Emphasize that this is the object that lets us speak of geodesics, curvature, and gradient flows among distributions.

Displacement Interpolation

Geodesics of measures

Move each particle straight from $x$ to $T(x)$ at constant speed.

McCann's path

$\mu_t=\big((1-t)\,\mathrm{id}+t\,T\big)_\#\mu$ is a $W_2$-geodesic.

The hinge

Convexity of entropy along $\mu_t$ encodes curvature.

Printed pp. 1-4Displacement Interpolation
Explain that Wasserstein geodesics move mass along straight particle paths, McCann's displacement interpolation. This dynamic picture is the hinge between old and new: convexity of entropy along these geodesics encodes Ricci curvature. Tell students this is where transport stops being a logistics problem and becomes differential geometry.

The New: Curvature, Flows, Inequalities

Ricci, synthetically

Lower Ricci bounds $\Leftrightarrow$ displacement convexity of entropy.

Otto calculus

Heat flow is the gradient flow of entropy in $W_2$.

Inequalities

Functional inequalities become convexity statements.

Lott–Sturm–Villani idea

$\displaystyle \mathrm{Ric}\ge K \iff \mathrm{Ent}\ \text{is}\ K\text{-convex along } \mu_t$

Heat as gradient flow

$\displaystyle \partial_t\mu_t=-\nabla_{W_2}\,\mathrm{Ent}(\mu_t)$

Printed pp. 1-4The New: Curvature and Gradient Flows
Lay out the modern arc. Displacement convexity of entropy is equivalent to lower Ricci bounds, the Lott-Sturm-Villani synthetic definition. The heat equation is the gradient flow of entropy in Wasserstein space, Otto calculus. Functional inequalities then follow as convexity statements. This is the heart of the book's novelty.
