Duality

Brenier's Theorem: Map = Gradient of Convex Potential

When the plan is a map

Quadratic cost plus an absolutely continuous source give a deterministic $T$.

Printed pp. 333-352Brenier's Theorem
Under the squared distance and an absolutely continuous source, the optimal plan collapses to a single-valued map equal to the gradient of a convex function. Brenier's theorem fuses optimal transport with convex analysis: no mass is split, and the convex potential is unique up to an additive constant.

Cyclical Monotonicity: the Geometry of Optimality

No profitable swap

No finite cycle of pairs can be relabelled to lower the total cost.

Quadratic case

This is exactly the subdifferential of a convex function — hence a gradient.

$c$-cyclical monotonicity

$\sum_i c(x_i,y_i)\le\sum_i c(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)})$

Printed pp. 333-352Cyclical Monotonicity
Cyclical monotonicity is the geometric fingerprint of optimality: no finite cycle of source-target pairs can be rearranged to lower the total cost. For the quadratic cost this is exactly the subdifferential of a convex function, which is why the map turns out to be a gradient. The support never crosses itself wastefully.

The Jacobian and the Monge-Ampere Equation

Mass conservation

Pushing $\mu$ through $T$ ties the densities by the Jacobian determinant.

Hessian = Jacobian

Since $T=\nabla\psi$, $DT=D^2\psi$, the Hessian of the convex potential.

Monge-Ampere

$\det\!\big(D^2\psi(x)\big)=\dfrac{f(x)}{g(\nabla\psi(x))}$

Analytic face

A fully nonlinear PDE; its solvability and smoothness drive regularity theory.

Printed pp. 333-352Monge-Ampere Equation
Pushing the source density through the map and conserving mass yields a Jacobian equation. With the map a convex gradient, its Jacobian is the Hessian of the potential, so mass conservation becomes the Monge-Ampere equation. This fully nonlinear PDE is the analytic face of the map and the gateway to regularity theory.

Displacement Interpolation and Wasserstein Geodesics

Straight-line travel

Each particle moves at constant speed from $x$ to its image $T(x)$.

Geodesic of densities

The curve $(\mu_t)$ is a constant-speed geodesic in Wasserstein space $P_2$.

McCann interpolation

$T_t(x)=(1-t)\,x+t\,T(x),\quad \mu_t=(T_t)_\#\mu$

Printed pp. 333-352Displacement Interpolation
Interpolate by letting each particle travel along the straight segment from its source to its Brenier image at constant speed. The resulting path of measures is a constant-speed geodesic of the Wasserstein space, and McCann's displacement interpolation is the curve along which energies become convex. Interpolation can expose regularity the endpoints hide.

The Dynamic Picture: Benamou-Brenier

Fluid mechanics

A density $\rho_t$ and velocity $v_t$ obey the continuity equation.

Least kinetic action

Minimising integrated kinetic energy returns $W_2^2$.

Continuity + action

$\partial_t\rho+\nabla\!\cdot(\rho\,v)=0,\;\; W_2^2=\inf\!\int_0^1\!\!\int\rho|v|^2$

Printed pp. 333-352Benamou-Brenier
The dynamic viewpoint replaces the static plan with a fluid flow. A density and a velocity field obey the continuity equation, and minimising the integrated kinetic energy recovers the squared Wasserstein distance. Benamou and Brenier turned transport into a least-action fluid-mechanics problem, with the optimal velocity a gradient field along the geodesic.

The Lagrangian Picture: Least-Action Trajectories

Follow each particle

Trace trajectories $\gamma(t)$ rather than the whole density.

Geodesics, no crossings

Paths minimise action and never intersect before time one.

Action minimisation

$\inf\int_0^1 \tfrac12|\dot\gamma(t)|^2\,dt,\quad \gamma(0)=x,\;\gamma(1)=T(x)$

Printed pp. 333-352Lagrangian Picture
The Lagrangian reading follows individual trajectories rather than the whole density. Each particle minimises an action, the trajectories are geodesics in the base space, and they never cross before the final time. This least-action picture connects optimal transport to classical mechanics and Hamilton-Jacobi theory through the evolving potential.
