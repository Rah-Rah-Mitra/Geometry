What Is a Coupling?

Definition

A coupling of $\mu$ and $\nu$ is a random pair $(X,Y)$ with $\mathrm{law}(X)=\mu$ and $\mathrm{law}(Y)=\nu$.

Existence is free

The product measure $\mu\otimes\nu$ (independent $X,Y$) always couples. The art is to do better than independence.

Why it helps

Any property holding pointwise for $(X,Y)$ averages into a property of $\mu$ versus $\nu$.

Marginal constraints

$$\pi(A\times\mathcal{Y})=\mu(A),\quad \pi(\mathcal{X}\times B)=\nu(B)$$

Set of couplings

$$\Pi(\mu,\nu)\ \text{is convex, nonempty, compact}$$

Printed pp. 21-28What Is a Coupling
A coupling of two probability measures is any joint law whose two marginals recover them. Couplings always exist, since the product measure is one, and the freedom in choosing the joint law is exactly the leverage every argument in this chapter exploits to its advantage.

Coupling as a Proof Method

The recurring move

Push all difficulty into building a clever joint law; then read off the bound coordinatewise.

Pointwise to distributional

An inequality $f(X,Y)\le g(X,Y)$ that holds almost surely integrates to a statement about $\mu$ and $\nu$.

Template

$$\text{hard:}\ \ \text{compare }\mu,\nu \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{easy:}\ \ \text{estimate }(X,Y)\ \text{pointwise}$$

Printed pp. 21-28Coupling as a Proof Method
The recurring move is to push the difficulty into the construction of a joint law and then read off a bound pointwise. A pathwise or coordinatewise inequality, once integrated against the coupling, becomes a statement about the two original distributions for free.

Example 1 — The Monotone Coupling on $\mathbb{R}$

Match the quantiles

Set $Y=T(X)$ where $T=F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_\mu$ matches the $p$-th quantile of $\mu$ to that of $\nu$.

Increasing map

$T$ is nondecreasing, so $(X,Y)$ are comonotone. Optimal for every convex cost at once.

Printed pp. 21-28The Monotone Coupling on the Line
On the real line the optimal coupling is explicit: match equal quantiles. The increasing rearrangement sends the p-th quantile of mu to the p-th quantile of nu, giving a nondecreasing transport map. This deterministic coupling is optimal for every convex cost simultaneously.

Rearrangement & Correlation Inequalities

Comonotone pairing

The increasing coupling makes large $X$ meet large $Y$. Any supermodular score is then maximized.

Hardy-Littlewood

$$\int X Y\,d\pi \ \le\ \int F_\mu^{-1}(p)\,F_\nu^{-1}(p)\,dp$$

Chebyshev sum / correlation

$$\mathbb{E}[fg]\ \ge\ \mathbb{E}[f]\,\mathbb{E}[g]\quad (f,g\ \text{monotone, same sense})$$

One construction, many corollaries

Each inequality becomes a pointwise comparison once the quantile matching is fixed.

Printed pp. 21-28Rearrangement and Correlation Inequalities
Coupling two variables through their shared quantile makes them comonotone, so any supermodular score is maximized. This single construction yields Hardy-Littlewood rearrangement bounds and Chebyshev's correlation inequality, each becoming a pointwise comparison after the increasing matching is fixed.

Example 2 — Synchronous Langevin Coupling

Shared noise

Two diffusions, different starts, one Brownian motion: $dX_t=-\nabla V(X_t)dt+\sqrt 2\,dB_t$, same $B$ for both.

Gap dynamics

$$\tfrac{d}{dt}\tfrac12|X_t-Y_t|^2 = -\langle \nabla V(X_t)-\nabla V(Y_t),\,X_t-Y_t\rangle$$

Printed pp. 21-28Synchronous Langevin Coupling
Run two diffusions from different starts but drive them with one shared Brownian motion. Under convexity of the potential the gap between the trajectories contracts deterministically, so shared noise converts an analytic spectral-gap question into a visible pathwise shrinking distance.

Pathwise Control $\Rightarrow$ Wasserstein Decay

Coupling estimate

The coupled pair $(X_t,Y_t)$ is a candidate joint law of the two time-$t$ distributions $\mu_t,\nu_t$.

Contraction

$$W_2(\mu_t,\nu_t)^2 \le \mathbb{E}\,|X_t-Y_t|^2 \le e^{-2\kappa t}\,|X_0-Y_0|^2$$

$\kappa$-convex potential

If $\nabla^2 V \succeq \kappa I$ with $\kappa>0$, the gap decays at rate $\kappa$ for every starting pair.

Convergence to equilibrium

Taking $\nu_0$ the stationary law gives exponential mixing of $\mu_t$ — an analytic theorem proved by a pointwise pathwise bound.

Printed pp. 21-28From Pathwise Control to Wasserstein
The coupled trajectories give a candidate joint law of the two time-t distributions, so their Wasserstein distance is bounded by the expected pathwise gap. A coordinate inequality holding almost surely upgrades immediately to exponential convergence of the laws to equilibrium.

Example 3 — Knothe-Rosenblatt Maps

No canonical order in $\mathbb{R}^n$

Build the map coordinate by coordinate: match the first marginal, then each conditional in turn.

Triangular structure

$$T_k(x) = T_k(x_1,\dots,x_k),\quad \partial_k T_k \ge 0$$

Printed pp. 21-28Knothe-Rosenblatt Triangular Maps
When no canonical order exists in higher dimension, build a map one coordinate at a time. Each conditional is matched by its own increasing rearrangement, producing a triangular map with nonnegative Jacobian whose structure makes determinant and trace estimates tractable.

A Transport Route to Isoperimetry

Map body onto competitor

Transport a set $K$ of fixed volume onto the unit ball $B$ using the triangular map $T$.

AM-GM on eigenvalues

$$\operatorname{div} T = \sum_k \partial_k T_k \ \ge\ n\big(\det \nabla T\big)^{1/n}$$

Volume constraint becomes surface bound

$$n\,|K|^{1/n}|B|^{(n-1)/n} \le \int_{\partial K}\!\! T\cdot\nu \le \mathrm{Per}(K)$$

Isoperimetric inequality

The ball minimizes perimeter at fixed volume — recovered from one pointwise change-of-variables estimate.

Printed pp. 21-28A Transport Route to Isoperimetry
Transport a reference body onto a competitor and track the Jacobian of the triangular map. The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality applied to its eigenvalues turns the volume constraint into a surface bound, recovering the isoperimetric inequality from a pointwise change-of-variables estimate.

Choosing the Right Coupling

Order $\rightarrow$ monotone matching

Comparison & rearrangement inequalities exploit the line's order through quantile pairing.

Convex drift $\rightarrow$ shared noise

Contraction to equilibrium exploits $\nabla^2 V\succeq\kappa I$ via synchronous coupling.

Conditioning $\rightarrow$ triangular map

Geometric inequalities exploit a triangular Jacobian built conditional by conditional.

Common skeleton

$$\text{clever }\pi \;+\; \text{pointwise inequality} \;\xrightarrow{\ \int d\pi\ }\; \text{distributional theorem}$$

The unifying lesson

Verification always collapses to one inequality evaluated pointwise, then averaged against the coupling.

Printed pp. 21-28Choosing the Right Coupling
The three examples differ only in which joint law is clever. Monotone matching exploits order, synchronous noise exploits convex drift, triangular maps exploit conditioning. In every case the verification collapses to one inequality evaluated pointwise and then averaged against the coupling.

Summary & Takeaways

Coupling is a method

Not just a definition: a flexible way to prove comparison theorems by construction.

Three faces

Contraction (Langevin), comparison (rearrangement), and transport-based inequality (Knothe-Rosenblatt).

The pattern to remember

Build a joint law making the inequality obvious pointwise; integrate; the marginals certify the result.

One line to keep

$$\text{hard comparison of }\mu,\nu \;\longrightarrow\; \text{easy pointwise estimate}$$
