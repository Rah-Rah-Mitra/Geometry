From Kantorovich Plan to Monge Map

Setting

$\mu$ absolutely continuous, $\nu$ arbitrary, cost the squared distance.

Quadratic cost

$c(x,y)=\tfrac12\, d(x,y)^2$

Question

When does the optimal plan $\pi$ concentrate on a graph $y=T(x)$?

Optimal plan

$\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu),\quad \mathrm{supp}\,\pi\ \text{is}\ c\text{-cyclically monotone}$

Plan vs. map

A map is a plan whose support is single-valued in $y$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$.

Strategy

Show the support is locally a graph, then identify $T$ from local data.

Kantorovich gives an optimal plan supported on a c-cyclically monotone set. The local approach asks when that support is actually a graph, so the plan collapses to a genuine transport map.

The $c$-Concave Potential

From monotonicity

A $c$-cyclically monotone set lies in the $c$-subdifferential $\partial_c\psi$ of some $c$-concave $\psi$.

c-transform

$\psi(x)=\inf_{y}\big[c(x,y)-\phi(y)\big]$

Contact / support

$(x,y)\in\mathrm{supp}\,\pi \;\Rightarrow\; \psi(x)+\phi(y)=c(x,y)$

Analogue

$\psi$ plays the role of Brenier's convex potential; the curved cost replaces the inner product.

Key move

The destination $y$ is now constrained by how $\psi$ touches $c(\cdot,y)$ at $x$.

Printed pp. 215-272The c-Concave Potential
Cyclical monotonicity packages the support inside the c-subdifferential of a c-concave potential psi. This potential is the manifold analogue of Brenier's convex function. Everything downstream is read from psi, so understanding how psi constrains destinations is the heart of the local method we develop here.

The Pointwise Selection Rule

Contact at $x$

If $\psi$ is differentiable at $x$ and $(x,y)$ is a contact pair, the function $z\mapsto c(z,y)-\psi(z)$ has a minimum at $x$.

Stationarity

$\nabla_x c(x,y)+\nabla\psi(x)=0$

Reading

The destination $y$ must produce a prescribed cost gradient at $x$. That is purely local information.

Printed pp. 215-272The Pointwise Selection Rule
At a point x where psi is differentiable, the contact condition forces the gradient of the cost in its first slot to balance the gradient of psi. This is a stationarity equation: any destination y in the support must make the map of x plus the cost gradient vanish. It selects y from purely local data.

Twist: Turning the Rule into a Map

Twist condition

$y\mapsto \nabla_x c(x,y)\ \text{is injective for each fixed }x$

Consequence

The stationarity equation $\nabla_x c(x,y)=-\nabla\psi(x)$ has at most one solution $y$.

Quadratic cost

For $c=\tfrac12 d^2$, $\nabla_x c(x,y)=-\exp_x^{-1}(y)$ wherever $\exp_x$ is a diffeomorphism.

Where twist holds

Away from the cut locus, $\exp_x$ inverts uniquely — twist is satisfied.

Outcome

Set-valued support collapses to a function $x\mapsto T(x)$.

Printed pp. 215-272Twist and Single-Valued Maps
The twist condition demands that fixing x and the cost gradient pins down a unique y. For the squared distance this holds away from the cut locus, where the exponential map is a diffeomorphism. Twist is exactly what turns the stationarity equation into a function rather than a set-valued relation.

The Cost-Exponential Formula

The optimal map

$$T(x)=\exp_x\!\big(\nabla\psi(x)\big)$$

Inversion

Solve $\nabla_x c(x,T(x))=-\nabla\psi(x)$ via $\exp_x^{-1}(T(x))=\nabla\psi(x)$.

Geometric meaning

$\nabla\psi(x)\in T_xM$ is the initial velocity of the minimizing geodesic from $x$ to $T(x)$.

Flat case check

On $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\exp_x(v)=x+v$, so $T(x)=x+\nabla\psi(x)=\nabla\big(\tfrac{|x|^2}{2}+\psi\big)$ — Brenier's gradient map.

Printed pp. 215-272Cost-Exponential Formula
Inverting the twist relation gives the central formula. The destination is the Riemannian exponential of the gradient of psi at x. The gradient of the potential is the initial velocity of the minimizing geodesic running from x to its image, so the map is the time-one flow of that geodesic.

Reading the Map on a Manifold

The recipe

1. Form $\nabla\psi(x)\in T_xM$. 2. Shoot the geodesic in that direction. 3. Stop at time one.

The whole map

$T$ is the time-one map of this gradient-geodesic flow over all source points.

Flat limit

Geodesics straighten to lines; the field becomes Brenier's straight-line transport.

Printed pp. 215-272Reading the Map on a Manifold
Walk the picture: at each source point the potential gradient is a tangent vector. Shoot the geodesic in that direction for unit time and you land at the destination. The whole transport map is this field of geodesics, recovering Brenier's straight-line transport when the manifold is flat Euclidean space.

Why the Gradient Exists Almost Everywhere

Semiconcavity

$x\mapsto \tfrac12 d(x,y)^2$ is locally semiconcave; the infimum $\psi$ inherits this.

Semiconcave bound

$\psi(x)-\tfrac{C}{2}\,|x|^2\ \text{is concave (in a chart)}$

Alexandrov

$\psi\ \text{is twice differentiable }\mathcal{L}^n\text{-a.e.}$

Approximate differentiability

Full-measure differentiability gives a well-defined $\nabla\psi(x)$ to feed the cost-exponential.

Regularity floor

This is the minimum smoothness the local approach needs — no convexity required.

Printed pp. 215-272Semiconcavity and Approximate Differentiability
The squared distance is locally semiconcave, so psi inherits semiconcavity. By Alexandrov's theorem a semiconcave function is twice differentiable almost everywhere, hence approximately differentiable on a full-measure set. That regularity is the minimum needed to define the gradient that drives the cost-exponential formula.

Cut Locus and Negligible Bad Sets

Bad set A

Points where $\psi$ is non-differentiable — a Lipschitz-type negligible set.

Bad set B

Points $x$ whose image $T(x)$ lands on $x$'s cut locus, where $c$ is not smooth.

Why harmless

$\mu\ll\mathcal{L}^n \;\Rightarrow\; \mu(\text{bad sets})=0$

Printed pp. 215-272The Cut Locus and Bad Sets
Two obstructions live on negligible sets: points where psi fails to be differentiable, and points whose destination sits on the cut locus where the squared distance loses smoothness. Absolute continuity of the source gives these sets zero mass, so the formula holds almost everywhere and the gaps never carry transported mass.

McCann's Theorem

Theorem

Let $M$ be a Riemannian manifold, $c=\tfrac12 d^2$, and $\mu\ll\mathrm{vol}$. Then there is a unique optimal map, and

$$T(x)=\exp_x\!\big(\nabla\psi(x)\big)$$

Hypotheses that matter

Absolute continuity of $\mu$; finiteness of the transport cost; $c=\tfrac12 d^2$.

What extends

This is Brenier's theorem with $\nabla(\tfrac12|x|^2)$ replaced by the exponential map.

Method credit

The proof is entirely local: potential, stationarity, twist, negligible bad sets.

Printed pp. 215-272McCann's Theorem
Assembling the pieces yields McCann's theorem: for the quadratic cost on a Riemannian manifold with an absolutely continuous source, there is a unique optimal map, given by the cost-exponential of a c-concave potential's gradient. This is the manifold extension of Brenier's theorem, proven by the local method.

Uniqueness and the Role of Absolute Continuity

Single-valued a.e.

Where $\mu$ has positive density, the selection rule fixes one destination for $\mu$-a.e. $x$.

Uniqueness

$T_1=T_2\quad \mu\text{-almost everywhere}$

Drop the hypothesis

If $\mu$ charges a non-differentiability set, mass can split — the plan stays set-valued.

Counterexample shape

An atom at a "corner" of $\psi$ sends mass to several contact destinations at once.

Takeaway

Absolute continuity is exactly what forbids splitting and forces a map.

Printed pp. 215-272Uniqueness Almost Everywhere
Uniqueness rests on absolute continuity. Where the source density is positive the selection rule is single-valued almost everywhere, so any two optimal maps agree off a null set. Drop absolute continuity and mass can split at non-differentiability points, and the map degenerates back into a genuine plan.
