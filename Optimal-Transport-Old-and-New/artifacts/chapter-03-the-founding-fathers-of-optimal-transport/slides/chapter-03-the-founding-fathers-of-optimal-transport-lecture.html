Monge 1781: Déblais et Remblais

The engineer's question

Cart a pile of earth (the déblai) to fill a hole (the remblai) of equal volume at minimal total work.

Cost = distance

Each grain pays for the distance it is carried; the bill is the sum over all the moved mass.

Printed pp. 29-38Monge 1781: Déblais et Remblais
Begin in 1781 with Monge's memoir on cuttings and fillings. A pile of earth, the deblai, must be carted to fill a hole, the remblai, of equal volume. Every grain travels somewhere, and the engineer pays the total work. Monge fixed the cost equal to the distance carried and asked which assignment minimises that bill.

The Monge Problem

Monge (1781)

$$\inf_{T_\#\mu=\nu}\ \int_{X} c\big(x,\,T(x)\big)\,d\mu(x)$$

Push-forward

$T_\#\mu=\nu$ means $\nu(B)=\mu\big(T^{-1}(B)\big)$ for every set $B$.

The unknown is a map

$T:X\to Y$ assigns to each source point $x$ a single destination $T(x)$.

The cost

$c(x,y)$ is the price of moving a unit from $x$ to $y$ — classically $c(x,y)=|x-y|$.

Printed pp. 29-38The Monge Problem
Formalise Monge as a search over transport maps. A map sends each source point to one target point and must push the source measure forward onto the target. We minimise the integral of the cost of displacement. The constraint that a single point cannot be split is exactly what makes this minimisation delicate and sometimes unsolvable.

Maps Cannot Split Mass

Indivisible mass

All mass at $x$ travels as one block to $T(x)$. A map is a deterministic, single-valued assignment.

The obstruction

One atom forced to feed two separated targets admits no map — an admissible $T$ may fail to exist.

Printed pp. 29-38Transport Maps and Non-Splitting
Stress the rigidity built into a map. Mass at a point is indivisible; it travels as one block to a single destination. Push-forward records that the target histogram is exactly the image of the source under T. When a single atom must supply two separated targets, no admissible map exists, foreshadowing why relaxation is needed.

Monge's Geometric Insight

Rays do not cross

For the distance cost, optimal transport segments never intersect — crossing paths could be uncrossed to shorten the total length.

Normal congruence

The rays cut orthogonally across a family of surfaces — an early geometry of transport directions.

Printed pp. 29-38Monge's Geometric Insight
Monge reasoned geometrically as well as analytically. For the distance cost in the plane he argued the optimal transport rays are straight segments that never cross, since crossing paths could be uncrossed to save length. The rays cut orthogonally across a family of surfaces, an early glimpse of the geometry later formalised through potentials and gradients.

Why the Monge Problem Stalls

Non-convex feasible set

The maps $\{T:T_\#\mu=\nu\}$ are not a convex or closed set; minimising sequences can leave it.

No map at all

If $\mu=\delta_{x_0}$ is a single atom and $\nu$ is spread out, no map satisfies $T_\#\mu=\nu$.

The gap

$$\delta_{x_0}\ \xrightarrow{\ T\ }\ \nu \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nu=\delta_{T(x_0)}$$

A pushed-forward atom stays an atom — it cannot become a spread measure.

Consequence

For over a century existence required extra regularity and stayed largely open.

Printed pp. 29-38Why Maps Can Fail
Here is the structural defect. The Monge problem optimises over maps, a set that is neither convex nor closed, so a minimising sequence can escape the admissible class. A Dirac source has no map to a spread target. Existence therefore requires extra regularity, and for over a century the problem stalled on exactly this point.

Kantorovich 1942: The Relaxation

From maps to plans

Do not track where each grain lands. Instead record how much mass flows between every pair of locations.

A coupling

A transport plan $\pi$ on $X\times Y$ couples $\mu$ and $\nu$; mass may now be split.

Printed pp. 29-38Kantorovich 1942: The Relaxation
Kantorovich's 1942 move was to stop tracking where each grain lands and instead record how much mass flows between every pair of locations. This object is a transport plan, a coupling of the two measures. Maps become a special case where the plan concentrates on a graph, and the search now ranges over a far friendlier set.

The Kantorovich Problem

Kantorovich (1942)

$$\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\ \int_{X\times Y} c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y)$$

Marginal constraints

$$\Pi(\mu,\nu)=\{\pi\ge 0:\ (p_X)_\#\pi=\mu,\ (p_Y)_\#\pi=\nu\}$$

Convex & compact

$\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is convex and weakly compact; the objective is linear in $\pi$.

Always solvable

A minimiser exists — an infinite-dimensional linear program over measures.

Printed pp. 29-38The Kantorovich Problem
State the relaxed problem precisely. We minimise the integral of the cost against a coupling whose two marginals are the prescribed source and target measures. The admissible couplings form a convex, weakly compact set, and the cost is linear, so a minimiser always exists. This is a tractable linear program over measures.

Monge versus Kantorovich

Monge — maps

Search over $T$. Non-convex feasible set. May have no solution. The unknown is deterministic.

Kantorovich — plans

Search over $\pi$. Convex compact set. Always solvable. Mass may split; maps are a special case.

Relaxation never raises the cost: $\inf_\pi \le \inf_T$, with equality under mild conditions (e.g. Brenier).

Printed pp. 29-38Monge versus Kantorovich
Place the two formulations side by side. Monge searches deterministic maps over a non-convex set where solutions may not exist. Kantorovich searches couplings over a convex compact set where they always do, and the optimal cost never exceeds Monge's. Under mild conditions the optimal plan is induced by a map, reconciling the two.

Kantorovich Duality

Dual problem

$$\sup_{\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le c(x,y)}\ \int\varphi\,d\mu+\int\psi\,d\nu$$

Prices as a certificate

The potentials $\varphi,\psi$ price source and target; their constraint says no route is undercut.

No duality gap

The minimal plan cost equals the maximal dual value — a verifiable proof of optimality.

Linear programming

For this linear-programming insight Kantorovich shared the 1975 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics.

Printed pp. 29-38Kantorovich Duality
Relaxation unlocks duality. The minimal transport cost equals a maximisation over pairs of potentials whose sum is bounded by the cost. The potentials act as prices: a certificate that no cheaper plan exists. Kantorovich's linear programming insight earned him the 1975 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for resource allocation.

The Modern Revival

Brenier

For quadratic cost the optimal map is $T=\nabla\phi$ with $\phi$ convex — linking transport to the Monge–Ampère equation.

McCann

Displacement interpolation: a geodesic of measures sliding mass along straight lines.

Otto

Diffusion as gradient flow on Wasserstein space — a Riemannian geometry of probability measures.

Confluence

Optimal transport fuses with fluid mechanics, PDE, and geometry — the subject of the rest of the book.

Printed pp. 29-38The Modern Revival
Trace the renaissance. Brenier identified optimal maps for quadratic cost with gradients of convex potentials, tying transport to the Monge-Ampere equation. McCann built displacement interpolation, Otto recast diffusion as gradient flow on Wasserstein space, and the field fused with fluid mechanics, partial differential equations, and Riemannian geometry.

Synthesis: One Question, Three Acts

Monge poses

The rigid map. Beautiful geometry, but existence is fragile.

Kantorovich relaxes

Plans give convexity, compactness, existence, and duality.

Moderns structure

Potentials, geodesics, and flows make the optimum computable and geometric.

The thread
