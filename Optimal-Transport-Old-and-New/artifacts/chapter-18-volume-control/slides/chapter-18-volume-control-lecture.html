The displacement interpolation

Optimal map

The map $T$ pushes $\mu_0$ to $\mu_1$ along minimizing geodesics.

Intermediate times

Each particle travels at constant speed; $T_t$ interpolates between identity and $T$.

The family

$\mu_t=(T_t)_\#\,\mu_0$ is a geodesic in Wasserstein space.

Interpolated map

$$T_t(x)=\exp_x\!\big(t\,\nabla\psi(x)\big)$$

Displacement interpolation

$$\mu_t=(T_t)_\#\,\mu_0,\qquad t\in[0,1]$$

Printed pp. 493-504The displacement interpolation
Recall the geodesic interpolation between two measures. The optimal map pushes the source forward along minimizing geodesics, and the family of intermediate measures is the displacement interpolation. Each particle rides its own geodesic at constant speed, so the time-t measure is the pushforward of the source by the interpolated map. This is the stage on which volume control plays out.

The Jacobian along geodesics

Mass conservation

The pushforward forces the density to scale by the inverse Jacobian.

What to control

Volume control reduces to controlling $\mathcal{J}(t,x)=\det dT_t(x)$.

Jacobian equation

$$\rho_0(x)=\rho_t\big(T_t(x)\big)\,\mathcal{J}(t,x)$$

Density at time $t$

$$\rho_t\big(T_t(x)\big)=\dfrac{\rho_0(x)}{\mathcal{J}(t,x)}$$

Printed pp. 493-504The Jacobian along geodesics
Mass conservation forces the density of the intermediate measure to equal the source density divided by the Jacobian determinant of the interpolated map. Controlling volume therefore means controlling this Jacobian. We study its evolution along each geodesic, where the determinant of the differential of the transport map encodes exactly how an infinitesimal volume swells or shrinks in transit.

The curvature-dimension hypothesis

Two bounds

$CD(K,N)$: Ricci bounded below by $K$, dimension bounded above by $N$.

Riccati control

The $N$-th root of $\mathcal{J}$ satisfies a one-dimensional comparison.

CD condition

$$\mathrm{Ric}\ge K\,g,\qquad \dim\le N$$

Differential inequality

$$\dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}\,\mathcal{J}^{1/N}\le -\dfrac{K}{N}\,|\dot\gamma|^2\,\mathcal{J}^{1/N}$$

Printed pp. 493-504The curvature-dimension hypothesis
The curvature-dimension condition CD(K,N) bundles a lower Ricci bound K with an upper dimension bound N. It is the exact hypothesis that lets one-dimensional comparison along each geodesic close. Under CD(K,N) the second derivative of the Jacobian's N-th root obeys a Riccati inequality, giving the sharp differential control that propagates into volume bounds.

Distortion coefficients

Model yardstick

$\beta_t^{(K,N)}(d)$ measures volume distortion in the model space of curvature $K$.

Explicit form

Trigonometric for $K>0$, hyperbolic for $K<0$, and $\beta_t\equiv 1$ when $K=0$.

Role

Every Jacobian estimate is phrased relative to $\beta_t$.

Positive curvature, set $\alpha=d\sqrt{K/(N-1)}$

$$\beta_t^{(K,N)}(d)=\left(\dfrac{\sin(t\alpha)}{t\,\sin\alpha}\right)^{N-1}$$

Flat limit

$$\beta_t^{(0,N)}(d)=1$$

Printed pp. 493-504Distortion coefficients
The distortion coefficients beta_t encode how a model space of curvature K and dimension N distorts volume between two points separated by distance d. They are explicit trigonometric or hyperbolic expressions in the rescaled distance. These coefficients are the universal yardstick: every Jacobian estimate in the chapter is phrased as a comparison against beta.

Comparison of the Jacobian

The squeeze

A geodesic bundle cannot contract or expand faster than the model space permits.

Concavity inequality

The $N$-th root of $\mathcal{J}$ dominates a $\beta$-weighted endpoint combination.

Jacobian comparison

$$\mathcal{J}(t,x)^{1/N}\ \ge\ (1-t)\,\beta_{1-t}^{1/N}\;+\;t\,\big(\mathcal{J}(1,x)\,\beta_t\big)^{1/N}$$

Reading

The interior volume element is trapped between explicit upper and lower envelopes.

Printed pp. 493-504Comparison of the Jacobian
The central estimate bounds the N-th root of the Jacobian below by an affine combination weighted by the distortion coefficients. Geometrically, a thin bundle of geodesics cannot contract faster than the model space allows, nor expand faster. This squeezes the intermediate volume element between explicit upper and lower model-space envelopes at every interior time.

Volume bounds on the density

Feed back

Insert the Jacobian comparison into mass conservation.

Displacement convexity

The result bounds $\rho_t$ by the endpoint densities and $\beta$.

Density bound

$$\rho_t(T_t x)^{-1/N}\ \ge\ (1-t)\,\beta_{1-t}^{1/N}\rho_0(x)^{-1/N}+t\,\beta_t^{1/N}\rho_1(Tx)^{-1/N}$$

Consequence

Pointwise upper and lower control of $\mu_t$ from curvature alone.

Printed pp. 493-504Volume bounds on the density
Feeding the Jacobian comparison back through mass conservation bounds the density of the time-t measure. The intermediate density is controlled by the source and target densities scaled by distortion coefficients. This is the displacement-convexity engine: it gives pointwise upper and lower bounds on mu_t purely from curvature, dimension, and the endpoint densities.

Bishop-Gromov comparison

Static shadow

Compare measured balls against the model space of curvature $K$.

Monotonicity

The volume ratio is nonincreasing in the radius.

Same source

Ball growth and transport distortion read one curvature bound.

Bishop-Gromov ratio

$$r\ \longmapsto\ \dfrac{\mathrm{vol}\,B(p,r)}{\mathrm{vol}\,B_K(r)}\quad\text{is nonincreasing}$$

The Bishop-Gromov inequality is the static shadow of these dynamic estimates. Comparing measured balls of different radii against the model space, the volume ratio is monotone nonincreasing. Our geodesic-bundle Jacobian computation reproduces this comparison, showing that ball-volume growth and transport volume distortion are two readings of the same curvature bound.

A curved Brunn-Minkowski inequality

From points to sets

Integrate the pointwise Jacobian bound over interpolated regions.

Set interpolation

$Z_t$ is the set of $t$-midpoints between $A$ and $B$.

Curved Brunn-Minkowski

$$\mathrm{vol}(Z_t)^{1/N}\ \ge\ (1-t)\,\tau\,\mathrm{vol}(A)^{1/N}+t\,\tau'\,\mathrm{vol}(B)^{1/N}$$

Meaning

The integrated form of infinitesimal volume control.

Printed pp. 493-504Brunn-Minkowski reading
Optimal transport upgrades Bishop-Gromov to a curved Brunn-Minkowski inequality. The measure of an interpolated set between two sets dominates a distortion-weighted combination of their measures. This is exactly the integrated form of the pointwise Jacobian bound, turning the infinitesimal volume control into a statement about whole interpolated regions.
