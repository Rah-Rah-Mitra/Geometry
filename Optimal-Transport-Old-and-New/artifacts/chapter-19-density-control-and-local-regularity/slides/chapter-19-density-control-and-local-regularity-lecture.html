The Displacement Interpolant

Setup

Let $\mu_0,\mu_1$ have a unique optimal map $T$. Slide each point a fraction $t$ of the way.

Geodesic in $W_2$

The curve $t\mapsto\mu_t$ is a constant-speed Wasserstein geodesic.

Interpolation map

$T_t(x) = (1-t)\,x + t\,T(x), \qquad \mu_t = (T_t)_{\#}\,\mu_0$

Key idea

Mass is carried by a single coupling, so $\mu_t$ is determined by how $T_t$ distorts volume.

Printed pp. 505-524The Displacement Interpolant
We fix the Wasserstein geodesic joining two measures and push the optimal map forward by a fraction of the displacement. The interpolant inherits its mass from the endpoints through a single optimal coupling, so understanding its density reduces to understanding how the interpolating map distorts volume at each time.

Why Density Can Blow Up

The hazard

Transport rays that focus toward a common region force mass together; the Jacobian collapses and density diverges.

Geometric cause

Sharply contracting geodesics concentrate mass — a curvature effect we must quantify.

Printed pp. 505-524Why Density Can Blow Up
Without curvature or convexity control, trajectories can focus toward a common region and the Jacobian of the interpolation map can collapse, sending density to infinity. The danger is geometric: geodesics that contract too sharply concentrate mass, so we must quantify how much the interpolation Jacobian is allowed to shrink.

The Jacobian of Interpolation

Change of variables

Density transforms by the determinant of the interpolation map's differential.

Strategy

Bound density by bounding the Jacobian away from zero along every ray.

Density formula

$\rho_t\big(T_t(x)\big)\,\det dT_t(x) = \rho_0(x)$

Consequence

$\rho_t$ is large exactly where $\det dT_t$ is small, so we estimate $\det dT_t$ from below.

Printed pp. 505-524The Jacobian of Interpolation
The density of the interpolant is the endpoint density divided by the Jacobian determinant of the interpolation map. Controlling density therefore means bounding this Jacobian away from zero, and the Jacobian factorizes through the geodesic, exposing the role of volume distortion coefficients along each transport path.

Distortion Coefficients

What they measure

How a geodesic spreads or contracts an infinitesimal bundle of transport rays.

Curvature enters

A lower Ricci bound $\mathrm{Ric}\ge K$ gives explicit comparison estimates $\tau_K^{(t)}$.

Jacobian factorization

$\det dT_t \;\ge\; \big(\tau_K^{(1-t)}(d)\big)^{n}$

Payoff

An abstract curvature hypothesis becomes a concrete pointwise lower bound on the Jacobian.

Printed pp. 505-524Distortion Coefficients
The volume distortion coefficients measure how a Riemannian geodesic spreads or contracts an infinitesimal bundle of transport rays. Under a lower Ricci bound these coefficients obey explicit comparison estimates, and substituting them into the Jacobian formula converts an abstract curvature hypothesis into a concrete pointwise density inequality.

The Density Bound

The estimate

$\rho_t$ at any interior time is capped by a curvature-dependent multiple of the larger endpoint density.

Uniform in time

One ceiling works for the whole family $\{\mu_t\}_{0

Central inequality

$\|\rho_t\|_{\infty} \;\le\; C(K,t)\,\max\big(\|\rho_0\|_{\infty},\,\|\rho_1\|_{\infty}\big)$

Reading it

Bounded endpoints $\Rightarrow$ bounded interior, with $C$ depending only on $K$ and $t$.

Printed pp. 505-524The Density Bound
Putting the pieces together yields the central estimate: the interpolant density at any intermediate time is bounded by a curvature-dependent multiple of the larger endpoint density. The bound is uniform in time, so the entire geodesic family lives below a single ceiling determined only by the endpoints and the lower curvature bound.

Local Regularity

From bounds to smoothness

Uniform density gives quantitative control of the interpolation maps on compact sets.

Rays don't cross

Optimality keeps transport rays disjoint, so velocities stay locally bounded.

Local control

$\sup_{x\in K}\,|\dot T_t(x)| \le L_K, \qquad \rho_t \le M_K \text{ on } K$

Use

One can pass to limits and differentiate along the geodesic without losing geometric control.

Printed pp. 505-524Local Regularity
Once densities are uniformly bounded, the interpolation maps gain quantitative regularity on compact sets. The transport rays do not cross, velocities stay locally bounded, and the family of maps becomes equicontinuous, so one can pass to limits and differentiate along the geodesic without losing control of the geometry.
