Smoothness

When is the optimal map continuous, or even smooth?

The question

Existence gives a map almost everywhere. Regularity asks whether it has no jumps.

The surprise

Smooth densities are not enough. The shape of the target can break continuity.

Printed pp. 281-332Smoothness of Optimal Maps
This chapter spans printed pages 281 to 332 and turns from existence to regularity. We already know a Brenier map exists almost everywhere, but here we ask whether it is continuous, differentiable, or smooth. The answer is delicate and depends jointly on the densities and on the geometry of the target domain.

From Existence to Regularity

Already known

Brenier: a convex potential $\psi$ with $T=\nabla\psi$ pushes $f$ to $g$.

New question

Is $\nabla\psi$ continuous? Differentiable? In $C^\infty$?

Setup

$T=\nabla\psi,\qquad T_\#\,(f\,dx)=g\,dy$

Warning

Smoothness of $f,g$ alone does not imply smoothness of $T$.

Printed pp. 281-332From Existence to Regularity
Existence theory hands us a convex potential whose gradient pushes source to target. Regularity is a separate, harder question. Emphasize that smooth data alone do not guarantee a smooth map; the supports and their shapes matter, and a single bad geometric feature can force the optimal map to jump.

The Monge-Ampere Equation

Change of variables

Push-forward of $f$ by $\nabla\psi$ equal to $g$ becomes a Jacobian identity.

Fully nonlinear

The unknown enters through the determinant of its Hessian.

Monge-Ampere

$$\det\big(D^2\psi(x)\big)=\frac{f(x)}{g(\nabla\psi(x))}$$

Reading

A second-order PDE for $\psi$ whose nonlinearity is the determinant.

Printed pp. 281-332The Monge-Ampere Equation
Differentiate the constraint that the gradient of psi pushes f forward to g. The change of variables yields the Monge-Ampere equation, a fully nonlinear second-order PDE for the potential. Stress that the determinant of the Hessian appears, which is what makes the equation nonlinear and ties regularity to convexity.

Ellipticity Needs Convexity

Where it is elliptic

The determinant operator is degenerate elliptic only on convex $\psi$, where $D^2\psi\succeq 0$.

Off the cone

Outside the convex class ellipticity and the comparison principle break down.

Printed pp. 281-332Ellipticity Needs Convexity
Monge-Ampere is degenerate elliptic only on convex functions, where the Hessian is positive semidefinite. Off the convex class the determinant operator loses ellipticity and the comparison principle fails. Brenier's potential is convex by construction, so we work inside the cone where the linearized operator behaves like a genuine elliptic problem.

Brenier Solutions Are Weak

No derivatives a priori

Brenier's $\psi$ solves the equation only in a measure-theoretic, weak sense.

Monge-Ampere measure

The subdifferential image of a set carries the mass that replaces $\det D^2\psi$.

Weak formulation

$$\mu_\psi(E)=\big|\partial\psi(E)\big|,\qquad \mu_\psi = \tfrac{f}{g\circ\nabla\psi}\,dx$$

Goal

Upgrade weak to classical: continuity, then $C^1$, then $C^\infty$.

Printed pp. 281-332Brenier Solutions Are Weak
Brenier solves the equation in a weak, measure-theoretic sense via the Monge-Ampere measure, with no a priori derivatives. The whole regularity programme is to upgrade this weak solution to a classical one. Make clear that bootstrapping requires two-sided density bounds plus a geometric condition on the target.

Caffarelli's Regularity Theorem

Theorem (Caffarelli)

If $0<\lambda\le f,g\le\Lambda$ on their supports and the target is convex, the Brenier potential $\psi$ is strictly convex and $C^{1,\alpha}$.

Bootstrapping

Smooth $f,g$ then give $\psi\in C^\infty$ by Schauder estimates.

Two ingredients

Two-sided density bounds and convex target. Both are essential.

Load-bearing

Drop convexity of the target and the conclusion fails outright.

Printed pp. 281-332Caffarelli's Regularity Theorem
State Caffarelli's theorem precisely: densities bounded above and below on their supports, and a convex target. Then the Brenier potential is strictly convex and continuously differentiable, and Schauder bootstrapping gives higher smoothness when the data are smooth. Convexity of the target is the load-bearing hypothesis, not a technicality.

Why Target Convexity Matters

Contact sets

Convexity keeps the contact set of $\psi$ from being severed by a hole in the support.

Holes hurt

A gap in the target forces $\partial\psi$ to contain a whole segment: a crease.

Printed pp. 281-332Why Target Convexity Matters
Convexity of the target guarantees the contact set of the convex potential cannot be cut by a hole in the support. If the target is non-convex, mass must travel to disconnected pieces, and the subdifferential of psi develops a segment. Geometrically the potential acquires a ridge, and the map is forced to be discontinuous there.

A Discontinuity Counterexample

Construction

Source: a single disc. Target: two disjoint blobs, no connecting bridge.

The split

Mass near the center must go left or right. No continuous choice works.

Printed pp. 281-332A Discontinuity Counterexample
Walk through Caffarelli's dumbbell picture. Source is a ball, target is two disjoint blobs joined by a thin or absent bridge. Mass near the center must split left and right, so the optimal map jumps across a seam. This is the canonical certificate that without target convexity continuity genuinely fails.

The Kink in the Potential

Jump equals crease

A discontinuous gradient is an edge in the graph of $\psi$ whose $\partial\psi$ is a segment.

Subdifferential

$\partial\psi(x_0)=[\,p_-,\,p_+\,]\ \text{is a segment} \Rightarrow T$ jumps

Printed pp. 281-332The Kink in the Potential
Tie the jump to the potential. A discontinuous gradient corresponds to a crease, an edge in the graph of the convex function whose subdifferential is a whole segment. Contrast the smooth paraboloid-like potential against the creased one; the transport field inherits the jump exactly where the crease lives.

General Costs on Manifolds

Beyond quadratic

On a manifold with cost $c(x,y)$ the potential solves a $c$-Monge-Ampere equation.

c-convexity

Plain convexity is replaced by $c$-convexity, defined through the mixed cost geometry.

c-Monge-Ampere

$$\det\!\big(D^2\psi - D^2_{xx}c\big)=\frac{f}{g}\,\big|\det D^2_{xy}c\big|$$

New obstruction

The mixed Hessian $D^2_{xy}c$ hides a curvature controlling regularity.

Printed pp. 281-332General Costs on Manifolds
Move beyond quadratic cost. On a manifold with a general cost the potential satisfies a c-Monge-Ampere equation, and convexity is replaced by c-convexity. The relevant geometry is now encoded in the mixed derivatives of the cost, setting the stage for a curvature-like obstruction discovered by Ma, Trudinger, and Wang.

The Ma-Trudinger-Wang Tensor

Fourth-order tensor

A cross-curvature built from four cost derivatives, evaluated on tangent pairs $(\xi,\eta)$.

MTW condition

$$\mathfrak{S}_c(\xi,\eta)\ge 0\ \text{whenever}\ \xi\perp\eta$$

Sign is the criterion

Nonnegative MTW supports continuity and regularity of the optimal map.

Negative MTW

Allows singular, discontinuous maps even with smooth, bounded densities.

Printed pp. 281-332The Ma-Trudinger-Wang Tensor
Introduce the MTW tensor as a fourth-order quantity built from the cost, a cross-curvature acting on pairs of tangent vectors. Its sign is the criterion: nonnegative MTW supports continuity and regularity, while negative MTW allows singular maps even with smooth, bounded data. It generalizes target convexity to curved settings.

Positive vs Negative Cost-Curvature

Sphere: regular

Round-sphere distance-squared cost satisfies MTW; optimal maps stay regular.

Negative: singular

Push the cost toward negative cross-curvature and counterexamples return.

Printed pp. 281-332Positive vs Negative Curvature
Contrast regimes. The round sphere satisfies the MTW condition and optimal maps are regular; perturb the cost toward negative cross-curvature and counterexamples reappear. Close by linking MTW positivity to a synthetic curvature of the cost, summarizing that smoothness is a geometric privilege, not a default.
