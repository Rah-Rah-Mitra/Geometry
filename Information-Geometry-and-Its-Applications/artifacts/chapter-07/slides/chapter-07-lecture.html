MLE in a Full Exponential Family

Match expectations

Solve $\eta(\hat\theta)=\bar\eta$ for the estimate.

No detour

In $\eta$-coordinates the MLE is the observed point itself.

MLE equation

$\hat\eta = \bar\eta = \tfrac1N\sum_a t(x_a)$

Consistency

$\bar\eta \to \eta$ as $N\to\infty$ by the law of large numbers.

Printed pp. 165-177MLE in a Full Exponential Family
In a full exponential family the sample mean of the sufficient statistic is an expectation-coordinate point. Matching the model expectation to that observed point gives the maximum likelihood estimate, so in eta coordinates the estimator is simply the observed point itself. There is no geometric detour, only the shrinking error cloud.

Cramer-Rao Bound and the Fisher Ellipse

Cramer-Rao bound

$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta) \succeq G(\theta)^{-1}/N$

Efficiency

Equality at first order defines a Fisher-efficient estimator.

Shrink rate

The ellipse contracts like $1/\sqrt{N}$.

Printed pp. 165-177Cramer-Rao Bound and the Fisher Ellipse
The observed expectation point has covariance about the inverse Fisher information divided by N, drawn as an ellipse that shrinks like one over root N. This is the visual form of the Cramer-Rao lower bound: no unbiased estimator can do better than this Fisher ellipse at first order, and equality defines efficiency.

Curved Families and Projection

Submanifold

A curved family $S$ sits inside a flat ambient family $M$.

Missed target

The observed point usually lands off $S$.

Printed pp. 165-177Curved Families and Projection
A curved exponential family is a lower-dimensional submanifold inside a flat ambient family. The observed point usually misses the model, so an estimator must choose a return rule. The maximum likelihood rule is the information-geometric projection, dropping the observed point onto the curve along the Fisher-normal direction.

Ancillary Fibers Define the Estimator

Fiber $A(u)$

Ambient points sharing one estimate.

Foliation

Fibers give local coordinates $(u,v)$.

Consistency

Each fiber passes through its model point $\eta(u)$.

Printed pp. 165-177Ancillary Fibers Define the Estimator
Fix a model point eta of u; the ambient observations assigned that same estimate form the fiber A of u. Together the fibers foliate a neighborhood, giving local coordinates u along the model and v along the fiber. Consistency forces each fiber to pass through its model point.

Fisher Efficiency as Orthogonality

Angle condition

Efficient fibers meet $S$ orthogonally in the Fisher metric.

Tilt cost

A tilted fiber leaks normal noise into $\hat u$.

Schur precision

$\bar g_{uu} = g_{uu} - g_{uv}\,g_{vv}^{-1}\,g_{vu},\quad \mathrm{Var}(\tilde u) = 1/\bar g_{uu}$

Optimum

Orthogonality kills $g_{uv}$, restoring the Cramer-Rao floor.

Printed pp. 165-177Fisher Efficiency as Orthogonality
Efficiency adds an angle condition: at first order the ancillary fiber must meet the model orthogonally in the Fisher metric. A tilted fiber leaks normal noise into the reported u and inflates variance. Eliminating the fiber coordinate by a Schur complement gives the first-order precision of u-tilde.

Higher-Order Asymptotics and Curvature

Beyond the tangent

Compare bias-corrected efficient estimators at order $1/N$.

Three curvatures

Model, coordinate, and ancillary-fiber contributions.

Second-order covariance

$\mathrm{Cov} = \tfrac1N G^{-1} + \tfrac1{N^2}\big(\Gamma_S + \Gamma_{\text{coord}} + \Gamma_A\big)$

MLE is special

Its ancillary family is m-flat, so $\Gamma_A=0$: second-order efficient.

Printed pp. 165-177Higher-Order Asymptotics and Curvature
First-order theory sees only the tangent. After bias correction, second-order covariance splits into three nonnegative curvature terms: of the model, of the chosen coordinates, and of the ancillary family. The bias-corrected MLE is special because its ancillary family is m-flat, so the estimator-dependent curvature term vanishes.

Testing Shares the Same Geometry

Boundaries as fibers

Rejection boundaries approach the null point as $N$ grows.

Shared first order

Efficient tests have boundaries orthogonal to $S$ at $u_0$.

Second order

Rao, Wald, LR, and LMP tests differ in boundary curvature and power.

Null geometry

$\{\,\bar\eta : \text{reject}\,\} \;\to\; \eta(u_0)\ \text{as}\ N\to\infty$

No free lunch

Outside exponential families, no uniformly best second-order test.

Printed pp. 165-177Testing Shares the Same Geometry
A large-sample test draws a rejection boundary that approaches the null point as N grows; varying the null turns these boundaries into another ancillary family. So estimation and testing share first-order geometry. Rao, Wald, and likelihood-ratio tests agree in the tangent approximation but differ at second order through boundary angle and curvature.

Recap and Boundary Cases

The arc

  • MLE = information projection.
  • Ancillary fibers encode the estimator.
  • Consistency: fibers through the model.
  • Efficiency: orthogonal fibers.

Curvature ledger

Model, coordinate, and fiber curvature explain second-order differences among efficient estimators.

Cautions

MLE optimality need not persist to every order; non-regular models can degenerate or diverge the Fisher metric.

Printed pp. 165-177Recap and Boundary Cases
To summarize: the MLE is a projection, ancillary fibers encode the estimator, consistency needs fibers through the model, efficiency needs orthogonality, and curvature explains second-order differences. The chapter warns that MLE optimality need not persist to every order, and that non-regular models break the Riemannian story entirely.
