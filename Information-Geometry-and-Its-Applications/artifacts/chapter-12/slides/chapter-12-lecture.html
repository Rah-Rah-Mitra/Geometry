Steepest Descent Needs a Metric

The fair unit ball

"Steepest" is undefined until we say what a unit step is. Euclidean: a sphere. Statistical: the Fisher ellipse $d\theta^\top G\, d\theta = \varepsilon$.

Same arrow, different length

Equal screen length is not equal statistical length on a curved manifold.

Constrained descent

$\min_{d\theta}\, \nabla L^\top d\theta \;\; \text{s.t.}\;\; d\theta^\top G\, d\theta = \varepsilon^2$

Solution shape

The minimizer points along $-G^{-1}\nabla L$, not $-\nabla L$.

Printed pp. 279-314Steepest Descent Needs a Metric
Steepest descent is only well defined after we fix what a unit step means. In Euclidean coordinates the fair ball is a sphere and the steepest direction is minus the gradient. On a statistical manifold the fair ball is the Fisher ellipse, so the honest steepest direction is reweighted by the inverse metric.

The Natural Gradient

Definition

$\tilde{\nabla} L = G(\theta)^{-1}\, \nabla L$

Fisher metric

$G_{ij} = \mathbb{E}\big[\partial_i \log p\, \partial_j \log p\big]$

Distribution space

Decreases loss fastest per unit of movement measured in KL, not in coordinates.

Invariance

Unchanged under smooth reparameterization $\theta \mapsto \xi(\theta)$ of the model.

Printed pp. 279-314The Natural Gradient
Solving the constrained steepest-descent problem in the Fisher metric gives the natural gradient: the ordinary gradient multiplied by the inverse Fisher matrix. It is the direction that decreases loss fastest per unit of movement in distribution space, making it invariant to smooth reparameterization of the model.

Euclidean vs Natural-Gradient Paths

Ordinary descent

Sees long narrow contours directly; tiny step needed for stability.

Natural descent

Metric rescaling makes the bowl isotropic, so the path is near-straight.

On a quadratic loss with anisotropic curvature, ordinary descent zig-zags across narrow valleys and must take tiny steps for stability. Natural gradient rescales by the metric, turning the elongated bowl into an isotropic one, so the path heads almost straight to the optimum in far fewer iterations.

Hessian Geometry & the Saddle Safeguard

Indefinite Hessian

Away from a minimum, $H$ has mixed-sign eigenvalues.

Saddle

$L(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 - y^2)$

Newton fails

Signed $H^{-1}$ aims the negative-curvature axis toward the saddle, stalling escape.

Absolute-Hessian fix

$|H| = U\,|\Lambda|\,U^\top$

Effect

Keeps curvature scaling, flips the unstable axis into an escape.

Printed pp. 279-314Hessian Geometry & the Saddle Safeguard
Newton geometry uses the Hessian, which can be indefinite away from a minimum. At a saddle the signed Hessian points the negative-curvature coordinate toward the saddle, stalling escape. Replacing each Hessian eigenvalue by its absolute value keeps curvature scaling while flipping the unstable direction into an escape direction.

Singular Models & Non-Identifiability

One hidden unit

$f(x;w,v) = v\,\tanh(w x)$

Hidden axes

If $v=0$, $w$ is invisible. If $w=0$, $v$ is invisible since $\tanh 0 = 0$.

Rank loss

Distinct parameters give the same distribution, so the Fisher matrix degenerates.

Consequence

Regular asymptotic theory (AIC, $\chi^2$) breaks on these axes.

Printed pp. 279-314Singular Models & Non-Identifiability
A singular model has parameters that produce identical distributions, so the Fisher matrix degenerates and loses rank. The one-hidden-unit map v tanh(w x) shows this: when v is zero, w is invisible, and when w is zero, v is invisible. These unidentifiable axes are exactly where regular asymptotic theory breaks down.

The unidentifiable directions form a singular set, a cross of axes in the parameter plane where Fisher eigenvalues collapse to zero. Plateaus & Blow-Down Coordinates

Plateaus

Near the singular set, identifiable-direction gradients shrink; loss barely moves over many iterations.

Blow-down

Collapse the singular set to one behavior point; dynamics become ordinary again.

Slow vs fast

$\dot\theta_{\text{ord}} \sim 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \text{plateau}$

Diagnosis

The metric reveals which flat directions are redundant rather than genuinely optimal.

Printed pp. 279-314Plateaus & Blow-Down Coordinates
Near the singular set ordinary gradients shrink in the identifiable direction, producing long plateaus where loss barely moves yet many iterations pass. Blow-down coordinates treat the collapsed set as a single behavior point, restoring ordinary dynamics. The metric tells us which directions are redundant rather than genuinely flat.

Learning Coefficient & RLCT

Bayes free energy

$F_n \approx n L_0 + \lambda \log n$

Learning coefficient

$\lambda$ is the real log canonical threshold (RLCT) of the singularity.

Regular case

$\lambda = d/2$ recovers the BIC penalty for $d$ parameters.

Singular case

$\lambda < d/2$: singular models generalize better than parameter counts suggest.

Printed pp. 279-314Learning Coefficient & RLCT
Singular learning theory replaces the parameter count by the real log canonical threshold, the learning coefficient lambda. The Bayes free energy grows like lambda times log n, and for singular models lambda is smaller than half the dimension, so singular models can generalize better than their parameter count suggests.

Damping & Takeaways

Damped natural gradient

$(G + \lambda I)\,d = \nabla L$

Tradeoff

Too little damping: huge steps near a collapsed eigenvalue. Too much: erases the metric correction.

Choose the metric

Natural gradient is the gradient after the statistical metric defines equal movement.

Watch the rank

Track eigenvalues and behavior-level coordinates, not just parameter counts.

Printed pp. 279-314Damping & Takeaways
Practical natural gradient solves a damped system with the inverse of the Fisher matrix plus lambda times the identity, trading a tiny bias for stability near collapsed eigenvalues. The chapter lesson: choose the metric deliberately, watch rank and eigenvalues, and move in behavior space rather than wandering through redundant parameters.
