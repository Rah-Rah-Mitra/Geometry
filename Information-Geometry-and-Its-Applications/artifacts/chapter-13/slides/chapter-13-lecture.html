PCA and Whitening

Covariance ellipse

Eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ are principal and minor axes; eigenvalues are squared axis lengths.

Whitening

Rescale so $\Sigma \to I$: the metric ellipse becomes a unit circle, the ideal start for ICA.

Printed pp. 315-358PCA and Whitening
PCA reads second-order geometry: the covariance draws an ellipse whose eigenvectors are principal axes and whose eigenvalues are squared axis lengths. Whitening rescales the cloud to identity covariance, turning the ellipse into a unit circle. That is the ideal pre-processing for ICA but also exposes the rotational danger we meet on the next slides.

Online Principal-Axis Learning

State on a manifold

The eigenvector estimate is constrained to the unit circle; updates are a trajectory, not Euclidean drift.

Anisotropy trade-off

A smaller minor eigenvalue draws a cleaner ellipse but can slow learning for unlucky step sizes.

Oja-type update

$w_{t+1} = \dfrac{w_t + \gamma\,(x_t^\top w_t)\,x_t}{\lVert w_t + \gamma\,(x_t^\top w_t)\,x_t\rVert}$

Diagnostic

Track the angle error between $w_t$ and the true principal axis as data streams.

Printed pp. 315-358Online Principal-Axis Learning
An online eigenvector update keeps its state on the unit circle, so learning is a trajectory on a manifold rather than free Euclidean drift. We track the angle error as data streams in. Stronger covariance anisotropy makes the metric ellipse easy to draw, yet it can slow convergence for unlucky step sizes, a tension worth probing in the lab cell.

ICA: A Rotation Remains

Where PCA stops

After whitening $\Sigma=I$, so second-order geometry has no preference left. Independence is not visible in covariance.

One angle to find

In 2D the residual ambiguity is a single rotation; a non-Gaussian contrast selects it.

Printed pp. 315-358ICA: A Rotation Remains
After whitening the covariance is identity, so PCA expresses no further preference. ICA demands independent coordinates, which covariance alone cannot detect. In two dimensions the entire remaining search is one rotation angle. A non-Gaussian contrast score selects that angle, recovering the original independent source axes from the linearly mixed observations.

The Gaussian Failure Mode

Non-Gaussian sources

Heavy or flat tails carve preferred directions; the fourth-moment contrast has a sharp peak.

Gaussian sources

Every rotation has identical covariance and marginals, so the angle is unidentifiable.

Contrast (excess kurtosis)

$J(\theta) = \sum_i \big|\,\mathbb{E}[y_i^4] - 3\,\big|, \quad y = R(\theta)\,x$

Lab check

Replace sources with two Gaussians: the contrast flattens and the recovered-source correlation stops being meaningful.

Printed pp. 315-358The Gaussian Failure Mode
Non-Gaussian sources carve preferred directions into the data, so the fourth-moment contrast has a sharp optimum. Two Gaussian sources flatten the contrast: every rotation has identical covariance and identical Gaussian marginals, so the rotation is unidentifiable. This failure mode is the cleanest demonstration of why independent component analysis fundamentally needs non-Gaussianity.

NMF: Positive-Cone Geometry

Cones, not axes

Basis vectors need not be orthogonal or independent; they and the activations must stay non-negative.

Additive parts

Data is a sum of non-negative rays, so a valid factorization never requires negative parts.

Printed pp. 315-358NMF: Positive-Cone Geometry
NMF drops orthogonality and independence and demands non-negativity instead. Data, basis rays, and activations all live in the non-negative cone, so a valid factorization is built from additive parts only. Multiplicative updates make this visible: multiplying by non-negative ratios can never turn a positive factor negative, protecting the cone assumption from positive initialization onward.

Sparse Models: The L1 Diamond

Constrained projection

The exact solution projects the unconstrained optimum onto the L1 diamond under the quadratic loss metric.

The wrong shortcut

Radial Euclidean shrinkage ignores the metric; with anisotropy it lands on the wrong vertex.

Printed pp. 315-358Sparse Models: The L1 Diamond
Sparsity constrains the solution to an L1 ball, which in two dimensions is a diamond. The exact answer projects the unconstrained optimum onto that polygon under the quadratic loss metric. Radial Euclidean shrinkage is a tempting shortcut, but when the metric is anisotropic it lands at the wrong vertex and the measured objective gap grows.

Bregman and Mirror Descent

Dual-coordinate steps

A convex potential $\psi$ maps primal points to dual coordinates; gradient steps act there, then map back.

Natural gradient

The Fisher-metric instance: the proximal term is a KL-type Bregman divergence.

Mirror / proximal step

$x_{t+1} = \arg\min_x \;\langle \nabla f(x_t), x\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\gamma} D_\psi(x \,\Vert\, x_t)$

Printed pp. 315-358Bregman and Mirror Descent
Mirror descent runs gradient steps in dual coordinates produced by a convex potential, then maps back through its gradient. The proximal step minimizes a linearized loss plus a Bregman divergence, so the geometry of the potential, not the Euclidean metric, controls the trajectory. The natural-gradient method is the Fisher-metric instance of exactly this scheme.

Barrier-Induced Dual Geometry

Barrier as metric

For the box $-1<\theta_i<1$ the log barrier $\psi$ supplies a Hessian metric and a gradient dual map.

Central path

Bends toward the boundary in primal $\theta$; becomes a straight ray in dual $\eta=\nabla\psi$.

Printed pp. 315-358Barrier-Induced Dual Geometry
A logarithmic barrier supplies both a metric, through its Hessian, and dual coordinates, through its gradient. The central path minimizes a weighted cost plus the barrier. In primal coordinates it bends toward the boundary, yet in dual coordinates eta equals grad psi it is the straight ray eta equals minus t times c, exactly the dually flat picture.

Proper Scores and the Hyvarinen Score

A game vs. Nature

A proper score is minimized in expectation by the true distribution, so honest reporting wins.

Normalizer-free

The Hyvarinen score uses derivatives of $\log q$, so unnormalized models are scored directly.

Hyvarinen divergence

$D_H(p:q) = \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}_p\!\Big[\big(\tfrac{d\log p}{dx} - \tfrac{d\log q}{dx}\big)^2\Big]$

Certifying check

The contour surface is accepted only because the score-field identity and normalization invariance agree numerically.

Printed pp. 315-358Proper Scores and Hyvarinen
A proper score makes estimation a game against Nature: the true distribution should minimize the expected score. The Hyvarinen score uses derivatives of the log density rather than its normalizer, so unnormalized models are scored directly. Its divergence equals half the expected squared difference of score fields, an identity we verify by numerical integration before trusting the surface.
