Primal potential

Convex $\psi(\theta)$ generates $\eta = \nabla\psi(\theta)$.

Dual potential

Convex $\varphi(\eta) = \nabla\varphi$ inverts to $\theta$.

A convex potential psi of theta generates the dual coordinates by its gradient, eta equals grad psi. The Legendre transform produces a second convex potential phi of eta whose gradient inverts the map. The two potentials are tied by a single scalar identity that drives the canonical divergence.

Canonical Divergence

Bregman form

Built from one point in $\theta$, the other in $\eta$.

Properties

Nonnegative, zero on the diagonal, asymmetric.

Bernoulli check

Equals reversed KL to $10^{-10}$ in the notebook.

Definition

$D[P:Q] = \psi(\theta_P) + \varphi(\eta_Q) - \theta_P\!\cdot\!\eta_Q$

Orientation

$D[P:Q] \neq D[Q:P]$ away from the diagonal.

Printed pp. 131-161Canonical Divergence
The canonical divergence is the Bregman gap between the potential surface and its supporting tangent plane. It is nonnegative, vanishes exactly on the diagonal, and is generally asymmetric. For a Bernoulli model it equals the reversed Kullback-Leibler divergence, which the notebook verifies to ten decimal places.

Pitfalls and Recap

Watch out

  • Dual connections share one metric; never two independent geometries.
  • A flat chart is not Euclidean: contours stay curved and asymmetric.
  • Orientation matters: confirm $D[P:Q]$ versus $D[Q:P]$.

Take away

  • Convex potentials become coordinates, projections, and divergences.
  • Pythagoras needs orthogonal primal/dual submanifolds.
  • Cuts are projections; leftover divergence is the lesson.
Printed pp. 131-161Pitfalls and Recap
Remember that dual connections share one metric and are never independent geometries, that a flat chart is not Euclidean, and that divergence orientation matters. Mixed coordinates only yield Pythagoras when submanifolds meet orthogonally in the dual sense. Cuts are projections; their leftover divergence is the lesson.
