Bilinear Forms Are Two-Slot Measuring Machines

A basis turns the form into a matrix; linearity lives in each input slot.

Definition Pattern

A bilinear form assigns a scalar to two vectors and is linear in each argument separately.

B(v,w)=vTMw
  • The matrix records all pairings of basis vectors.
  • Changing coordinates changes the matrix, not the underlying pairing.
  • Symmetry is an additional condition, not part of bilinearity itself.
A bilinear form is best introduced as a machine with two input slots. Once a basis is chosen, every value comes from a matrix multiplication, so the abstract pairing becomes computational. The diagram emphasizes that changing either input changes the output linearly while the matrix stores all pairwise interactions of basis vectors.

Quadratic Forms Remember Only The Symmetric Part

q(v)=B(v,v)
Polarization

For a symmetric pairing, the mixed value can be recovered from the quadratic form:

B(v,w)=1/2 [q(v+w)-q(v)-q(w)].

What Disappears

A skew part contributes zero to B(v,v), so diagonal samples cannot detect it.

Contours show the symmetric quadratic profile; the dashed circulation marks information not seen by q.

Symmetry is the moment when the two slots stop having separate personalities. The symmetric part controls the quadratic expression seen along a single vector, while the skew part disappears on the diagonal. This is why quadratic data can recover a symmetric pairing but cannot remember oriented antisymmetric information.

An Inner Product Is A Matrix Of Rulers

Coordinate Formula

With a chosen basis, the inner product is represented by a symmetric positive-definite matrix G.

<x,y>G=xTGy
  • Lengths become ||x||G2=xTGx.
  • Angles come from the same pairing.
  • Orthogonality means <x,y>G=0, not visual right angles.
An inner product is a symmetric positive pairing, and in coordinates it is a positive-definite matrix G. The unit circle for this metric is usually an ellipse on the page, so ordinary visual perpendicularity can be misleading. The key move is to trust the pairing value rather than the Euclidean look of the drawing.

Metric Gram-Schmidt Builds A Frame The Metric Understands

Frame Test

Columns Q are orthonormal for the metric precisely when

QTGQ=I.

  • Projection coefficients use <.,.>G.
  • The output frame may still look slanted in ordinary coordinates.
  • Inside the frame, metric computations become ordinary dot products.
Metric Gram-Schmidt is ordinary Gram-Schmidt with every dot product replaced by the G inner product. The output vectors can look nonorthogonal in the page coordinates and still satisfy Q transpose G Q equals the identity. This is the coordinate trick that makes later metric calculations look Euclidean inside the chosen frame.

Orthonormal Coordinates Hide The Metric Matrix

Original Coordinates

<x,y>=xTGy

Cross terms encode the skew of the coordinate grid relative to the metric.

G-Orthonormal Coordinates

<a,b>=aTb

The same geometry is expressed in a frame where the metric matrix is I.

This slide isolates the coordinate change viewpoint. The original coordinates carry a non-identity metric matrix, while the G-orthonormal frame carries the identity matrix. Nothing geometric changed; only the measuring grid was rewritten so the metric no longer has visible cross terms.

Self-Adjointness Depends On The Pairing

<Av,w>G=<v,Aw>G
Matrix Test

In coordinates, the condition is

A^{T}G=GA.

Common Trap

The raw matrix A does not need to be symmetric unless the basis is orthonormal for G.

The self-adjoint condition means the operator can move from one side of the pairing to the other without changing the value. In nonorthonormal coordinates the matrix itself may not look symmetric, so the right test is A transpose G equals G A. This is the linear algebra pattern behind shape operators on surfaces.

The Spectral Theorem Supplies Orthogonal Axes

Spectral Theorem

A self-adjoint linear map on a finite-dimensional real inner product space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

The spectral theorem says that self-adjoint maps have an orthonormal eigenbasis for the relevant inner product. In a G-metric setting, the eigenvectors are scaled so E transpose G E equals the identity and the operator becomes diagonal. This is the algebraic reason principal axes are stable objects rather than coordinate artifacts.

Eigenvectors Are Extremes Of A Quadratic Profile

RA(x)=<Ax,x>G / <x,x>G
  • Restrict to the metric unit circle.
  • Maximum and minimum occur in eigendirections.
  • The extreme values are the corresponding eigenvalues.
The Rayleigh quotient gives a variational view of eigenvectors. Evaluating the quotient on the metric unit circle produces a height profile whose maximum and minimum occur along eigendirections. This picture prepares the audience for normal curvature as a quadratic profile on tangent directions.

The Surface Bridge: First Form, Second Form, Shape Operator

Geometry Translation

The first fundamental form I is the inner product on a tangent plane.

Representation

The second fundamental form II is represented by a unique I-self-adjoint map W:

II(v,w)=I(Wv,w).

This is the bridge from Appendix A0 to differential geometry. The first fundamental form supplies the inner product on a tangent plane, and the second fundamental form supplies a symmetric bilinear form. The shape operator is the self-adjoint map that represents the second form through the first.

Eigenvectors of the shape operator give principal directions; eigenvalues give principal curvatures.

Appendix A0 In Three Habits
1. Ask What Measures

The inner product is the metric matrix controlling length, angle, and orthogonality.

2. Test Symmetry With The Metric

Use ATG=GA, especially in skewed or surface-adapted coordinates.

3. Diagonalize Geometric Operators

Self-adjoint maps have orthonormal eigenframes; shape operators produce principal directions.

Fast Check

Given I and II, form W=I-1II, verify WTI=IW, and read its eigenpairs.

The final recap keeps three habits in view. First, always ask which inner product is measuring the tangent plane. Second, test symmetry with the metric, not with visual appearance in arbitrary coordinates. Third, read principal directions as the spectral theorem applied to a geometric operator.
