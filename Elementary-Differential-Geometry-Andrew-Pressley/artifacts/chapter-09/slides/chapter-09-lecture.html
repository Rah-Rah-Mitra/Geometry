Chapter 09: Geodesics

Straightest curves, shortest paths, and coordinates built from motion

Lecture Thesis

A geodesic is a curve whose acceleration has no tangential component. From that local statement we get ODEs, conserved quantities, stationary length, exponential maps, and special coordinates.

Core Definition

Geodesic Means No Tangential Acceleration

Definition

For a unit-speed curve \(\gamma(s)\) on a surface \(S\), write the ordinary acceleration as

\(\gamma'' = (\gamma'')^T + (\gamma'')^\perp.\)

The curve is a geodesic when \((\gamma'')^T=0\), equivalently \(\nabla_{\gamma'}\gamma'=0\).

  • The curve can curve in ambient space.
  • Its surface direction does not rotate.
  • Unit speed makes the statement clean; constant speed follows for geodesic solutions.
Spend time on the decomposition because it is the conceptual hinge of the chapter. The ambient acceleration vector is not required to be zero; a great circle on a sphere accelerates toward the center in three-dimensional space. What vanishes is the part of acceleration that lies in the tangent plane, so the surface sees the curve as unforced. This is the differential-geometric version of a straight line.
Coordinate Form

The Geodesic Equation Is A Second-Order System

Coordinate Equation
\(\ddot u^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}(u)\dot u^i\dot u^j=0,\qquad k=1,2.\)

The Christoffel symbols package how the coordinate basis changes along the surface.

Initial Data

Choose a starting point and a tangent velocity. Standard ODE theory then gives a unique local geodesic while the solution remains in the coordinate patch.

Present the equation as the coordinate translation of zero covariant acceleration. Students do not need to memorize every Christoffel expression at this moment; they need to understand that the symbols are determined by the first fundamental form and its derivatives. The second-order nature is important because it explains why a point and velocity determine the local curve. This also prepares the examples on the sphere, cylinder, and surfaces of revolution.
Example 1

On The Sphere, Geodesics Are Great Circles

ODE Diagnostics

The notebook solves the geodesic equation and checks three stable quantities: speed, angular momentum, and distance from the plane of the great circle.

\(\gamma(s)=\cos(s)p+\sin(s)v,\quad |p|=1,\;v\perp p.\)

A small circle has constant height, but its acceleration has a tangential surface component unless it is the equator of some plane through the center.

Use the sphere because it turns the definition into a familiar picture. A great circle is not straight in space, but its acceleration points normal to the sphere, so the tangential component vanishes. The displayed formula gives the geometric reason: the curve stays in a two-dimensional plane through the origin. Contrast this with a latitude circle, whose ambient acceleration is not purely surface-normal unless the latitude is a great circle.
Example 2

A Local Isometry Turns Lines Into Helices

Cylinder Map
\((u,z)\mapsto(\cos u,\sin u,z)\)

This map preserves the first fundamental form locally: \(du^2+dz^2\). Therefore straight lines in the unwrapped plane become geodesics on the cylinder.

  • Horizontal lines become circles around the cylinder.
  • Vertical lines become generators.
  • Slanted lines become circular helices.
This example is a good place to say that geodesics are intrinsic. The cylinder can be cut and flattened without stretching, and in the flat sheet geodesics are ordinary straight lines. When the sheet is wrapped back up, those lines appear as circles, generators, or helices depending on slope. This demonstrates why a curve that looks spatially complicated may still be locally shortest and straight on the surface.
Surfaces Of Revolution

Clairaut: Symmetry Gives A Conserved Quantity

Invariant
\(r(u)\sin\psi = \hbox{constant}\)

Here \(r(u)\) is the distance to the axis of revolution and \(\psi\) is the angle the geodesic makes with a meridian.

Turning Rule

If the conserved value is larger than the available radius, the geodesic cannot enter that band. It must turn where the equality becomes tight.

Frame Clairaut's relation as Noether's theorem in elementary clothing: rotational symmetry creates a conserved momentum. The formula says that a geodesic trades radius against angle. Near a narrow waist, a path with too much angular momentum cannot keep moving inward, so it turns around. This gives students a prediction tool before they solve any explicit differential equation.
Special Curves

Meridians Are Geodesics; Parallels Need A Test

Meridians

By symmetry, a meridian is the fixed curve of reflection in a plane through the axis, so its acceleration has no sideways tangential component.

Parallels

A parallel is geodesic only at a critical radius. Intuitively, the surface profile has no first-order push toward a wider or narrower neighboring circle.

This slide turns the Clairaut discussion into concrete curve families. Meridians are geodesics for a structural reason: the surface has a reflection symmetry that keeps the meridian fixed, and uniqueness prevents the geodesic from leaving it. Parallels are subtler because most circles around the axis want to drift toward a nearby radius. The critical-radius condition tells students exactly which parallels can be geodesics.
Variational Meaning

Shortest Curves Must Be Geodesics

First Variation

For a fixed-endpoint variation \(\gamma_t\), a length-minimizing curve has first derivative zero at \(t=0\).

\(\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0} L(\gamma_t)=0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \nabla_{\gamma'}\gamma'=0.\)

Geodesic is a necessary condition for local shortestness, not a permanent guarantee of global minimum.

This is where straightest and shortest meet. A shortest path with fixed endpoints cannot be improved by any small endpoint-fixed deformation, so the first variation of length must vanish. The Euler-Lagrange conclusion is the geodesic equation. Stress the logical direction: every sufficiently short minimizing segment is geodesic, but a geodesic may stop minimizing after it passes a cut point or conjugate behavior.
A Necessary Caution

Geodesic Does Not Always Mean Globally Shortest

Local Versus Global

Near any point, geodesics supply shortest routes for sufficiently small endpoints. Across larger distances, multiple geodesics may compete.

Cut-Locus Signal

On the sphere, all geodesics from the north pole meet again at the south pole at distance \(\pi\). Beyond that radius, uniqueness and minimizing behavior fail.

This caution prevents a common misconception. A geodesic is the critical curve for length, and locally it is the path produced by the straightest possible motion. But critical points need not be global minima. On a sphere the long arc of a great circle is still geodesic, even though the complementary arc is shorter. This motivates the exponential map and the cut-locus picture in the next part of the lecture.
Exponential Map

Initial Velocities Become Surface Points

Definition

The exponential map sends a tangent vector \(v\in T_pS\) to the point reached at time one by the geodesic with \(\gamma(0)=p\) and \(\gamma'(0)=v\).

\(\exp_p(v)=\gamma_v(1)\)

Small disks in \(T_pS\) map cleanly to neighborhoods of \(p\); large disks may fold or collapse.

Define the exponential map as a way to repackage the initial-value theorem. Instead of listing all geodesics through a point, we let each tangent vector tell us which geodesic to follow and how far. For small vectors this behaves like a coordinate chart. The sphere shows why one must be careful at larger radius: every direction of length pi from the north pole reaches the same south pole.
Coordinates From Geodesics

Geodesic Polar Coordinates Make Radial Curves Straight

Metric Shape
\(ds^2=du^2+G(u,v)\,dv^2,\qquad G_u(0,v)=0.\)

Gauss' lemma says radial and angular directions are orthogonal in geodesic coordinates.

Inspection Target

Look for distance circles crossing radial geodesics at right angles. The coordinate grid is built from motion, not imposed from outside.

Geodesic coordinates are the payoff of the exponential map. Starting from a point, the radial coordinate is literal arclength along a geodesic, and the angular coordinate records the initial direction. The metric form shown on the slide expresses Gauss' lemma: radial and angular directions are orthogonal. This is a powerful simplification because it adapts the coordinate grid to intrinsic distance.
Gauss Lemma

Distance Spheres Meet Radial Geodesics Orthogonally

Proof Idea

Vary the initial velocity while holding its length fixed. The radial derivative measures speed along the geodesic, while angular derivatives change direction. The first variation identity forces their inner product to vanish.

\(\langle \partial_u,\partial_v\rangle = 0\)

The statement is local, but it is the engine behind normal coordinates and distance-minimizing claims near a point.

Do not try to prove every analytic detail here; use the slide to explain the mechanism. The radial direction changes how far along the same initial direction we travel, while the angular direction changes the initial direction without changing the radius. The first variation formula says these two motions are orthogonal at the endpoint. This is why geodesic circles behave like ordinary circles to first order around their center.
Looking Ahead

Geodesic Coordinates Remember Curvature

What \(G(u,v)\) Measures

In \(ds^2=du^2+G\,dv^2\), the coefficient \(G\) records how neighboring radial geodesics separate as \(u\) grows.

Bridge To Later Chapters

Curvature controls this separation. Positive curvature focuses geodesics, while negative curvature spreads them apart.

This slide is a bridge, not a new theorem-heavy section. Once radial geodesics become coordinate lines, the remaining metric coefficient tells us how distance changes around the angular direction. That coefficient will later encode curvature through how rapidly nearby geodesics converge or diverge. This prepares students to see geodesics as measuring devices, not only as paths.

Instructor Prompts

Exercises For The Room

1. Sphere Check

Show that \(\gamma(s)=\cos(s)p+\sin(s)v\), with \(p\perp v\), has acceleration normal to the unit sphere.

2. Cylinder Check

Use the map \((u,z)\mapsto(\cos u,\sin u,z)\) to explain why every wrapped straight line is a geodesic.

3. Clairaut Prediction

If \(r(u)\sin\psi=c\), decide where a trajectory with fixed \(c\) can and cannot pass on a surface of revolution.

Discussion Target

For each prompt, students should identify the invariant being tested: normal acceleration, preservation of the metric, or conserved rotational momentum.

These prompts are designed to be solved from the lecture rather than from copied textbook exercises. The sphere check reinforces the distinction between ambient acceleration and tangential acceleration. The cylinder check makes intrinsic geometry visible through a local isometry. The Clairaut prediction asks students to reason qualitatively from a conserved quantity, which is often more valuable than solving a complete ODE.
Takeaways

What To Carry Forward

  • A geodesic has zero covariant acceleration: \(\nabla_{\gamma'}\gamma'=0\).
  • The coordinate equation is a second-order ODE determined by the metric.
  • Local isometries preserve geodesics, explaining helices on cylinders.
  • Symmetry produces conserved quantities such as Clairaut's relation.
  • Shortest curves are geodesic locally, but global minimizing can fail.
  • The exponential map turns tangent vectors into geodesic coordinates.
Close by returning to the chapter thesis. The entire chapter can be remembered as the unfolding of one condition: zero tangential acceleration. From that condition we get equations, examples, conserved quantities, variational consequences, and coordinates. The next natural question is how curvature influences the behavior of these geodesic families, especially how fast nearby geodesics separate or focus.
