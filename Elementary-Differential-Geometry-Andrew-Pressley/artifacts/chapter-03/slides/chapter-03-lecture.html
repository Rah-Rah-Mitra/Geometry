Simple Closed Means More Than Periodic

Definition

A regular plane curve is closed when its position and tangent data repeat after one period. It is simple when the trace has no self-intersection except the periodic endpoint identification.

Common Failure

A figure-eight can be smooth and closed, but it has two lobes with opposite winding behavior. Area and vertex statements need hypotheses that exclude this ambiguity.

Stress that closedness is a parametrization condition, while simplicity is a property of the trace. The diagrams separate the two ideas: both curves return to their starting data, but only the oval has a clean bounded component that can serve as the inside. This distinction will keep the area and vertex theorems honest later.

Orientation Gives The Interior A Sign

\(A_{\mathrm{signed}}=\frac12\int (x\,dy-y\,dx)\)
Diagnostic

Positive orientation makes the signed area positive and gives winding number \(1\) for interior probes. Reversing the same trace changes the sign, not the shape.

Use orientation as the bridge between geometry and computation. The same geometric oval can produce opposite signed integrals depending on direction, so the sign is not a nuisance; it records how the boundary is traversed. Mention that winding number gives a compatible test for interior points.

Winding Number Reads The Inside From The Boundary

Boundary-Based Test

For a point not on a simple positively oriented curve, the total change of the angle from the point to the curve is \(2\pi\) inside and \(0\) outside.

\(\displaystyle \operatorname{wind}_{\gamma}(p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta \arg(\gamma(t)-p)\)
Use In The Notebook

Sampled angle unwrapping turns this into a numerical probe before any area or convexity claim is trusted.

This is a good place to make the Jordan curve intuition operational without proving the theorem in full. The angle from an interior point must make one full turn as the boundary is traversed; an exterior point has its directions unwind back to net zero. In computations, unwrapping arctangent values is a practical version of that idea.

Green's Formula Converts Area To Boundary Data

\(\displaystyle A=\frac12\int_a^b\bigl(x(t)y'(t)-y(t)x'(t)\bigr)\,dt\)
Ellipse Check

For \(x=a\cos t,\;y=b\sin t\), the integrand is constant \(ab\), so the boundary integral returns \(\pi ab\).

The formula is the chapter's main computational engine. It says the area can be read by walking around the boundary and adding signed infinitesimal contributions, rather than filling the interior. The ellipse check is useful because students can verify the integral quickly and see why orientation controls the sign.

The Isoperimetric Question

Isoperimetric Inequality

For a simple closed plane curve of length \(L\) enclosing area \(A\),

\(L^2 \ge 4\pi A\)

Equality occurs precisely for a circle.

Roundness Quotient

\(Q=4\pi A/L^2\) makes the inequality a scale-free diagnostic: circles have \(Q=1\), distortions push \(Q\) below \(1\).

Frame the theorem as an optimization statement, not only an inequality. Among all simple closed curves with a fixed enclosed area, the circle minimizes boundary length; equivalently, among curves of fixed length, it maximizes area. The quotient Q is valuable in the notebook because it turns a theorem into a number that can be compared across families.

Roundness Deficit Measures Distance From Equality

\(\displaystyle \delta=\frac{L^2}{4\pi A}-1\)
Perturb A Circle

A radial ripple can preserve visual closure while increasing length faster than area. The deficit records that lost efficiency.

Inspection Target

Watch the boundary become wavier while the quotient bar shrinks. The formula is sensitive to global shape, not just local curvature spikes.

The deficit is a useful pedagogical reparameterization of the inequality. Instead of checking whether L^2-4pi A is nonnegative, the class reads a dimensionless penalty that vanishes exactly at the circle. The three panels also warn against judging roundness by appearance alone, because small ripples can have a noticeable length cost.

One Proof Strategy: Recenter And Decompose Modes

Proof Roadmap
  • Choose coordinates so the curve is balanced around an origin.
  • Express boundary data using periodic functions.
  • Apply a Wirtinger-type inequality to nonconstant modes.
  • Equality leaves only the circular first mode.
Keep this slide at the roadmap level; the purpose is to reveal the architecture of the proof without reproducing a textbook derivation. Students should hear that global inequalities often become statements about periodic functions after the curve is parametrized carefully. The equality case is especially important: the proof mechanism does not merely prove an inequality, it identifies why only a circle can make every residual vanish.

The Deficit Comes From Nonnegative Residuals

Analytic Shape

A Wirtinger inequality says that for a periodic function with the right average removed, derivative energy dominates function energy.

\(\displaystyle \int_0^{2\pi} f'(t)^2\,dt \ge \int_0^{2\pi} f(t)^2\,dt\)
Geometric Meaning

The circle is the case where the leftover oscillatory content is exactly the lowest mode allowed by closure and centering.

The main idea is that the proof transforms a geometric problem into a sum of quantities that cannot be negative. That is why equality is so rigid: every residual term must disappear simultaneously. If time is short, emphasize the conceptual ledger rather than the detailed algebra, because the ledger is what students will reuse in later global geometry arguments.

Vertices Are Curvature Extrema

Definition

A vertex of a regular plane curve is a point where signed curvature has a local maximum or local minimum.

Four-Vertex Theme

A smooth simple closed convex curve has at least four vertices. The theorem is global: convex closure forces curvature to rise and fall repeatedly.

Connect the word vertex to curvature rather than to polygonal corners. On a smooth oval, vertices are the points where bending is locally most or least intense. The theorem is surprising because it extracts a minimum oscillation count from qualitative assumptions: smooth, simple, closed, and convex.

The Four-Vertex Theorem Needs Convex Simplicity

Convex Oval

At least four curvature extrema are forced.

Simple But Not Convex

Indentations change how curvature signs and extrema behave.

Closed But Self-Crossing

The inside is ambiguous and theorem hypotheses fail.

This slide protects students from overgeneralizing the theorem. Convexity is not decorative; it controls the sign and geometric meaning of curvature around the whole curve. The nonconvex and self-crossing sketches show why one should verify hypotheses before turning a numerical vertex count into evidence for a theorem.

A Four-Vertex Proof Has A Sign-Change Ledger

Contradiction Shape

If curvature had too few extrema, a carefully chosen comparison function would keep a fixed sign pattern around the curve.

Global Constraint

Periodic closure and convexity make the corresponding integral ledger balance to zero, contradicting the one-sided sign pattern.

The proof of the four-vertex theorem can be presented as a bookkeeping argument about signs and integrals. If curvature changed direction too few times, the graph would have a sign structure incompatible with the global closure identities. The slide's purpose is not to hide the proof, but to identify what the proof is really using: periodicity, convexity, and a forced cancellation.

Computational Lab: Treat Assumptions As Data

Pipeline
  • Sample a periodic curve.
  • Check simplicity and winding.
  • Compute \(A\), \(L\), and \(Q\).
  • Find local extrema of sampled curvature.
Interpretation Rule

A low vertex count is not meaningful until the curve passes the simple convex checks required by the theorem.

This slide connects the lecture to the chapter notebook. The computational workflow is not just about producing measurements; it is about deciding which measurements have theorem-level meaning. Students should leave with the habit that geometry software must track assumptions such as simplicity, orientation, and convexity alongside numerical values.

Three Misconceptions To Defuse

Closed Means Simple

False. Periodic data can still trace a self-intersecting curve, and the bounded region is no longer a single clean object.

Area Is Always Positive

False for oriented boundary integrals. Reversing the traversal changes signed area while preserving the geometric region.

Four Vertices Are Visual Corners

False. Vertices are extrema of smooth curvature, often appearing where the curve bends most or least.

Good global curve arguments first ask: What hypotheses make the quantity meaningful?
These are the errors most likely to appear when students move from local curve theory to global statements. Each misconception has a corresponding test: check self-intersection, record orientation, and define vertices through curvature rather than visual sharpness. The final question is meant as a reusable habit for later chapters on surfaces as well.

Chapter 3 Takeaways

Topology First

Simple closed curves support inside-outside tests, winding number, and signed area with a stable interpretation.

Inequality Second

The isoperimetric inequality says the circle is the unique optimal shape for length versus area.

Curvature Last

Convex closed curves cannot keep curvature monotone; at least four extrema must occur.

Close by returning to the big theme: global curve theory turns a boundary into invariant information about a whole region and the way it bends. The most important formulas are not isolated tricks; they are linked by orientation, closure, and convexity assumptions. This prepares the next unit, where surfaces will require the same discipline about local measurements and global meaning.
