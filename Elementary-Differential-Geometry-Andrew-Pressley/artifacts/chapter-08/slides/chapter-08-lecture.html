Reading the local shape of a surface from the shape operator: determinant, half-trace, eigenvalues, and the global consequences they force.

This lecture turns Chapter 8 into a shape-operator story. The goal is not to memorize three unrelated curvature names, but to see determinant, trace, and eigenvalues as three views of one self-adjoint map. Keep returning students to the question: what does the normal do when we move in each tangent direction?

The Chapter Is A Linear-Algebra Machine

At one point of an oriented surface

The shape operator \(W:T_pS\to T_pS\) measures the first-order change of the unit normal.

Product
\(K=\det W\)
Average
\(H=\frac12\operatorname{tr}W\)
Spectrum
\(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\)
Computational translation: after choosing an orthonormal tangent frame, \(W\) becomes a symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix.
Start by locating every object at a single point. The determinant and trace are basis-independent, so they survive any orthonormal tangent frame we choose. The diagram emphasizes that curvature is not painted on the surface; it is read from how the normal field changes along tangent directions.

Three Invariants, One Quadratic

Curvature Data From \(W\)

If \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) are the eigenvalues of \(W\), then

\(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2,\qquad H=\frac{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}{2}\)

The principal curvatures are recovered from the characteristic equation

\(x^2-2Hx+K=0\).

What To Inspect

  • \(K\) detects whether the two principal bendings reinforce or oppose.
  • \(H\) records the signed average bending.
  • \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) give the extremal normal curvatures.
This slide gives the algebraic spine of the chapter. Emphasize that the equation \(x^2-2Hx+K=0\) is not a new theorem floating in isolation; it is the characteristic polynomial with curvature names inserted. Once students accept that translation, many later statements become controlled algebra rather than separate miracles.

Normal Curvature Samples The Operator

Euler Formula

In a principal orthonormal frame, a unit tangent direction \(u(\theta)\) has normal curvature

\(k_n(\theta)=\kappa_1\cos^2\theta+\kappa_2\sin^2\theta\).

The extrema occur on the principal axes unless \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2\).

Use this as the bridge from matrix language back to geometry. A direction in the tangent plane gives a normal section, and the curvature of that section is the quadratic form associated with \(W\). The \(\cos^2\) and \(\sin^2\) weights also explain why the principal values bound all normal curvatures.

Principal Directions Are The Extremal Slices

Non-Umbilic Point

When \(\kappa_1\ne\kappa_2\), the principal directions are two perpendicular tangent directions.

  • One maximizes normal curvature.
  • The other minimizes normal curvature.
  • Every other direction blends the two values.

Umbilic exception: if \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2\), every tangent direction has the same normal curvature, so no preferred principal axes are visible.

The visual is a measuring device, not a literal surface. The radius records the normal curvature in each tangent direction, making the eigenvector statement visible as a maximum-minimum statement. Point out the umbilic case because it is where the usual picture of two special axes disappears.

Local Shape From A Second-Order Model

In principal coordinates near a non-umbilic point, the quadratic approximation is

\(z=\frac12(\kappa_1x^2+\kappa_2y^2)\).

Elliptic

\(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) same sign, so \(K>0\).

Hyperbolic

Opposite signs, so \(K<0\).

Parabolic

One value is zero, so \(K=0\).

This slide lets students see why the product \(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2\) controls the sign classification. The model is only second order, but it captures whether the surface bends the same way, bends oppositely, or fails to bend in one principal direction. It is also a good place to separate parabolic behavior from being an actual plane.

Common Trap

\(K=0\) does not say the surface is a plane. It says the product of the two principal curvatures vanishes.

Lecture Check

Ask which row contains a cylinder. The answer is parabolic at ordinary points: one zero direction and one curved direction.

The table organizes the vocabulary without making vocabulary the point. Students should learn to infer the row from the principal curvatures rather than trying to recognize a surface by name. The cylinder question is a quick diagnostic for whether they have confused flatness with planarity.

Constant \(K\) Becomes An ODE For Revolution Models

Arc-Length Meridian

For a surface of revolution

\(\sigma(u,v)=(f(u)\cos v,\ f(u)\sin v,\ g(u))\)

with \(f'(u)^2+g'(u)^2=1\), the Gaussian curvature is

\(K=-\frac{f''(u)}{f(u)}\).
Sphere band

\(K>0\) from sinusoidal radius.

Cylinder

\(K=0\) because \(f''=0\).

Negative model

\(K<0\) when \(f''\) and \(f\) agree in sign.

Check

Constancy is a residual in the saved notebook artifacts.

This is a computationally friendly part of the chapter because a two-variable curvature question collapses to a one-variable equation. The radius function \(f\) carries the sign and constancy of \(K\) in these models. The point is not that every surface is a surface of revolution, but that these examples make constant curvature inspectable.

Flat Does Not Mean Planar

Developable Intuition

A flat surface has \(K=0\), so one principal curvature may vanish while the other remains nonzero.

  • Planes: both principal curvatures vanish.
  • Cylinders and cones: one principal direction is straight.
  • Tangent developables: ruling directions remain the flat directions.

The local theorem is about curvature, not about whether the surface looks visually flat in three-dimensional space.

The ruling lines are the inspection target on this slide. Along a ruling, the normal does not change in the way it would along the transverse bending direction, so one principal curvature is zero. This gives a concrete antidote to the tempting but false statement that zero Gaussian curvature means an open piece of a plane.

Parallel Surfaces Transform Principal Curvatures

Offset By Signed Distance \(\lambda\)

Move each point along the chosen unit normal. In a principal frame,

\(\kappa_i^\lambda=\frac{\kappa_i}{1-\lambda\kappa_i}\).

The formula is valid until a denominator vanishes.

\(K_\lambda=\frac{K}{1-2\lambda H+\lambda^2K},\qquad H_\lambda=\frac{H-\lambda K}{1-2\lambda H+\lambda^2K}\)
The denominators carry the geometry of focal behavior: the parallel surface can become singular when the offset reaches a principal radius of curvature. The formulas also show why \(K\) and \(H\) are algebraically coupled under normal offsets. This is the setup for the chapter's bridge between constant mean curvature and constant positive Gaussian curvature.

Constant \(H\) Has A Hidden Constant-\(K\) Shadow

Algebraic Bridge

If \(H=1/(2R)\) is constant, offsetting by \(\lambda=R\) sends many curvature pairs to a shared Gaussian curvature value when the offset is regular.

\(K_R=\frac{1}{R^2}\)

The singular exceptions occur where \(1-R\kappa_i=0\).

Instructor prompt: track which expression changes because of the offset and which expression becomes constant after substituting \(H=1/(2R)\).

This slide is deliberately algebraic, because the geometric content is in the substitution. Ask students to keep \(K\) variable at first, substitute \(H=1/(2R)\), and then simplify the parallel-surface expression. The regularity caveat matters: the clean formula lives away from the offset distance where the parallel surface folds or focuses.

Mean Curvature Separates Balance From Product

\(H=0\) Is A Signed Balance

Mean curvature can vanish even when the surface is strongly curved.

\(H=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \kappa_2=-\kappa_1\)

Then \(K=-\kappa_1^2\le0\), unless both principal curvatures vanish.

Chapter 8 prepares this distinction; the deeper minimal-surface theory comes later.

This is a conceptual pause before the global result. Mean curvature is an average, so it can cancel signs that Gaussian curvature multiplies. The slide also previews why minimal surfaces tend to have saddle behavior rather than bowl behavior, without leaning on the later chapter's theory.

A Compact Surface Must Have \(K>0\) Somewhere

Support-Sphere Argument

Choose a point of the compact surface farthest from the origin. The sphere centered at the origin with that radius supports the surface there.

At the support point, both normal curvatures are forced to have compatible sign, so their product is positive.

For an ellipsoid with semiaxes \(a>b>c\), the farthest point has curvature product \(a^2/(b^2c^2)>0\), consistent with the support-sphere bound.

This is the lecture's global moment. Compactness is used only to guarantee that a farthest point exists, but once it exists the argument is local at that contact point. The support sphere gives a comparison object whose curvature forces the surface curves through the contact point to bend in compatible directions.

Proof Roadmap: From Maximum Distance To Positive \(K\)

1. Maximize

The squared distance function reaches a maximum on a compact surface.

2. Touch

The centered sphere of that radius is tangent at the farthest point.

3. Compare

Every surface curve through the point lies inside the supporting sphere to second order.

4. Conclude

The two principal normal curvatures have the same sign, hence \(K>0\).

Where Compactness Enters

Only at the existence of a farthest point. Without that point, the supporting sphere argument has no place to start.

Where Curvature Enters

Only after tangency, by examining second derivatives of curves through the contact point.

This proof roadmap helps prevent students from treating the compactness theorem as mysterious. Separate the topological input from the differential-geometric input: compactness produces a maximum, while second derivatives at the contact point produce curvature information. That separation also makes the result easier to remember.

Worked Classification From A Shape Matrix

At A Point, Suppose

\(W=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\)

Then \(\operatorname{tr}W=6\), \(\det W=8\), and the eigenvalues are \(4\) and \(2\).

Read The Geometry

  • \(H=3\): the average signed bending is positive.
  • \(K=8\): the point is elliptic.
  • Principal axes are rotated \(45^\circ\) from the chosen frame.
This example is intentionally small enough to do at the board. The off-diagonal terms show that the original basis is not principal, while the eigenvalues still immediately give \(K\), \(H\), and the local type. It is a good final check that students can move from a matrix to geometric language without needing a picture of the whole surface.

Takeaways

Local Algebra

The shape operator is the organizing object. \(K\), \(H\), and principal curvatures are determinant, half-trace, and eigenvalues.

Local Geometry

The signs of the principal curvatures distinguish elliptic, hyperbolic, parabolic, and planar behavior.

Global Constraint

Compact surfaces in three-space cannot avoid positive Gaussian curvature everywhere.

Exit Question

Given only \(H\) and \(K\) at a point, solve \(x^2-2Hx+K=0\). What do the two roots tell you about the visible local model?

Close by making the algebra-to-geometry pipeline explicit one last time. The exit question forces students to reconstruct the principal curvatures from \(H\) and \(K\), then classify the point from the roots. That is the core skill the chapter is meant to leave behind.
