Isometries of Euclidean Spaces

Distance-preserving maps are rigid affine motions: an orthogonal frame change followed by one translation.

Open by framing the appendix as a structural cleanup of Euclidean motion. The learner already knows translations, rotations, and reflections geometrically; the goal here is to make their common algebraic shape visible. Emphasize that the source span is short but powerful because it turns distance preservation into the affine formula used throughout differential geometry.

Use this as the map for the session, not as a list to rush through. The lecture repeatedly returns to the same computational object, an affine map with a matrix and a vector. Students should hear that the diagrams are inspections of one theorem from several angles: distance, orientation, fixed points, and composition.

What An Isometry Preserves

Definition. A map F: R^n -> R^n is a Euclidean isometry when ||F(v)-F(w)|| = ||v-w|| for every pair of points.

The condition is global. A finite constellation should keep the same distance ledger before and after applying F.

D_{ij}=||p_i-p_j||, D'_{ij}=||F(p_i)-F(p_j)||
The definition is intentionally pairwise: no privileged origin, axis, or parameterization is mentioned. This is why a translation can be an isometry even though every coordinate changes. Point to the ledger idea and ask students what would fail first if one vertex were stretched away from the others.

Affine Normal Form

Structural theorem. Every Euclidean isometry has the form F(v)=Pv+a, where P^T P=I. Conversely, every such formula is an isometry.
  • a moves the origin to F(0).
  • P records how the coordinate frame is rigidly turned or reflected.
  • Orthogonality is the algebraic version of preserving length and angle.
State the theorem as the central fact of the appendix. The important pedagogical move is to separate the two jobs: the matrix controls the frame, and the vector controls the location. When students later meet surface isometries, this Euclidean result should feel like the flat model sitting in the background.

Translations: The Frame Is Silent

Special case. A translation has F(v)=v+a. The linear part is I, so the determinant is 1.

Every point receives the same displacement. Parallel equal arrows are the visual certificate of a pure translation.

F(v)-v=a for all v
Translations are the easiest case to underestimate, because nothing appears to rotate or reflect. Stress that the affine formula needs them: without the vector a, every isometry would be forced to fix the origin. The diagram should be read as a vector field whose uniformity rules out shear, scaling, and rotation about a point.

Orthogonal Matrices Preserve Inner Products

Algebraic certificate. If P^T P=I, then (Pv) dot (Pw)=v dot w for all vectors v,w.

Lengths come from v dot v. Angles come from the dot product formula, so one identity controls both.

||Pv-Pw|| = ||P(v-w)|| = ||v-w||
This is the algebraic hinge of the theorem. Rather than treating orthogonality as a matrix slogan, walk through the inner product computation and let length preservation fall out as a special case. The message is that an orthogonal matrix is exactly a rigid change of frame at the origin.

Determinant Sign Records Orientation

Direct versus opposite. For an orthogonal matrix, det P is either 1 or -1. The sign tells whether oriented area or volume keeps its handedness.
  • det P=1: direct isometry.
  • det P=-1: opposite isometry.
  • Translations do not affect this sign.
The determinant is a compact orientation ledger. In the plane it can be read through signed area; in three dimensions it becomes handedness of ordered triples. Make clear that direct and opposite describe orientation behavior, not whether the motion is visually dramatic or small.

Reflection In A Hyperplane

Formula. For a unit normal N and hyperplane v dot N=d, the reflection is R_H(v)=v-2(v dot N-d)N.

Points on the mirror have zero signed offset. All other points travel perpendicularly across the mirror by twice that offset.

Fixed Sets Reveal The Motion

Translation

Usually no fixed point: solving v+a=v requires a=0.

Reflection

A whole hyperplane is fixed. This visible stillness is the mirror.

Rotation

In the plane, a nontrivial rotation has one fixed center. In space, an axis may be fixed.

Fixed sets are a useful diagnostic because they translate algebra into geometry. The equation F(v)=v becomes a linear system once F(v)=Pv+a is known. Invite students to compare how much of the space remains still: none, one point, a line, a plane, or an axis depending on the motion.

Plane Rotations Are Direct Isometries

Rotation matrix. R_theta is orthogonal and has determinant 1. Its columns are the rotated coordinate basis.
R_theta = [cos theta, -sin theta; sin theta, cos theta]

Because orientation is preserved, oriented area keeps the same sign even while the frame turns.

This slide makes the columns of a rotation matrix tangible. Each column is where a basis vector lands, so orthogonality says the image basis remains perpendicular and unit length. The determinant being one adds the orientation information: the ordered basis still has the same handedness.

Composition Carries The Translation

Affine composition. If F(v)=Pv+a and G(v)=Qv+b, then G(F(v))=QPv+Qa+b.

The second linear part acts on the first translation. That is the small bookkeeping step that prevents the formula from being just matrix multiplication.

Composition is where the affine viewpoint earns its keep. Students often remember the QP part but forget that the translation a is rotated or reflected by Q before b is added. Tie this to doing one rigid motion and then measuring the next one in the moved coordinate frame.

Common Mistakes To Prevent

Forgetting the affine part

An isometry need not fix the origin. Always allow a=F(0).

Confusing det with distance

Both signs preserve distances. The sign records orientation behavior only.

Reflecting along the mirror

A reflection moves points perpendicular to the mirror, not parallel to it.

Dropping Q in composition

The translation in F becomes Qa before adding b.

Over-reading pictures

Classify with identities and residuals; diagrams guide inspection.

Treating P as arbitrary

The matrix must be orthogonal. A shear can preserve some distances but not all.

Use this slide as a checkpoint before the summary. Each mistake corresponds to a specific algebraic omission: the translation vector, determinant sign, normal direction, composition rule, or orthogonality condition. A good way to run the slide is to ask which invariant would catch each error.

Takeaways

One formula. Euclidean isometries are exactly F(v)=Pv+a with P^T P=I.
  • Distances are preserved because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products.
  • Translations move the origin but do not change orientation.
  • Reflections are opposite and fix hyperplanes.
  • Rotations are direct; in the plane, two reflections produce one.
  • Composition follows (Q,b) o (P,a) = (QP, Qa+b).
Close by returning to the formula and asking students to name what each term controls. The appendix is valuable because it compresses familiar rigid motions into a reusable algebraic language. The next time an isometry appears, the first reflex should be to look for the orthogonal matrix, the translation vector, determinant sign, and fixed-set equation.
