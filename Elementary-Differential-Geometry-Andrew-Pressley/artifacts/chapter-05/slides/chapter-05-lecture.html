Chapter 5: Examples Of Surfaces

A working catalog of regular surfaces: level sets, quadrics, ruled surfaces, surfaces of revolution, compact examples, and local graph models.

Printed pages 95-120 Notebook 05 16-slide lecture

The chapter is less a list of curiosities than a set of tests for recognizing when a recipe really gives a regular surface.

Open the lecture by framing the chapter as a catalog with a purpose: each example teaches a recognition test. The examples will look varied, but the recurring question is whether nearby points can be described by two smooth independent parameters. Emphasize that the deck uses original schematic visuals, not textbook figures, to keep attention on the ideas.

Examples Are A Diagnostic Toolkit

02

Lecture Thesis

A surface recipe is trustworthy when it supports a local two-dimensional coordinate system with a nonzero normal direction.

rank \(d\sigma=2\)   or   \(\nabla F \ne 0\)
RecognizeLevel, parametric, and graph descriptions can encode the same local geometry.
CompareSpheres, tori, cones, cylinders, and quadrics fail or succeed in different ways.
DiagnoseSingular parameters and singular points are different problems.

Working Principle

Whenever possible, translate a visual surface into a map, a gradient, or a matrix. The algebra then tells us which local picture is legitimate.

This slide sets the organizing principle for the chapter. Students often treat the examples as separate objects to memorize, so redirect them toward the tests that classify the examples. The key phrase is local two-dimensional coordinate system: global pictures can be beautiful, but regularity is checked nearby.

Level Surfaces: Normals From Gradients

03

Recognition Test

If \(S=F^{-1}(c)\) and \(\nabla F(p)\ne 0\) at each point under discussion, then the level set has a well-defined normal and behaves locally like a graph.

T_pS=\{v:\nabla F(p)\cdot v=0\}

The cone vertex is the warning sign: the equation may still be meaningful while regularity fails at a point.

Use the gradient as the first universal diagnostic. It gives the normal direction, and its nonvanishing is what lets the implicit function theorem convert an equation into a local surface. The important distinction is that a set can be visually surface-like almost everywhere while still failing regularity at a special point.

Quadrics: Matrix Signature Becomes Shape

04

Algebraic Lens

A centered quadric is controlled by a symmetric matrix. Diagonalizing the quadratic form separates scale, sign, and degeneracy.

x^T A x = 1

Positive, mixed, and zero eigenvalues correspond to ellipsoid-like, hyperboloid-like, and cylindrical or conical behavior.

This slide connects familiar pictures to linear algebra. The learner should see that classifying quadrics is not visual guesswork; it is mostly eigenvalue bookkeeping after a change of orthonormal coordinates. The cone appears again because it is the model where the algebra signals a geometric singularity.

Parametric Patches: Two Independent Directions

05

Patch Test

A map \(\sigma(u,v)\) is a regular patch when the velocity vectors \(\sigma_u\) and \(\sigma_v\) are linearly independent.

\(\sigma_u \times \sigma_v \ne 0\)

This test is local in the parameter domain; a bad coordinate choice can fail even when the surface has another good chart nearby.

Here the chapter changes from implicit equations to parametrizations. Students should identify the two coordinate curves and then inspect whether their tangent directions collapse into one line. The cross product is not introduced as a computational ritual; it is the visible normal to the patch.

The Sphere Needs More Than One View

06

Graph Patches On The Sphere

Near a point where \(z\ne 0\), the sphere can be written as a graph over the \(xy\)-plane. Near the equator this remains fine locally, but no single graph covers the whole sphere.

z=\pm\sqrt{r^2-x^2-y^2}
This slide uses the sphere to separate local success from global coverage. A graph patch is perfectly legitimate where the chosen projection behaves well, but the surface as a whole needs an atlas. This prepares the later inverse-function-theorem discussion without forcing all of that machinery at once.

Surfaces Of Revolution: One Curve Swept Around An Axis

07

Standard Form

Rotate a profile \((f(u),g(u))\) around the \(z\)-axis:

\(\sigma(u,v)=(f(u)\cos v,\ f(u)\sin v,\ g(u))\)

Regularity asks that the profile be regular and that the rotating radius does not collapse except at controlled endpoint behavior.

The surface of revolution is an efficient example because the parameters have direct meanings: one moves along the profile and the other rotates. The formula also exposes the danger at the axis, where the angular parameter may stop producing a real direction. Ask students to point to where the cross product would vanish in the picture.

Ruled Surfaces: A Moving Line Builds The Patch

08

Line-Sweep Recipe

A ruled surface is traced by a base curve and a moving direction field.

\(\sigma(u,v)=\alpha(u)+v\,\beta(u)\)

The straight rulings are easy to see; the regularity test still depends on whether the \(u\)-motion and \(v\)-motion remain independent.

Ruled surfaces are a good test of geometric intuition because they can look curved while containing a straight line through every point. The formula makes the two motions explicit: slide along the base curve, then move along the selected line. Point out that a cylinder, a cone away from its vertex, and a hyperbolic paraboloid all fit naturally into this perspective.

Cylinder Versus Cone: Same Family, Different Failure

09

Common Mistake

The cone is not disqualified because it has straight lines. It fails to be regular at the vertex because all angular directions collapse there.

\(\sigma_u \times \sigma_v=0\) at the apex

Away from the vertex, the same cone has ordinary surface patches.

This comparison is meant to prevent a common overcorrection. Students may hear that the cone has a singularity and then mentally discard the entire cone, but the failure is localized at the apex. The cylinder keeps a nonzero circular direction everywhere, while the cone loses that direction exactly where the radius becomes zero.

The Torus: Two Angles, One Compact Surface

10

Product-Of-Circles Patch

With \(R>r>0\), the standard torus is generated by rotating a small circle whose center travels around a larger circle.

\(\sigma(u,v)=((R+r\cos u)\cos v,\ (R+r\cos u)\sin v,\ r\sin u)\)

The two parameters are circular, so the rectangle model identifies opposite edges.

The torus is a compact example where the parametrization has periodic redundancy. Make clear that the rectangle used for parameters is a bookkeeping device, not a literal sheet with four boundary curves on the surface. This is a useful bridge from local differential geometry to the topological idea of gluing edges.

Compact Examples: Closed, Bounded, And Watertight

11

Sphere

Compact and simply connected in the visual sense: no handle and no boundary curve.

\(\chi=2\)

Torus

Compact with one handle. The coordinate model is periodic in two independent directions.

\(\chi=0\)

Punctured Model

Bounded is not enough. Removing a limiting point destroys closedness and changes the surface behavior.

bounded \(\not\Rightarrow\) compact
This slide treats compactness as a geometric checklist rather than a word to recite. The sphere and torus are the positive examples, while a punctured or incomplete model shows why boundedness alone is insufficient. The Euler characteristic values are included as compact memory anchors, not as a full topological computation.

Triply Orthogonal Systems: Confocal Quadrics As Coordinates

12

Coordinate Idea

A triply orthogonal system is a three-parameter family of surfaces in space whose mutual intersections meet at right angles.

Confocal quadrics provide the model: ellipsoid-like, one-sheet-like, and two-sheet-like members share focal data while supplying orthogonal coordinate surfaces.

This is the most conceptually dense example in the chapter, so keep the goal modest. Students should understand the phrase coordinate surfaces: fixing one coordinate gives a surface, and different families cross orthogonally. The confocal quadric picture is valuable because it shows a whole organized system, not just an isolated surface.

Inverse Function Theorem: Local Graph Control

13

Regular Patch Consequence

If one \(2\times2\) minor of \(d\sigma\) is invertible, then the patch can be re-expressed locally as a graph over the corresponding coordinate plane.

\((u,v)\mapsto (x(u,v),y(u,v))\) invertible near \(p\)

The same surface may choose different coordinate planes in different neighborhoods.

This slide ties the examples back to the theorem-level machinery. The inverse function theorem says that a good projection can become a local coordinate system, which is why checking a minor of the derivative matters. Stress that this is local control, not a promise that one projection works globally.

Diagnose A Surface Recipe

14

In-Class Prompt

Apply the workflow to a cone, a cylinder, and a torus. For each one, name the regular part and one coordinate choice that explains it.

This slide converts the chapter into a repeatable procedure. It is meant to be used actively: ask students to run the workflow on examples rather than merely read it. The loop at the bottom is important because a failed chart may expose either a true singularity or just a poor local coordinate choice.

Misconceptions To Remove Early

15

False Shortcut

A nice drawing does not prove regularity. The test must find two independent tangent directions or a nonzero normal.

False Objection

A parametrization can have seams or repeated values without making the underlying surface singular.

False Global Demand

One chart rarely describes a whole compact surface. An atlas is not a nuisance; it is the correct language.

False Algebra Fear

Gradients, cross products, and matrix signatures are geometric instruments: each one answers a visible question.

This slide collects the main conceptual errors that tend to survive the first pass through examples. The most important one is confusing parametrization defects with surface defects, especially for periodic examples like the torus. Use this as a quick oral check before moving to later chapters where the first fundamental form depends on reliable patches.

Recap: What Chapter 5 Adds

16
  1. Level surfaces are regular where the gradient supplies a nonzero normal.
  2. Parametric patches are regular where two coordinate velocities are independent.
  3. Quadrics, ruled surfaces, and revolution surfaces are families of examples with different diagnostic signatures.
  4. Compact surfaces require coverage by local charts, not a single global formula.

Bridge To The Next Chapter

Once a patch is regular, the surface can inherit measurements from \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Chapter 6 turns these examples into metric geometry through the first fundamental form.

ds^2 = E\,du^2 + 2F\,du\,dv + G\,dv^2

The final formula is a preview rather than a new lesson: it reminds students that regularity is the entry ticket to intrinsic measurement.
