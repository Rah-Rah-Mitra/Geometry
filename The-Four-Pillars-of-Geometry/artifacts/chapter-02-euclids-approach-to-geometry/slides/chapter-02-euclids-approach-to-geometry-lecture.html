Axiomatic foundation

Euclid’s Five Postulates

P1: Line Through Two Points

A straight line can be drawn from any point to any other point. This is the straightedge axiom.

P2: Extend a Line

A finite straight line can be extended continuously in a straight line. Lines have no endpoints.

P3: Draw a Circle

A circle can be drawn with any center and any radius. This is the compass axiom.

P4: Right Angles are Equal

All right angles are congruent to one another. This provides a universal standard of perpendicularity.

P5: The Parallel Postulate

Given a line and a point not on it, there is exactly one line through the point parallel to the given line. This axiom separates Euclidean from non-Euclidean geometry.

P1–P4 are local construction rules; P5 is a global claim about parallelism.
Visible concept: five axioms generate all of Euclidean geometry.Inspection target: which postulate each proof relies on.
Present the five postulates as the foundation. The first four are about local constructions; the fifth, the parallel postulate, is global and controversial. Emphasize that the entire structure of Euclidean geometry rests on accepting all five, and changing the fifth produces non-Euclidean geometries in Chapter 8.
Triangle congruence

SAS, ASA, SSS: When Are Two Triangles the Same?

SAS

Two sides and the included angle determine a unique triangle. If they match, the triangles are congruent.

ASA

Two angles and the included side determine a unique triangle. The third angle and remaining sides follow.

SSS

Three sides determine a unique triangle (up to reflection). If all three match, congruence is guaranteed.

\(\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF \iff\) rigid motion maps one to the other
Visible concept: congruence is tested by matching specific data.Inspection target: which combination of data suffices.
Explain congruence through the SAS, ASA, and SSS criteria. Two figures are congruent when there is a rigid motion mapping one onto the other. In practice, matching a specific combination of sides and angles suffices to guarantee a unique triangle, and the three criteria cover the cases where this guarantee holds.
Area dissection

The Pythagorean Theorem by Rearrangement

Dissection Proof

Both arrangements tile a square of side \(a+b\) with the same four right triangles. The remaining area in arrangement 1 is \(c^2\); in arrangement 2 it is \(a^2 + b^2\).

Why Dissection?

Area-based proofs avoid coordinates entirely. They show that the Pythagorean theorem follows from the postulates and congruence alone, without any algebraic machinery.

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$
Visible concept: rearranging congruent pieces reveals area equalities.Inspection target: verify the four triangles are identical in both tilings.
Show the Pythagorean theorem through area dissection. The classical proof rearranges two copies of the right triangle to fill a large square in two different ways, revealing that the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the areas of the squares on the two legs.
Circle theorems

Thales’ Theorem: Right Angles from Semicircles

Statement

If \(AB\) is a diameter of a circle and \(P\) is any point on the semicircle, then \(\angle APB = 90°\).

Proof Sketch

Draw radius \(OP\). Triangles \(OAP\) and \(OBP\) are isosceles (radii equal). Their base angles sum to \(180° - \angle AOB = 90°\).

Connection to Ch. 1

This is the same theorem used in the geometric mean construction for \(\sqrt{a}\). The right angle at \(P\) guarantees the altitude formula \(h^2 = 1 \cdot a\).

Visible concept: circles enforce right angles.Inspection target: the isosceles triangles from the center.
Introduce Thales theorem: an angle inscribed in a semicircle is always a right angle. Prove it by drawing the radius to the inscribed point and using the isosceles triangle property. This result connects circles to right angles and was already used in Chapter 1 for the square-root construction.
Generalization

The Inscribed Angle Theorem

Statement

An inscribed angle is half the central angle that subtends the same arc: \(\angle APB = \tfrac{1}{2}\angle AOB\).

Corollary

All inscribed angles subtending the same arc are equal, regardless of where the vertex lies on the circle.

Forward Link

This theorem underpins the cross-ratio invariance in projective geometry (Chapter 5): four concyclic points have a fixed cross-ratio because inscribed angles are arc-determined.

\(\angle APB = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\angle AOB\)
Visible concept: inscribed angles are controlled by arcs.Inspection target: why the factor of 2 appears.
Present the inscribed angle theorem as a generalization of Thales: the inscribed angle is always half the central angle subtending the same arc. This powerful result links arc measure to angle measure and will reappear when projective geometry uses cross-ratios in Chapter 5.
Golden ratio construction

The Regular Pentagon and the Golden Ratio

Golden Ratio

Divide a segment so the whole is to the longer part as the longer is to the shorter: \(\phi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618\).

Construction

The golden ratio is constructible because it involves only \(\sqrt{5}\). Once \(\phi\) is constructed, mark five equally-spaced points on a circle using the ratio to determine arc lengths.

Pentagon Property

In a regular pentagon, the ratio of diagonal to side equals \(\phi\). Every diagonal intersects another in the golden ratio, creating a self-similar pattern.

$$\phi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}, \qquad \phi^2 = \phi + 1$$
Visible concept: the golden ratio connects algebra to regular polygons.Inspection target: verify the self-similar diagonal intersections.
Walk through the construction of a regular pentagon. The key is the golden ratio: dividing a segment so that the whole is to the longer part as the longer part is to the shorter. Once you can construct the golden ratio, the pentagon follows by marking five equally-spaced points on a circle.

Key Takeaway

Euclid turned geometry from an empirical craft into a deductive science. Every theorem rests on the five postulates, and congruence is the engine that drives most proofs.
