Vector algebra

Addition and Scalar Multiplication

Addition

Place vectors tip-to-tail. The sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by the two vectors.

Scalar Multiplication

Scaling by \(c > 0\) stretches; by \(c < 0\) reverses direction. The result lies on the same line through the origin.

Centroids

The centroid of a triangle with vertices \(A, B, C\) is \(G = \frac{1}{3}(A+B+C)\). It is the average of the position vectors and divides each median in ratio \(2:1\).

\(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = (u_1+v_1,\; u_2+v_2), \quad c\,\mathbf{v} = (cv_1,\; cv_2)\)
Visible concept: addition is the parallelogram law.Inspection target: verify commutativity geometrically.
Present vector addition and scalar multiplication geometrically. Addition is the parallelogram diagonal; scalar multiplication stretches or compresses along the direction. Emphasize that these operations are defined without coordinates and can be verified once coordinates are chosen.
Bases and span

Linear Independence and Bases

Linear Independence

Vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\) are linearly independent if the only solution to \(c_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k \mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}\) is \(c_i = 0\) for all \(i\).

Basis

A basis for \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a maximal linearly independent set: \(n\) independent vectors. Every vector has a unique decomposition into basis components.

Standard Basis

In \(\mathbb{R}^2\): \(\mathbf{e}_1 = (1,0)\), \(\mathbf{e}_2 = (0,1)\). Coordinates are the coefficients in the standard-basis decomposition.

$$\mathbf{v} = v_1 \mathbf{e}_1 + v_2 \mathbf{e}_2 = (v_1, v_2)$$
Visible concept: independent vectors span the plane.Inspection target: uniqueness of decomposition.
Explain linear independence as the condition that no vector in a set is a combination of the others. In the plane, two linearly independent vectors form a basis: every other vector can be written uniquely as a linear combination. The standard basis gives the familiar coordinate axes.
Metric from algebra

The Euclidean Inner Product

Definition

\(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta\).

What It Measures

  • Length: \(|\mathbf{u}| = \sqrt{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}}\).
  • Angle: \(\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|}\).
  • Orthogonality: \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0 \iff \theta = 90°\).

Projection

The projection of \(\mathbf{u}\) onto \(\mathbf{v}\) is \(\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|^2}\,\mathbf{v}\).

Visible concept: the inner product encodes projection.Inspection target: verify orthogonality from the formula.
Introduce the inner product as the algebraic tool that measures angles and lengths. The formula is the sum of component products, but the geometric meaning is a projection: the inner product of u and v measures how much u points in the direction of v, scaled by their lengths.
Fundamental inequality

Cauchy–Schwarz and Triangle Inequality

Cauchy–Schwarz

\(|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}|\,|\mathbf{v}|\), with equality iff \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) are parallel. This guarantees \(|\cos\theta| \leq 1\).

Triangle Inequality

\(|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}| + |\mathbf{v}|\), with equality iff \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\) point in the same direction. The straight line is shortest.

Proof Idea

Expand \(|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|^2 = |\mathbf{u}|^2 + 2\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} + |\mathbf{v}|^2\). Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to bound the cross term.

$$|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}|\,|\mathbf{v}| \implies |\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}|+|\mathbf{v}|$$
Visible concept: the short side of a triangle bounds the sum.Inspection target: when does equality hold?
Derive the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality from the inner product. The inequality says the absolute value of the inner product is at most the product of the lengths, with equality only when the vectors are parallel. Present the triangle inequality as a consequence: the straight line is the shortest path.
Matrix and complex viewpoints

Rotations via Matrices and Complex Numbers

Rotation Matrix

Rotation by \(\theta\) counterclockwise is the linear map with matrix:$$R_\theta = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$$

Complex Multiplication

Identify \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with \(\mathbb{C}\). Rotation by \(\theta\) is multiplication by \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\). The matrix and complex forms encode the same operation.

Properties

  • \(R_\theta^T = R_{-\theta} = R_\theta^{-1}\) (orthogonal).
  • \(\det R_\theta = 1\) (orientation-preserving).
  • \(R_\alpha R_\beta = R_{\alpha+\beta}\) (angles add).
$$R_\theta\,\mathbf{v} \leftrightarrow e^{i\theta}\,z \qquad (\mathbf{v} \leftrightarrow z = v_1 + iv_2)$$
Visible concept: rotation preserves length and adds angles.Inspection target: verify orthogonality of the matrix.
Show how rotation by angle theta acts as a linear map with matrix entries involving cosine and sine. Then present the complex-number viewpoint: multiplication by the unit complex number at angle theta performs the same rotation. The two descriptions are equivalent and each has advantages.

Key Takeaway

The inner product is the central object of Euclidean vector geometry. It defines lengths, angles, orthogonality, and projections, and it makes rotation matrices meaningful.
