Foundation

The Cartesian Coordinate System

Key Idea

Every point in the plane is uniquely identified by an ordered pair \((x, y)\). This converts geometric objects into sets of number pairs.

What Changes

Constructions become calculations. Proofs become algebraic identities. Verification becomes computation.

\(\text{point} \leftrightarrow (x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2\)
Visible concept: geometry becomes number pairs.Inspection target: locate a point by its projections.
Introduce the Cartesian coordinate system: a number line for each axis, an ordered pair for each point. Emphasize that this single idea turns every geometric question into an algebraic question and every algebraic identity into a geometric theorem. The coordinate plane is the bridge between Chapters 1-2 and Chapters 3-4.
Lines in coordinates

Line Equations: Slope, Intercept, and General Form

Slope-Intercept: \(y = mx + b\)

Slope \(m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\) measures steepness. Intercept \(b\) is where the line crosses the \(y\)-axis. Cannot represent vertical lines.

General Form: \(ax + by + c = 0\)

Handles all lines including verticals. The normal vector \((a, b)\) is perpendicular to the line.

Parallel & Perpendicular

Lines are parallel when \(m_1 = m_2\). Lines are perpendicular when \(m_1 m_2 = -1\) (negative reciprocal slopes).

$$m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1 \iff \text{perpendicular}$$
Visible concept: slope encodes direction algebraically.Inspection target: verify perpendicularity from slope product.
Present line equations in slope-intercept and general form. Slope measures rise over run and encodes the angle a line makes with the horizontal. Lines are parallel when slopes are equal, perpendicular when slopes are negative reciprocals. General form handles vertical lines that slope-intercept cannot.
Metric geometry

Distance and Midpoint from Coordinates

Distance Formula

From the Pythagorean theorem: \(d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\).

Midpoint Formula

The midpoint is the average: \(M = \left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\).

Circle Equation

A circle of radius \(r\) centered at \((h,k)\) is the set of points at distance \(r\): \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\).

$$d(P,Q) = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2}$$
Visible concept: Pythagoras becomes a coordinate formula.Inspection target: the right triangle with legs parallel to axes.
Derive the distance formula from the Pythagorean theorem applied to a right triangle with legs parallel to the axes. Then show that the midpoint is the average of coordinates. These two formulas convert every metric question into coordinate arithmetic.
Algebraic intersection

Where Lines Meet Circles

Method

Substitute \(y = mx + b\) into \((x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\). Expand to get a quadratic in \(x\). Solve with the quadratic formula.

Discriminant Test

  • \(\Delta > 0\): two intersection points (secant).
  • \(\Delta = 0\): one point (tangent).
  • \(\Delta < 0\): no intersection (miss).

Coordinate vs. Compass

This is the algebraic version of the compass-and-straightedge intersection from Chapter 1. The quadratic captures exactly which constructions are possible.

$$\Delta = b'^2 - 4ac \quad\begin{cases} >0 & \text{secant} \\ =0 & \text{tangent} \\ <0 & \text{miss}\end{cases}$$
Visible concept: the discriminant counts intersections.Inspection target: verify each case geometrically.
Show how to find where a line meets a circle by substituting the line equation into the circle equation and solving the resulting quadratic. The discriminant tells the count: two intersections, one tangency, or no meeting. This is the coordinate version of compass-and-straightedge intersection.
Rigid motions

Every Isometry Is One of Four Types

Translation

\((x,y) \mapsto (x+a, y+b)\). No fixed points. Preserves orientation.

Rotation

\((x,y) \mapsto\) rotation by \(\theta\) about center \(C\). One fixed point. Preserves orientation.

Reflection

Mirror across a line. Fixes the mirror line pointwise. Reverses orientation.

Glide Reflection

Reflection followed by translation along the mirror line. No fixed points. Reverses orientation.

Classification Rule

  • Orientation-preserving + no fixed point = translation.
  • Orientation-preserving + fixed point = rotation.
  • Orientation-reversing + fixed line = reflection.
  • Orientation-reversing + no fixed point = glide reflection.

Matrix Form

Every isometry is \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\) where \(A\) is orthogonal: \(A^T A = I\), \(\det A = \pm 1\). The sign of the determinant separates orientation-preserving from reversing.

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}, \quad A^T A = I$$
Visible concept: isometries are fully classified by fixed points and orientation.Inspection target: determine the type from the matrix.
Introduce the isometry classification theorem: every distance-preserving map of the plane is a translation, rotation, reflection, or glide reflection. Coordinates make this classification possible because each isometry has a specific matrix-plus-translation form that can be tested directly.
Factorization theorem

The Three-Reflections Theorem

Theorem

Every isometry of the Euclidean plane can be expressed as a composition of at most three reflections.

Why Three Suffices

Reflections are the atoms of symmetry. Two reflections compose to a translation or rotation; three compose to a glide reflection. No isometry needs more.

\(\text{isometry} = R_1 \circ R_2 \circ R_3\) (at most)
Visible concept: reflections generate all rigid motions.Inspection target: count reflections for each isometry type.
Present the three-reflections theorem: every isometry can be written as a composition of at most three reflections. Translations and rotations use two reflections; glide reflections use three. This factorization shows that reflections are the atoms of Euclidean symmetry.
