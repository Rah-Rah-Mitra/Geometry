Klein’s program

The Transformation Group Hierarchy

Euclidean (Isometries)

Preserve distance, angle, area. Maps: \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\) with \(A^T A = I\). Four types: translation, rotation, reflection, glide reflection.

Affine

Preserve parallelism, ratios, and centroids. Maps: \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\) with \(\det A \neq 0\). Distances and angles change, but midpoints survive.

Projective

Preserve incidence and cross-ratio only. Maps: homogeneous \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto M\mathbf{x}\) with \(\det M \neq 0\). Parallel lines can become intersecting.

What Each Level Loses

LevelLostKept
Euclidean → AffineDistance, angleParallelism, ratios
Affine → ProjectiveParallelism, ratiosIncidence, cross-ratio

Erlangen Principle

A geometric property is meaningful for a geometry if and only if it is invariant under the corresponding transformation group. The group defines the geometry.

Visible concept: larger groups preserve fewer properties.Inspection target: what invariant distinguishes each level.
Present the hierarchy of transformation groups. Isometries preserve distance and are the transformations of Euclidean geometry. Affine maps preserve parallelism and ratios but not distance. Projective maps preserve only incidence and cross-ratio. Each larger group preserves less structure but reveals more general geometric properties.

Spherical Geometry: Great Circles and Angle Excess

Geodesics

The shortest path between two points on a sphere is a great circle arc. Great circles always intersect, so there are no parallel lines.

Angle Excess

The angle sum of a spherical triangle exceeds \(180°\) by an amount equal to the area: \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma = \pi + \text{Area}(\Delta)\).

Symmetry Group

The symmetry group of the sphere is \(O(3)\). Rotations form \(SO(3)\), which is the orientation-preserving subgroup.

Visible concept: curvature creates angle excess.Inspection target: verify that larger triangles have more excess.
Introduce spherical geometry as the geometry of the unit sphere with its intrinsic metric. Great circles are the geodesics and they always intersect, so there are no parallel lines. The sum of angles of a spherical triangle exceeds 180 degrees by an amount equal to the area of the triangle.
Rotation algebra

Quaternions: Smooth Rotations in 3D

Definition

\(q = a + bi + cj + dk\) with \(i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1\). Quaternion multiplication is associative but not commutative.

Rotation Action

A unit quaternion \(q\) (with \(|q|=1\)) rotates a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) by conjugation: \(\mathbf{v}' = q\,\mathbf{v}\,\bar{q}\). The axis is \((b,c,d)/|(b,c,d)|\) and the angle is \(2\arccos(a)\).

Advantages

  • No gimbal lock (smooth parametrization).
  • Composition = quaternion multiplication.
  • Interpolation (SLERP) for smooth animation.
$$q\,\mathbf{v}\,\bar{q} \quad\text{rotates } \mathbf{v} \text{ by angle } 2\arccos(a)$$
Visible concept: conjugation by a unit quaternion is a rotation.Inspection target: verify that the length is preserved.
Present quaternions as a four-dimensional extension of complex numbers used to represent rotations in three dimensions. A unit quaternion q acts on a vector v by conjugation: v maps to qvq inverse. This avoids gimbal lock and provides a smooth parametrization of SO(3).
Symmetry classification

Finite Rotation Groups of Three-Space

Cyclic: \(C_n\)

Rotations by multiples of \(360°/n\) about a single axis. Order \(n\). Examples: bilateral symmetry (\(C_2\)), propeller blades (\(C_3\)).

Dihedral: \(D_n\)

Rotations of a regular \(n\)-gon, including \(n\) rotational symmetries and \(n\) half-turn axes through edge midpoints. Order \(2n\).

Platonic: \(T, O, I\)

Rotation groups of the Platonic solids. Tetrahedral (order 12), octahedral/cubic (order 24), icosahedral/dodecahedral (order 60).

Classification Theorem

Every finite subgroup of \(SO(3)\) is isomorphic to one of: \(C_n\), \(D_n\), \(T\), \(O\), or \(I\). There are no other possibilities.

Euler’s Formula

For a convex polyhedron with rotation group \(G\): \(V - E + F = 2\). The orbit-counting lemma connects the number of fixed points to the group order, proving the classification.

$$\text{Finite } \leq SO(3): \quad C_n,\; D_n,\; T_{12},\; O_{24},\; I_{60}$$
Visible concept: five families exhaust finite 3D rotation symmetry.Inspection target: match each Platonic solid to its group.
Classify the finite rotation groups of three-space: cyclic groups from rotations about an axis, dihedral groups from rotations of a regular polygon, and the three exceptional groups from the Platonic solids, namely the tetrahedral, octahedral, and icosahedral groups.
Topology

\(SO(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3\): Rotations and Projective Space

Unit Quaternions = \(S^3\)

The set of unit quaternions \(\{q : |q|=1\}\) is the three-sphere \(S^3 \subset \mathbb{R}^4\).

Double Cover

Both \(q\) and \(-q\) produce the same rotation: \(q\mathbf{v}\bar{q} = (-q)\mathbf{v}(-\bar{q})\). So \(SO(3) = S^3 / \{q \sim -q\}\).

Result

The quotient \(S^3 / \{\pm 1\}\) is the real projective space \(\mathbb{RP}^3\). The rotation group has the same topology as three-dimensional projective space.

$$SO(3) \cong S^3 / \{\pm 1\} = \mathbb{RP}^3$$
Visible concept: the quaternion double cover identifies antipodal points.Inspection target: verify that q and -q give the same rotation.
Present the three-sphere S3 as the space of unit quaternions and show that the rotation group SO(3) is the quotient S3 divided by the identification q equals minus q. This identification gives the real projective space RP3, connecting topology, algebra, and geometry.
