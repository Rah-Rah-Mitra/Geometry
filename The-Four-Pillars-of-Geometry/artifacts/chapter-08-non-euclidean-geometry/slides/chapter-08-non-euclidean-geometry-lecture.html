Model I

The Upper Half-Plane Model

Space

Points: \(\{z \in \mathbb{C} : \text{Im}(z) > 0\}\). The boundary \(\text{Im}(z) = 0\) is at infinity.

Metric

\(ds = \frac{|dz|}{\text{Im}(z)}\). Near the real axis, distances are enormous; high up, distances are small.

Geodesics

Semicircles centered on the real axis and vertical half-lines. These are the “straight lines” of hyperbolic geometry.

\(ds^2 = \frac{dx^2 + dy^2}{y^2}\)
Visible concept: the metric inflates near the boundary.Inspection target: verify that semicircles are perpendicular to the axis.
Present the upper half-plane model. The points are the upper half of the complex plane, and the metric is the Euclidean metric scaled by one over the imaginary part. This scaling makes distances near the real axis enormous and distances high up small, creating the hyperbolic effect.
Model II

The Poincaré Disk Model

Space

Points: the open unit disk \(\{z : |z| < 1\}\). The boundary circle represents infinity.

Geodesics

Circular arcs perpendicular to the boundary circle, plus diameters. Every pair of points determines a unique geodesic.

Parallels

Through a point not on a geodesic, infinitely many geodesics miss the given one. This is the failure of the parallel postulate.

\(ds = \frac{2\,|dz|}{1-|z|^2}\)
Visible concept: geodesics curve to meet the boundary at right angles.Inspection target: count the parallels through a point.
Present the Poincare disk model as the conformal map of the upper half-plane to the unit disk. Geodesics become circular arcs perpendicular to the boundary circle and diameters. The boundary circle represents infinity and is not part of the hyperbolic plane.
Isometry group

Möbius Transformations as Hyperbolic Isometries

Definition

In the upper half-plane: \(z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}\) with \(a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}\), \(ad-bc > 0\).

Properties

  • Preserve the hyperbolic metric \(ds = |dz|/y\).
  • Map geodesics to geodesics.
  • Preserve angles (conformal).
  • Form the group \(PSL(2,\mathbb{R})\).

Types

  • Hyperbolic: translate along a geodesic (two fixed points on boundary).
  • Elliptic: rotate about an interior point (one fixed point inside).
  • Parabolic: limit rotation (one fixed point on boundary).
$$z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}, \quad ad-bc > 0$$
Visible concept: the trace classifies the isometry type.Inspection target: match each type to its fixed-point behavior.
Introduce Mobius transformations as the isometry group of the hyperbolic plane. In the upper half-plane model, these are maps of the form z maps to az plus b over cz plus d with real coefficients and positive determinant. They preserve the hyperbolic metric and map geodesics to geodesics.

The Hyperbolic Distance Formula

Upper Half-Plane

For \(z_1, z_2\) with the same real part (vertical geodesic):$$d(z_1, z_2) = \left|\ln\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right|$$For general points, the geodesic is a semicircle and the integral yields:

General Formula

$$\cosh d(z_1,z_2) = 1 + \frac{|z_1 - z_2|^2}{2\,\text{Im}(z_1)\,\text{Im}(z_2)}$$

Disk Model

$$d(w_1,w_2) = 2\,\text{artanh}\frac{|w_1-w_2|}{|1-\bar{w}_1 w_2|}$$

$$\cosh\,d(z_1,z_2) = 1 + \frac{|z_1-z_2|^2}{2y_1 y_2}$$
Visible concept: exponential crowding near the boundary.Inspection target: compute distances for vertical pairs.
Derive the hyperbolic distance formula. In the upper half-plane, the distance between two points is the length of the unique geodesic connecting them, computed by integrating the metric along the semicircular arc. Angle preservation

Conformality: Correct Angles, Distorted Distances

What Conformal Means

The angle between two curves at a point is the same in the Euclidean and hyperbolic metrics. Both models are conformal maps from the hyperbolic plane to the Euclidean plane.

Why It Matters

Conformality lets us visually verify angle relationships in the model. Perpendicularity, angle bisection, and tangent angles all appear correctly.

What Changes

Distances and areas are distorted: objects near the boundary appear small in the Euclidean picture but are actually hyperbolicly large.

Three-Reflections Theorem

Every hyperbolic isometry factors into at most three reflections across geodesics, paralleling the Euclidean result from Chapter 3.

  • Hyperbolic translation: 2 reflections across ultra-parallel geodesics.
  • Rotation: 2 reflections across intersecting geodesics.
  • Limit rotation: 2 reflections across asymptotic geodesics.
\(\text{hyperbolic isometry} = R_1 \circ R_2 \circ R_3\) (at most)
Visible concept: angles look right; distances do not.Inspection target: perpendicular geodesics appear perpendicular.
Explain conformality: both models preserve angles. A Euclidean angle between two curves at a point equals the hyperbolic angle. This is why the models look correct locally even though distances are wildly distorted. Present the three-reflections theorem for hyperbolic isometries.
Unification

Three Curvature Regimes: Spherical, Euclidean, Hyperbolic

Spherical (K > 0)

  • No parallel lines.
  • Angle sum > \(\pi\).
  • Finite area.
  • Isometry: \(O(3)\).

Euclidean (K = 0)

  • Unique parallel.
  • Angle sum = \(\pi\).
  • Infinite area.
  • Isometry: \(E(2)\).

Hyperbolic (K < 0)

  • Infinitely many parallels.
  • Angle sum < \(\pi\).
  • Infinite area (exponential growth).
  • Isometry: \(PSL(2,\mathbb{R})\).

Angle-Sum Formula

For a triangle of area \(A\):$$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = \pi + K \cdot A$$Positive curvature gives excess; negative curvature gives deficit.

Parallel Postulate

The number of parallels determines the curvature, and the curvature determines the geometry. The parallel postulate is genuinely independent of the other axioms.

$$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = \pi + K \cdot \text{Area}(\Delta)$$
Visible concept: curvature controls angle sum and parallel count.Inspection target: verify the formula for each regime.

Spherical geometry has positive curvature and angle excess; Euclidean geometry has zero curvature and angle sum exactly pi; hyperbolic geometry has negative curvature and angle deficit. The parallel postulate determines curvature, and curvature determines the geometry.
