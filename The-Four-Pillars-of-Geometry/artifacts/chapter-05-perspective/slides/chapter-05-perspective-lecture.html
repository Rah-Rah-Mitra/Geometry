Four Ideas of Projective Perspective

1. Vanishing PointsParallel lines converge on the picture plane at a point determined by their direction.
2. Projective PlaneThe ordinary plane plus a line at infinity where parallels meet.
3. Homogeneous CoordinatesTriples up to scaling give an algebraic handle on points at infinity.
4. Cross-RatioThe projective invariant that replaces Euclidean distance.

Learning Targets

  • Explain vanishing points from the geometry of projection.
  • Describe the projective plane and its line at infinity.
  • Convert between Cartesian and homogeneous coordinates.
  • Compute cross-ratios and verify their projective invariance.

Why Projective?

In projective geometry, there are no parallel lines and no distances. What remains is incidence (points on lines) and the cross-ratio. This simplification reveals structure hidden by Euclidean metric assumptions.

Vanishing Points and the Horizon

Rule

Parallel lines in 3D project to lines through a common vanishing point. Different directions yield different vanishing points.

Horizon Line

All vanishing points of horizontal lines lie on the horizon. The horizon is the image of the line at infinity.

Straightedge Only

Perspective constructions use only a straightedge: no compass needed. Incidence and collinearity are the only tools.

Visible concept: direction determines vanishing point.Inspection target: trace parallel families to their convergence.
Explain vanishing points through the mechanics of perspective projection. When a family of parallel lines is projected onto a picture plane, their images converge at a single vanishing point. Different families of parallels have different vanishing points, and all vanishing points lie on the horizon line.
Completing the plane

The Projective Plane: No More Parallel Lines

Construction

Add one “point at infinity” for each family of parallel lines. All these ideal points form the “line at infinity.”

Incidence Axiom

In the projective plane, any two distinct points determine a unique line, and any two distinct lines meet in exactly one point. No exceptions.

Duality

Points and lines play symmetric roles. Every theorem about points lying on lines has a dual theorem about lines passing through points.

$$\mathbb{RP}^2 = \mathbb{R}^2 \cup \ell_\infty$$
Visible concept: ideal points complete incidence.Inspection target: every pair of lines now meets.
Present the projective plane as the Euclidean plane augmented by a line at infinity. Each family of parallel lines shares a single point at infinity, and these ideal points form the line at infinity. In this model, every two distinct lines meet in exactly one point, eliminating the special case of parallel lines.
Algebraic framework

Homogeneous Coordinates

Definition

A point in \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) is an equivalence class \([x:y:z]\) where \((x,y,z) \sim (\lambda x, \lambda y, \lambda z)\) for \(\lambda \neq 0\).

Ordinary Points

When \(z \neq 0\), divide through: \([x:y:z] = [x/z : y/z : 1]\). The Cartesian coordinates are \((x/z,\, y/z)\).

Points at Infinity

When \(z = 0\): \([x:y:0]\) represents the direction \((x,y)\). These are the ideal points on the line at infinity.

Lines

A line is \(ax + by + cz = 0\). The line at infinity is \(z = 0\). Duality: lines also have homogeneous coordinates \([a:b:c]\).

$$[x:y:z] \sim [\lambda x:\lambda y:\lambda z] \quad \text{for } \lambda \neq 0$$
Visible concept: projective points are lines through the origin.Inspection target: what happens when z=0.
Introduce homogeneous coordinates: a point in the projective plane is represented by a triple of numbers up to scaling. The point at infinity in direction m is represented as the equivalence class of vectors with zero third coordinate. This algebraic framework makes projection a linear operation.
Projective maps

Projections and Linear Fractional Transformations

Central Projection

Project from line \(\ell_1\) to line \(\ell_2\) through a center \(O\). Each point on \(\ell_1\) maps to where the line through \(O\) and that point meets \(\ell_2\).

Linear Fractional Map

In coordinates, the projection is: \(x' = \frac{ax+b}{cx+d}\) with \(ad-bc \neq 0\). These maps form the group \(\text{PGL}(2)\).

What Is Preserved?

  • Cross-ratio: YES
  • Distance: NO
  • Midpoints: NO
  • Angles: NO
$$x' = \frac{ax+b}{cx+d}, \quad ad - bc \neq 0$$
Visible concept: projection changes spacing but preserves cross-ratio.Inspection target: verify unequal spacing on the target line.
Present projections and linear fractional maps. A projection from one line to another through a center is described by a linear fractional transformation. These maps preserve the cross-ratio but generally change distances, midpoints, and angles. They are the natural transformations of projective geometry.
Projective invariant

The Cross-Ratio: What Projection Cannot Change

Definition

For four collinear points \(A, B, C, D\):$$\text{cr}(A,B;C,D) = \frac{AC \cdot BD}{BC \cdot AD}$$where distances are signed.

Invariance

If a projection maps \(A,B,C,D\) to \(A',B',C',D'\), then \(\text{cr}(A,B;C,D) = \text{cr}(A',B',C',D')\). Distances change; cross-ratio does not.

Significance

The cross-ratio is to projective geometry what distance is to Euclidean geometry: the fundamental measurement. Every projective property can be expressed through cross-ratios.

Visible concept: cross-ratio survives projection.Inspection target: compute before and after projection.
Define the cross-ratio of four collinear points and show that it is invariant under projection. The formula involves ratios of signed distances between the four points. Since projections change distances but preserve cross-ratios, this is the fundamental measurement tool in projective geometry.

Key Takeaway

Projective geometry removes the special status of parallel lines and replaces distance with the cross-ratio. What survives projection is incidence and cross-ratio; everything else is an artifact of a chosen coordinate system.
