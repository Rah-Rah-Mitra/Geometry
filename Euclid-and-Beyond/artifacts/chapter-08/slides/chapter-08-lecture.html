Roadmap2 / 16

Definitions

The Incidence Skeleton

Convex Polyhedron

A solid boundary assembled from finitely many polygonal faces, with every segment joining two boundary points staying inside the solid.

  • V counts vertices, where faces meet.
  • E counts edges, shared by two faces.
  • F counts faces, each a polygon.
  • The one-skeleton is the graph made from vertices and edges.
Incidence data3 / 16
Start with the combinatorial skeleton because most of the chapter's formulas are counting statements before they are metric statements. A convex polyhedron has a graph on the sphere, but the graph alone does not remember edge lengths or angles. The key pedagogical move is to separate incidence from metric shape and then reconnect them later.
Global Count

Euler's Formula Is A Topological Budget

V - E + F = 2
For Convex Polyhedra

The alternating count stays fixed under subdivision, projection to the sphere, and planar drawing with one face opened outward.

The formula is not about regularity. It is about the surface being a sphere.

Euler characteristic4 / 16
Present Euler's formula as an invariant with a budget interpretation. Subdividing a face by adding an edge and a vertex changes V and E together, so the alternating sum stays put. This prepares students for why the formula constrains possible regular solids before any measurement is used.
Piecewise Flat Curvature

Curvature Is Stored At Vertices

Angle Defect

At a vertex of a convex polyhedron, add the face angles meeting there. The defect is the positive amount by which that sum falls short of a full turn.

sum of all defects = 720 degrees
Reading The Formula

Convex polyhedra place all Gaussian curvature into finitely many point charges.

Defect budget5 / 16
Move from counting to geometry by introducing angle defect. The surface is flat inside each face and along unfolded edges, so curvature can only appear at vertices. The total defect of 720 degrees is the angular version of Euler characteristic for a sphere, and it will later explain why only a few regular vertex patterns are possible.

Why Only Five?

Regularity requires identical regular polygon faces and the same number of faces at each vertex. The incident angles must total less than 360 degrees, leaving only the five listed patterns.

Common Misread

Euler's formula alone does not classify the solids. It must be paired with face regularity and a strict angle inequality at every vertex.

Platonic solids6 / 16
This slide is the chapter's most recognizable classification result. Keep the emphasis on the mechanism, not memorization of the five names. The face angle inequality supplies the short list of possible vertex patterns, while Euler's formula checks the resulting incidence counts and dual relationships.
Regularity Test

The Inequality At One Vertex

q · angle of a regular p-gon < 360 degrees
Vertex Symbol

A pattern p^q means that q regular p-gons meet at every vertex.

  • Triangles allow 3, 4, or 5 around a vertex.
  • Squares allow exactly 3 around a vertex.
  • Pentagons allow exactly 3 around a vertex.
  • Hexagons already tile the plane, so they leave no positive defect.
Local classification7 / 16
Derive the five Platonic cases from a single local inequality. A regular p-gon has a fixed interior angle, and q copies must leave room for positive curvature at the vertex. Students should notice that the plane tiling cases are exactly the boundary where the defect becomes zero.
Duality

Swap Faces And Vertices

Dual Construction

Place a new vertex in each face, connect two new vertices when their old faces shared an edge, and obtain a new polyhedron.

V* = F,   E* = E,   F* = V

Euler's formula is self-dual because the alternating count is unchanged by this swap.

Dual polyhedra8 / 16
Duality is a structural shortcut through the classification. Once the cube is known, the octahedron arrives by exchanging face centers with vertices, and the same happens for the dodecahedron and icosahedron. Point out that the tetrahedron is self-dual, which is a useful test that students understand the construction.
Rigidity

Cauchy's Theorem: Faces Lock The Shape

Cauchy Rigidity

If two convex polyhedra have congruent corresponding faces arranged with the same adjacency, then the polyhedra are congruent as solids.

Interpretation

Convexity turns local face congruence into global metric rigidity. The boundary cannot flex like a hinged paper model while staying convex.

Cauchy rigidity9 / 16
State Cauchy's theorem as a rigidity result rather than as a technical proof exercise. The proof strategy compares corresponding dihedral angles and assigns signs according to whether they open or close. A combinatorial sign-change argument then contradicts the possibility of a nontrivial flex for a convex polyhedron.
Proof Architecture

How The Sign Argument Works

1

Compare Edges

Each edge receives a sign from the change in its dihedral angle: larger, smaller, or unchanged.

2

Walk Around Vertices

A vertex lemma forces a limited number of alternations among the incident edge signs.

3

Count Globally

Euler's formula prevents enough alternating sign patterns from appearing everywhere on the sphere.

local sign restrictions + Euler characteristic => no flex
Rigidity mechanism10 / 16
This slide is deliberately a proof roadmap, not a full proof. Students should see the surprising reuse of Euler's formula inside a metric rigidity theorem. The local lemma limits sign alternation around a vertex, and the global graph count shows that a nonzero sign pattern cannot be distributed over the whole spherical graph.
Beyond Platonic

Semiregular Solids Relax Face Type

Semiregular Idea

Keep regular polygonal faces and the same cyclic face pattern at every vertex, but allow more than one kind of regular polygon.

  • Uniform local pattern replaces one-face-type regularity.
  • The angle sum must still be less than 360 degrees.
  • Euler's formula still filters possible incidence data.
Semiregular patterns11 / 16
Semiregular polyhedra show how much of the Platonic argument survives after a controlled relaxation. The vertex pattern remains uniform, but the faces can come from several regular polygons. This is a good place to ask students which part of the five-solid proof used identical faces and which part used only the angle defect condition.
Construction

Truncation Turns Vertices Into Faces

Truncation

Slice off every vertex by a plane near that vertex. Old faces lose their corners, and each old vertex becomes a new face.

vertex data becomes face data

The notebook artifacts for this chapter use truncation as a computational way to test semiregular incidence patterns.

Truncation12 / 16
Truncation is a bridge from the regular solids to richer families. It also illustrates dual thinking: information formerly concentrated at vertices becomes visible as new polygonal faces. When teaching this, emphasize that a small uniform cut changes the combinatorics predictably while preserving a high degree of symmetry.

Symmetry groups13 / 16
The symmetry section connects polyhedra to group theory without requiring a long algebra detour. A rotation is understood by what it fixes: a vertex pair, a face pair, or an edge pair. Counting rotations by axes and orders gives the familiar group sizes and explains why dual solids have the same rotation group.
Counting Symmetries

Axes Give A Clean Rotation Inventory

Counting Pattern

For a regular solid, nontrivial rotations are grouped by axes through opposite vertices, opposite faces, or opposite edges.

  • An axis of order n contributes n - 1 nonidentity rotations.
  • Axes are counted geometrically, not by named coordinates.
  • Add the identity rotation at the end.

Board Exercise

Test A Proposed Vertex Pattern

Prompt

A semiregular candidate has vertex pattern triangle, square, pentagon, square. Decide whether the local angle test permits convexity, then state what Euler data you would need next.

  • Compute the four incident regular polygon angles.
  • Compare their sum with 360 degrees.
  • If there is positive defect, ask whether global incidence can close on a sphere.
Exercise15 / 16
This exercise asks students to use the chapter's main filter rather than recall a table. The local angle sum determines whether convexity is even possible at one vertex. If it passes, the next question is global: can the required numbers of faces, edges, and vertices satisfy the incidence equations and Euler's formula?
