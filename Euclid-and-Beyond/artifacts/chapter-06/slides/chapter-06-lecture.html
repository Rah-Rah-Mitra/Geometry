Straightedge and compass

The field ledger

Invariant

[K : Q] = 2m

Any coordinate constructible from rational input lies in a finite tower whose nontrivial steps are quadratic.

  • Line-line intersection: field arithmetic.
  • Line-circle or circle-circle intersection: one square-root gate.
  • Odd irreducible degree blocks compass construction.
Stress that the ledger is a bookkeeping device for coordinates, not a recipe for drawing every line. Starting from rational data, line-line intersections use field arithmetic and circle intersections add at most a square root. The important invariant is multiplicative: a tower built from quadratic gates has total degree a power of two.
Classical failures

Three requests, three algebraic signals

Double the cube

x3 = 2

The required edge ratio has irreducible degree 3.

Trisect an angle

8c3 - 6c - 1 = 0

A simple angle can force an irreducible cubic.

Square the circle

side2 = pi

The obstruction uses transcendence, not merely odd degree.

Present the famous problems as three different algebraic failure modes. Doubling the cube and trisecting a general angle already fail because a cubic degree appears where only powers of two are allowed. Squaring the circle is even farther away because the needed relation involves pi, and the obstruction is transcendence rather than just an odd algebraic degree.
A cubic appears

Angle trisection as an equation

Identity

cos(3 theta) = 4 cos3(theta) - 3 cos(theta)

For trisecting 60 degrees, the doubled coordinate satisfies

u3 - 3u - 1 = 0.

The irreducible cubic is the obstruction signal.

Walk through the cosine identity as a bridge between a geometric request and an algebraic test. If a 60 degree angle were trisected by straightedge and compass, then the associated cosine value would live in a quadratic tower. The cubic relation is a compact way to see the obstruction without drawing a complicated failed construction.
Necessary filter

Quadratic towers reject odd prime degrees

Compass-only degree ledger
  • Every new coordinate is algebraic over the previous field by degree at most 2.
  • The total degree of a successful coordinate divides a power of 2.
  • An irreducible cubic cannot be a subfield of such a tower.
The tree visual should be read from left to right: each compass-level choice splits a field by degree at most two. A number may have a minimal polynomial of degree four or eight and still pass this first filter, but degree three cannot pass. Mention that the filter is necessary in this general form, and in several chapter examples it becomes decisive once irreducibility is checked.
Regular polygons

Vertices become roots of unity

Translation

A regular n-gon asks for the real parts of n-th roots of unity.

zetan = cos(2 pi/n) + i sin(2 pi/n)

The degree signal is controlled by phi(n), then reduced to the real subfield when n is odd and greater than 2.

Shift from impossibility to classification by regular polygons. The vertices are roots of unity, so the algebraic question is controlled by the cyclotomic field rather than by a single ad hoc diagram. For straightedge and compass, the relevant group size must have only the prime two in its degree signal.
A positive surprise

Why the 17-gon passes the compass test

Degree signal

deg(2 cos(2 pi/17)) = 8 = 23

The orbit of nonzero residues modulo 17 has 16 elements; pairing conjugates gives the real degree 8. Repeated halving organizes the construction.

For the regular 17-gon, the real coordinate 2 cos(2 pi over 17) has degree eight, which is 2 cubed. That degree alone does not draw the polygon, but it opens the door to a tower of quadratic choices. The nested period boxes in the visual show how a complicated root-of-unity orbit can be repeatedly split in half.
Misconception check

A power-of-two degree is a filter, not the whole proof

Warning

For arbitrary algebraic numbers, minimal degree alone can hide the behavior of the full splitting field.

Regular polygons are friendlier because the cyclotomic groups are abelian and their degree signals are explicit.

Pause here to prevent a common misconception. Degree equal to a power of two is a strong signal, but arbitrary algebraic numbers require more than the minimal degree alone; the behavior of conjugates and the splitting field can still matter. The slide's warning prepares students for the later quartic example whose root field and splitting field tell different stories.
Changing the tool changes the field

Marked ruler: a new algebraic gate

Neusis move

A marked segment of fixed length is slid until two incidence constraints hold simultaneously.

  • Compass-only: quadratic gates.
  • Marked ruler: conchoid-style constraints.
  • Algebraic effect: cubic and quartic gates enter.
Introduce the marked ruler as a controlled enlargement of the construction language. A neusis step slides a fixed marked distance until two incidence conditions are met, and that motion hides a conchoid-type curve. The key contrast is that the added move can encode cubic and quartic behavior that compass-only constructions cannot reach.
Hidden curve

The conchoid behind a neusis step

Elimination idea

The sliding mark introduces a distance equation and two incidence equations.

After eliminating the sliding parameter, the remaining intersection can be quartic.

neusis constraint -> quartic gate

Use the diagram as a moving-constraint picture rather than a static finished construction. The marked segment forces a fixed distance while one endpoint lies on a line and the other endpoint targets a ray or second line. Eliminating the sliding parameter produces a quartic intersection problem, which is exactly the algebraic door the chapter wants to open.
Cubic gate

Cubic equations enter the geometry

New capability

Once angle trisection or an equivalent neusis operation is allowed, a real cubic can be attacked geometrically.

x3 + ax + b = 0

Connect the earlier trisection obstruction to the new marked-ruler power. The same cubic shape that blocked straightedge and compass can be attacked once angle trisection or an equivalent neusis move is admitted. This is not magic; it is a controlled addition of a new algebraic operation to the field ledger.
Quartic route

Quartics via a cubic resolvent

Dependency pattern

A quartic is organized by a cubic resolvent, then finished by quadratic choices.

This places quartic solving inside the marked-ruler toolkit: cubic gate plus square-root gates.

The quartic route should be read as a dependency graph rather than as a formula to memorize. Solving a quartic can be organized by reducing part of the problem to a cubic resolvent and then finishing with square-root choices. This fits the chapter's theme: construction rules are best understood by the field operations they authorize.
Finite extensions

Root field and splitting field are different ledgers

Diagnostic

A single chosen root may generate a smaller field than the field containing all roots.

Construction permissions can depend on the symmetry required to split the polynomial completely.

This slide distinguishes two degrees that students often merge. A single root can generate a degree-four extension while the full collection of roots may live in a larger splitting field. For construction questions, that larger symmetry can reveal obstructions or permissions that the minimal polynomial degree by itself might conceal.

Polygon filters by prime content

Filter

compass: prime content only 2

For regular polygons this is not just a heuristic; cyclotomic degrees make the test exact.

With a marked ruler, powers of two and three are allowed in this simplified polygon filter, giving examples such as the 9-gon that were not compass-only.

Straightedge and compass build quadratic towers; the 17-gon succeeds because its real cyclotomic data descends through such a tower; marked rulers add cubic and quartic gates.
