The Appendix Has Three Jobs

pages 281-283

1. Make volume intrinsic

An oriented top-degree form supplies the signed density needed to integrate functions without choosing coordinates as the primary object.

2. Define divergence invariantly

The identity \((\operatorname{div}X)\omega=L_X\omega\) measures how the vector field changes the volume element.

3. Recover the trace formula

When a torsion-free connection preserves volume, divergence becomes \(\operatorname{trace}(V\mapsto\nabla_VX)\).

Use the roadmap to separate the three jobs: define volume so integration is meaningful, define divergence through the Lie derivative, and connect that analytic divergence to covariant derivatives. Tell students that the source span is not a new theory of curvature, but a compact lemma library for curvature identities used elsewhere.

Orientation Turns an n-Form Into Volume

definition layer

Definition

On an oriented \(n\)-manifold, a volume element is a top form \(\omega\) that evaluates positively on each positive coordinate frame.

The notation \(dv\) is an intuitive name for this chosen \(\omega\).

Stress that an orientation chooses which ordered bases count as positive. A volume element is then an n-form that returns a positive number on every positive coordinate frame. The point is not coordinates themselves, but the ability to assign a consistent signed density across overlapping charts.

Integration Needs Either Compactness or Support Control

setup layer

Compact manifold

If \(M\) is compact, every smooth vector field has nowhere to escape. The global integral of a top-degree density is finite once a volume element is fixed.

Closed setting

\(\int_M f\,dv\) is a global signed volume integral.

Noncompact manifold

If \(M\) is noncompact, the same formal identity remains local when the vector field has compact support.

Explain the compact support condition before stating the compact case. If the manifold is noncompact, an integral can still be local when the function or vector field vanishes outside a controlled region. The slide prepares students for the source remark that compactness can be traded for compact support.

Divergence Is the Lie Derivative of Volume

invariant definition

Definition

\[(\operatorname{div}X)\omega=L_X\omega\]

This definition uses a vector field \(X\), a fixed volume element \(\omega\), and the Lie derivative along \(X\).

Present divergence as the rate at which the volume form changes along the vector field flow. This definition avoids choosing a metric first. In Euclidean coordinates it becomes the familiar sum of partial derivatives, but here the invariant object is the Lie derivative identity.

The Proof Differentiates an Invariant Integral

flow argument

1

Let \(\varphi_t\) be the local flow generated by \(X\).

2

The integral of a pulled-back volume form is constant under change of variables.

3

Differentiate at \(t=0\) to obtain a Lie derivative term.

4

Use \((\operatorname{div}X)\omega=L_X\omega\) to identify the integral.

Proof skeleton

\[ 0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\int_M\varphi_t^{-1*}\omega =-\int_M L_X\omega =-\int_M\operatorname{div}X\,dv. \]

Walk through the proof as a differentiation under a symmetry of integration. The flow pulls the volume form back, and the integral of the pulled form over the manifold is constant. Differentiating at zero produces the Lie derivative term, so the integrated divergence must vanish.

Stokes Gives the Same Identity in One Line

alternate proof

Cartan formula

\[ L_X\omega=d(\iota_X\omega)+\iota_X(d\omega). \]

For a top form, \(d\omega=0\), so \(L_X\omega=d(\iota_X\omega)\). Stokes then sends the integral to the boundary.

Use Cartan's formula as the alternate one-line proof. Since an n-form on an n-manifold is automatically closed, the Lie derivative becomes an exact n-form. Stokes then turns its integral into a boundary integral, which is zero when the compact manifold has no boundary.

Green's Theorem: The Form to Remember

main theorem

Theorem

\[ \int_M \operatorname{div}X\,dv=0 \]

For an oriented compact manifold with fixed volume element \(dv\), every smooth vector field \(X\) has zero total divergence.

Orientation

Chooses the sign of volume and makes the integral coherent.

Compactness

Prevents mass from escaping through infinity.

No boundary

Removes the flux term in the Stokes interpretation.

Fixed volume

Defines what divergence is measuring.

State the theorem in the form students should remember. The assumptions are doing real work: orientation supplies signed volume, compactness removes escape to infinity, and absence of boundary removes flux terms. The conclusion is a global cancellation, not a claim that divergence is pointwise zero.

Compact Support Replaces Compact Manifold

remark 1

Closed compact case

  • The whole manifold is the integration domain.
  • Every smooth vector field is admissible.
  • No infinity term can appear.

Noncompact support case

  • The active region is contained in a compact subset.
  • The vector field vanishes near infinity.
  • The same cancellation is local to the support.
Compare the compact and compact-support cases. On a closed compact manifold, every smooth vector field is allowed. On a noncompact manifold, the same cancellation needs the field to die off outside a compact set. This explains why boundary and infinity are treated as possible sources of flux.

A Parallel Volume Connection Computes Divergence by Trace

proposition

Proposition

If \(\nabla\) has no torsion and \(\nabla\omega=0\), then

\[ \operatorname{div}X=\operatorname{trace}(V\mapsto\nabla_VX). \]

The coordinate-free definition becomes the trace of a linear endomorphism of \(T_xM\).

Introduce the connection proposition as the computational version of divergence. Once a torsion-free connection preserves the volume form, the abstract Lie derivative definition equals the trace of the endomorphism V maps to nabla_V X. This is the formula one actually uses in tensor calculations.

The Tensor \(A_X\) Explains the Sign

proof detail

A

Define \(A_X=L_X-\nabla_X\) as a derivation acting on tensors.

B

Parallel volume gives \(L_X\omega=A_X\omega\).

C

The action on a top form gives \(\operatorname{div}X=-\operatorname{trace}A_X\).

D

If torsion is zero, \(A_XY=-\nabla_YX\), so the two minus signs cancel.

The sign in the proof can feel slippery, so make the bridge explicit. The tensor A_X measures the difference between Lie differentiation and covariant differentiation. Its trace gives minus divergence, and torsion-free symmetry changes A_X Y into negative nabla_Y X, restoring the familiar positive trace formula.

The Metric Supplies a Natural Volume Form

Riemannian case

Coordinate formula

\[ dv=\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n. \]

The definition by oriented orthonormal frames is independent of which compatible frame is chosen.

Explain the Riemannian specialization. A metric gives preferred unit frames, and an oriented unit frame defines volume by sending that frame to one. In coordinates this becomes the square root of the determinant of the metric matrix times the ordered coordinate wedge.

Levi-Civita Parallel Transport Preserves \(dv\)

metric compatibility

Reason

A metric connection preserves inner products. Along a continuous path, an oriented orthonormal frame remains an oriented orthonormal frame.

Therefore \(\nabla dv=0\) for the Riemannian volume element.

Connect the previous slide to the Levi-Civita connection. Parallel transport for a metric connection preserves inner products, so orthonormal frames remain orthonormal; the orientation is preserved along continuous transport. Therefore the Riemannian volume form is parallel, and Green's theorem applies directly to Riemannian divergence.

A Local Chart Recovers the Familiar Formula

worked example

Euclidean chart

For \(X=P\partial_x+Q\partial_y\) and \(dv=dx\wedge dy\),

\[ \operatorname{div}X=\partial_xP+\partial_yQ. \]

Positive and negative local rates can cancel globally.

Use the local chart example to calm notation. For a planar field with components P and Q, the invariant definition reduces to partial_x P plus partial_y Q. A compactly supported field can expand in one region and compress elsewhere, yet the total signed expansion integrates to zero.

Why Curvature Arguments Want This Appendix

integration scaffold

Global proof move

Curvature identities often produce

\[ \int_M \operatorname{div}Y\,dv+\int_M Q\,dv=0. \]

Green's theorem removes the first integral, so signs of \(Q\) can force rigidity.

Tie the appendix back to curvature identities. Many global Riemannian arguments create a divergence term while differentiating tensors such as Killing fields or curvature contractions. Green's theorem discards the integrated divergence, leaving sign-controlled terms that can force rigidity on compact manifolds.

Four Ways the Identity Can Fail

hypothesis audit

Boundary

Stokes contributes a flux term unless the boundary contribution vanishes.

No orientation

A globally signed volume element may not exist on the manifold itself.

No support control

On noncompact manifolds, divergence can leak through infinity.

Wrong connection

The trace formula needs preserved volume and torsion-free conversion.

Treat this as a checklist slide. The common mistakes are forgetting a boundary term, using a volume form without orientation, ignoring support at infinity, or applying the trace formula with a connection that has torsion or does not preserve the volume. Each mistake breaks a specific arrow in the proof.

Instructor Lab: Perturb One Hypothesis

class activity

Experiment

Start from a compact oriented surface with a chosen volume form. Change exactly one assumption, then predict the new integral identity.

  • Add a boundary and identify the flux term.
  • Move to a noncompact chart and remove compact support.
  • Reverse the orientation and track the sign convention.
  • Use a connection that does not preserve volume.
Frame the lab as a way to test hypotheses, not as busy computation. Ask students to change exactly one assumption at a time: add a boundary, remove compact support, reverse orientation, or perturb the connection. Their job is to predict which formula survives and which integral gains an extra term.

Three Takeaways for Later Chapters

summary

Volume

An oriented volume element is the density against which all later integral identities are measured.

\(\omega=dv\)

Divergence

Divergence records the Lie derivative of that density along a vector field.

\((\operatorname{div}X)\omega=L_X\omega\)

Trace

A compatible torsion-free connection converts the invariant definition into a trace.

\(\operatorname{div}X=\operatorname{tr}\nabla X\)

Memory line

Green's theorem turns an integrated divergence into a vanished global term, making sign arguments possible.

Close by returning to the three durable ideas. Divergence is a Lie derivative of volume, compact oriented integration kills exact top-degree changes, and a compatible torsion-free connection converts the invariant definition into a trace. Those ideas are the scaffold for later curvature and rigidity proofs.
