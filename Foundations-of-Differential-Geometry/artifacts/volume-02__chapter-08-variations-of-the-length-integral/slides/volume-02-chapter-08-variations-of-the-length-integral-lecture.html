Variation Fields Are Linear Data

Families of curves

Definition

A variation \(c(t,s)\) produces a field \(X(t)=\partial_s c(t,0)\) along the base curve.

For the length problem, \(X\) is the tangent vector to path space at the curve.
For a geodesic variation, \(X\) is not arbitrary: it satisfies the Jacobi equation.
Endpoint conditions decide whether boundary terms survive in first and second variation.
Start with the operational meaning of a variation field. Students should see that the chapter is not varying abstract formulas; it is differentiating an actual family of curves. The field X records the velocity with which each point on the original curve moves as the family parameter changes.

The Jacobi Equation

Linearized geodesic equation

Jacobi Field

Along a geodesic \(\gamma\) with \(T=\dot{\gamma}\), a field \(J\) satisfies

\[\nabla_T\nabla_TJ+R(J,T)T=0.\]

Why This Equation Appears

  • Differentiate the geodesic equation through a geodesic variation.
  • Curvature measures the failure of the two differentiations to commute.
  • Initial data \(J(a)\) and \(\nabla_TJ(a)\) determine the whole field.
Present the Jacobi equation as the linearization of geodesic motion, not as a mysterious second order equation. The same initial value logic as ordinary differential equations remains, but the coefficient term is curvature along the geodesic, so the equation is already geometric.

Conjugate Points And The Exponential Map

Rank loss is visible

Equivalence

A point \(\gamma(b)\) is conjugate to \(\gamma(a)\) when a nonzero Jacobi field vanishes at both endpoints.

Exponential View

Conjugacy occurs when \(d(\exp_x)\) is singular at the tangent vector reaching the point.

The first conjugate point is the earliest place where the exponential map stops being locally full rank along a ray.

Emphasize the two interchangeable diagnostics. Along the geodesic, conjugacy is detected by a Jacobi field with two zeros. In the tangent space, it is detected by the differential of the exponential map losing rank, which explains why conjugate points are where geodesic coordinates begin to fold.

Riemannian Jacobi Fields Split Cleanly

Tangential versus normal

Riemannian Simplification

Metric compatibility and skew curvature imply the tangential component is affine in arc length.

Consequences

  • Normal Jacobi fields carry the curvature information.
  • If a Jacobi field is perpendicular at two points, it stays perpendicular.
  • Wronskian pairings of Jacobi fields are constant along the geodesic.
Separate the tangential bookkeeping from the normal geometry. The tangential part largely reflects reparametrization, while the normal part records how nearby geodesics actually spread or refocus. This split also prepares students for the index form, where normal variations are the essential variations for endpoint problems.

Index Integral Before Morse Theory

A quadratic diagnostic

Energy Balance

\(I(X,X)=\int_a^b\{\|\nabla_TX\|^2-\langle R(T,X)T,X\rangle\}\,dt\)

Read The Two Terms

  • \(\|\nabla_TX\|^2\): bending cost of the variation field.
  • \(\langle R(T,X)T,X\rangle\): curvature contribution along the geodesic.
  • Boundary terms vanish for fixed endpoints.
Introduce the index integral before naming the full Morse theorem. It is the second variation written as a symmetric bilinear form. The sign competition matters: derivative energy makes variations expensive, while positive curvature can make transverse variations reduce length and eventually signal conjugate points.

Curvature Bounds Control Focusing

Rauch comparison

Positive Curvature

Lower bounds \(K\ge k>0\) force conjugate points within a spherical scale.

Flat Model

Euclidean comparison fields grow linearly and supply a neutral reference.

Nonpositive Curvature

If sectional curvature is nonpositive, the index form blocks conjugate points.

Comparison Principle

Compare Jacobi field lengths under curvature inequalities; growth or focusing in a model controls growth or focusing in the target.

Explain comparison as a disciplined replacement for drawing intuition. Higher positive curvature bends geodesic families inward faster, while nonpositive curvature prevents the collapse needed for conjugate points. The chapter uses the index integral to prove these statements and then reuses them in global geometry.

First Variation: Geodesics Are Critical

Length functional

First Variation

For fixed endpoints, \(dL_\gamma(X)=0\) for every variation field \(X\) exactly when \(\gamma\) is geodesic.

Teaching Point

The theorem converts a global-looking shortest path question into a local critical point condition. It does not yet prove minimum behavior.

Critical is weaker than minimizing. The second variation is needed to detect stability.
Pause on the distinction between critical and minimizing. First variation explains why geodesics are the only candidates for local shortest paths under fixed endpoints. It does not decide whether the candidate is stable, because a saddle point also has zero first derivative.

Second Variation And Boundary Terms

The Hessian of length

Fixed Endpoints

At a geodesic, the Hessian is represented by the index form \(I(X,Y)\) on vector fields vanishing at the endpoints.

When Endpoints Move

Boundary terms record endpoint velocity. The chapter tracks them carefully before specializing to fixed endpoints and broken variations.

Integration by parts moves derivatives off one field and exposes the Jacobi operator.
The second variation slide should feel like a Hessian calculation with geometric coefficients. For fixed endpoints, boundary terms disappear and the index form becomes the central object. When endpoints move or the field is only piecewise smooth, the same formula explains the correction terms.

Jacobi Fields Are Equality Cases

Null directions of the index form

Variational Characterization

A field \(J\) is Jacobi exactly when \(I(J,Y)=0\) for all admissible \(Y\), subject to the matching conditions.

Interpretation

  • The Jacobi equation is the Euler-Lagrange equation of the index form.
  • Conjugate points create nontrivial null directions.
  • Null directions mark failure of strict local minimality.
Make the equality statement memorable. Jacobi fields are not only solutions of a differential equation; they are precisely the directions where the second variation fails to see positive curvature of the length functional. This is the bridge from local ODE analysis to Morse index counting.

Conjugacy Destroys Minimizing Beyond It

Stability threshold

Minimizing Test

If a conjugate point lies strictly between the endpoints of a geodesic segment, that segment is not minimizing between its endpoints.

Myers-Type Payoff

Positive sectional or Ricci lower bounds force conjugate points on long geodesics, bounding diameter and yielding compactness consequences.

Connect the technical conjugate-point machinery to a concrete geometric consequence. A geodesic can remain critical past a conjugate point, but it can no longer be globally shortest on the longer segment. This is how curvature lower bounds become diameter bounds and compactness theorems.

Morse Index Counts Conjugate Points

Negative directions

Index Theorem

For a geodesic segment, the index of the fixed-endpoint index form equals the total multiplicity of conjugate points in the open interval.

What Is Being Counted

  • Index: number of independent directions with negative second variation.
  • Nullity: dimension of Jacobi fields vanishing at both endpoints.
  • Multiplicity: dimension of the corresponding conjugate-point kernel.
Treat the Morse index theorem as the accounting system for the chapter. The index form starts positive near the initial point. Each conjugate point is a crossing where new negative directions appear, and multiplicity records how many independent Jacobi fields vanish there.

Cut Locus: Where Minimizers Stop

Frontier of uniqueness

Cut Point

Along a unit geodesic from \(x\), the cut point is the first point after which that geodesic stops minimizing distance from \(x\).

Two Mechanisms

  • A first conjugate point appears.
  • Another minimizing geodesic reaches the same point.
The cut locus packages minimization failure into a geometric object. At a cut point, either the geodesic has reached its first conjugate obstruction or a second minimizing geodesic has arrived. This gives a useful frontier between normal coordinates and the global topology of the manifold.

The Exponential Cell And Examples

Cut function \(\mu\)

Normal Domain

The open cell \(E=\{tX:0\le t<\mu(X),\|X\|=1\}\) maps diffeomorphically to the complement of the cut locus.

1Sphere: antipodal point is the visible cut locus.
2Projective space: antipodal identification cuts earlier.
3Flat torus: lattice choices create a polygonal cut boundary.
4Compactness: bounded cut radius covers the manifold.
Use the tangent-space picture to make the cut function concrete. Before the boundary, the exponential map gives honest normal coordinates. On the boundary, either rank failure or competing minimizers appear. The examples show that the cut locus is sensitive to topology, not only to curvature.

Nonpositive Curvature Removes Conjugacy

Cartan-Hadamard direction

Global Consequence

If \(M\) is complete with \(K\le 0\), then \(\exp_x:T_xM\to M\) is a covering map. If \(M\) is simply connected, it is a diffeomorphism.

Proof Logic

  • Nonpositive curvature gives no conjugate points.
  • Rauch comparison shows exponential distances do not shrink.
  • A distance-increasing local map from a complete space becomes a covering.
Now let the earlier machinery pay off globally. In nonpositive curvature, Jacobi fields do not refocus, so the exponential map has no conjugate-point singularities. Comparison then supplies distance control, and completeness upgrades the local statement into a covering result.

Center Of Gravity And Fixed Points

Average an orbit

Barycenter Functional

For a compactly supported measure, minimize \(J(x)=\int d(x,f(a))^2\,d\mu(a)\).

Fixed Point Payoff

A compact isometry group fixes the unique center of gravity of any orbit.

The proof reduces uniqueness to the tangent space through the global exponential chart, then uses invariance of the distance functional.
The center-of-gravity construction is a pleasant surprise after the index theorem. Nonpositive curvature gives enough convexity to average points without linear structure. For a compact isometry group, average one orbit and use uniqueness to show the barycenter cannot move.

Closing Checklist

How to read a variational theorem

1Identify the curve family and its variation field.
2Decide whether endpoints are fixed, free, or broken.
3Write the Jacobi equation and boundary conditions.
4Translate curvature assumptions into index-form signs.
5Locate conjugate points or prove they cannot occur.
6Move from local stability to cut locus or global topology.
Chapter Moral

The geometry of distance is encoded in the second derivative of length along geodesic variations.

End with a reusable protocol. For every theorem in this chapter, ask what is being varied, which endpoint conditions are imposed, what the Jacobi equation says, and how the sign of the index form changes. That habit turns the chapter from a list of results into one coherent variational machine.
