Homotopy of Paths

Path homotopy

Deform one path to another fixing endpoints.

Equivalence

It partitions paths into classes.

Homotopy

$H:[0,1]^2\to X,\ H(s,0)=f,\ H(s,1)=g$

Concatenation

Printed pp. 317-371Homotopy of Paths
Two paths with the same endpoints are path homotopic when one deforms continuously into the other keeping the endpoints fixed. Path homotopy is an equivalence relation, and concatenation of paths descends to a well-defined operation on classes. These classes are the elements from which the fundamental group is assembled.

Definition of the Group

Elements

Homotopy classes of loops at a base point.

Operation

Concatenation, with reversal as inverse.

Notation

$\pi_1(X,x_0)$

Invariance

Base point is irrelevant when path connected.

Printed pp. 317-371Definition of the Group
Fixing a base point, the homotopy classes of loops at that point form a group under concatenation, with the constant loop as identity and the reversed loop as inverse. This is the fundamental group. For path connected spaces the choice of base point does not matter up to isomorphism, so it is an invariant of the space.

Covering Spaces

Definition

Evenly covered neighborhoods of disjoint sheets.

Lifting

Covering map

$p:\widetilde{X}\to X,\ p^{-1}(U)=\bigsqcup V_\alpha$

Strategy

Turn group questions into combinatorics of sheets.

Printed pp. 317-371Covering Spaces
A covering space maps onto a base space so that each point has an evenly covered neighborhood, a disjoint union of sheets each mapped homeomorphically. Paths and homotopies lift uniquely once a starting point is chosen. Covering spaces translate questions about the fundamental group into combinatorics of sheets and lifting.

The Fundamental Group of the Circle

Covering

The real line covers the circle.

Winding

Lifting a loop counts how often it winds.

Result

$\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$

Significance

Printed pp. 317-371The Fundamental Group of the Circle
Using the real line as a covering space of the circle, lifting a loop records how many times it winds around, an integer. This shows the fundamental group of the circle is the integers under addition. It is the first nontrivial computation and the engine behind the chapter's applications.
