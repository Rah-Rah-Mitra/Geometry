The claim

Depth

Printed pp. 228-240The Tychonoff Theorem
Open the chapter, which spans printed pages 228 through 240. The Tychonoff theorem asserts that an arbitrary product of compact spaces is compact in the product topology. It is one of the most important and surprising theorems of topology, equivalent to the axiom of choice, with deep consequences across analysis and logic.

Printed pp. 228-240Statement
The theorem states that the product of any family of compact topological spaces, indexed by an arbitrary set, is compact. The subtlety lies in the infinite case, where the product topology uses only finitely many constraints. This restraint is exactly what makes compactness survive into arbitrary products.

Finite Products

Tube lemma

Cover a slice, thicken it to a tube.

Induction

Two factors, then any finite number.

Tube

$\{x\}\times Y \subseteq W \Rightarrow U\times Y \subseteq W$

Difficulty

Only the infinite case is hard.

Printed pp. 228-240Finite Products
For finitely many factors, compactness of the product follows from the tube lemma applied repeatedly. Given an open cover, one covers each slice by finitely many basic open sets and assembles a finite subcover. The genuine difficulty appears only when infinitely many factors must be handled simultaneously.

Printed pp. 228-240Arbitrary Products
The general proof uses the finite intersection property: a space is compact when every collection of closed sets with the finite intersection property has nonempty intersection. One extends a family to a maximal one using Zorn's lemma, then projects to find a point lying in the closure of every set.

Choice and Equivalence

Equivalence

Tychonoff is equivalent to the axiom of choice.

Why

A point of the product picks one coordinate per factor.

Tools

Zorn's lemma or the well-ordering theorem.

Foundations

It sits precisely at the level of choice.

Printed pp. 228-240Choice and Equivalence
The Tychonoff theorem is logically equivalent to the axiom of choice; choosing a point of the product is choosing one coordinate from each factor. This is why the proof invokes Zorn's lemma or the well-ordering theorem. The theorem thus sits exactly at the level of choice in the foundations of mathematics.
