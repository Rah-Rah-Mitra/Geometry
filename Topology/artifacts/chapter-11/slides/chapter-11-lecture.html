Open the chapter, which spans printed pages 403 through 442. The Seifert-van Kampen theorem computes the fundamental group of a space from those of two open pieces and their overlap. To state it we first build the algebra of free products and free groups, then deduce the fundamental groups of wedges of circles and of surfaces.

Printed pp. 403-442Free Products
The free product of groups consists of reduced words alternating letters from the factors, multiplied by concatenation and reduction. It is the coproduct in the category of groups, the most efficient way to combine groups with no relations imposed between them. This algebraic construction is the target of the theorem's amalgamation.

Geometry

Printed pp. 403-442Free Groups
A free group on a set of generators consists of reduced words in those generators and their inverses, with no relations beyond cancellation. Every group is a quotient of a free group, giving presentations by generators and relations. Free groups are the fundamental groups of wedges of circles, the simplest nontrivial examples.

The Theorem

Hypotheses

Conclusion

Free product amalgamated over the intersection.

Amalgamation

$\pi_1(X) \cong \pi_1(U) *_{\pi_1(U\cap V)} \pi_1(V)$

Relations

Printed pp. 403-442The Theorem
If a space is the union of two open path connected sets with path connected intersection, all containing the base point, then its fundamental group is the free product of the two pieces' groups amalgamated over the intersection's group. The relations identify loops that the intersection sees in both pieces. This is the central computational tool.

Printed pp. 403-442Wedges of Circles
Applying the theorem to a wedge of circles shows its fundamental group is free on one generator per circle. More generally, attaching a two-cell along a loop adds that loop as a relation. This gives a recipe for reading a presentation of the fundamental group directly off a cell structure.

Printed pp. 403-442Fundamental Groups of Surfaces
Representing a surface as a polygon with edges identified, the theorem yields a presentation whose single relation is the edge word. The torus gives a commuting pair of generators, while higher-genus and nonorientable surfaces give the standard surface relations. These computations set up the classification of surfaces.
