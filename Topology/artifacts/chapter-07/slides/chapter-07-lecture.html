Complete Metric Spaces

Definition

Every Cauchy sequence converges in the space.

Examples

The reals are complete; the rationals are not.

Cauchy

$\forall \varepsilon>0\ \exists N:\ m,n>N \Rightarrow d(x_m,x_n)<\varepsilon$

Use

Contraction mapping and existence theorems.

Printed pp. 261-291Complete Metric Spaces
A metric space is complete when every Cauchy sequence converges within it. The real line is complete while the rationals are not. Completeness is not a topological invariant, since homeomorphic spaces may differ in it, but it is exactly the condition under which the contraction mapping principle and many existence theorems apply.

Completion

Theorem

Every metric space sits densely in a complete one.

Construction

Equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences.

Completion

$X \hookrightarrow \widehat{X}$, dense and complete

Examples

Reals from rationals; function spaces.

Printed pp. 261-291Completion
Every metric space embeds isometrically as a dense subset of a complete metric space, its completion, which is unique up to isometry. The construction takes equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences. This is how the reals arise from the rationals and how abstract function spaces acquire the completeness analysis requires.

The Arzela-Ascoli Theorem

Statement

Characterizes compact families of continuous functions.

Conditions

Pointwise bounded and equicontinuous.

Equicontinuity

$\forall \varepsilon\ \exists \delta:\ d(x,y)<\delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon\ \forall f$

Use

Existence proofs for differential equations.

Printed pp. 261-291The Arzela-Ascoli Theorem
The Arzela-Ascoli theorem characterizes compact subsets of the space of continuous functions on a compact domain: a family is relatively compact in the uniform topology exactly when it is pointwise bounded and equicontinuous. This is the central compactness criterion of analysis, underlying existence proofs for differential equations.

Printed pp. 261-291Topologies on Function Spaces
On the set of functions between spaces we compare topologies: pointwise convergence, uniform convergence, and the compact-open topology that interpolates between them. The compact-open topology makes evaluation continuous and is the natural one for mapping spaces, central to homotopy theory and the study of function spaces.

Printed pp. 261-291Modes of Convergence
Pointwise limits of continuous functions need not be continuous, but uniform limits always are. Uniform convergence on compact sets gives the local control that preserves continuity on locally compact domains. Distinguishing these modes clarifies when limits behave well.
