We open the chapter by asking how to estimate an unknown density when the data live on a curved space such as the Stiefel or Grassmann manifold. The kernel estimator generalizes the Euclidean picture by replacing line bumps with rotationally symmetric bumps placed at each observed frame.

Data on Special Manifolds

Stiefel

$V_{k,p}$ collects orthonormal $p\times k$ frames.

Grassmann

$G_{k,p}$ collects $k$-dimensional subspaces.

Symmetry

Both carry an invariant action of $O(p)$.

Orthonormality

$X \in V_{k,p}: \quad X^{\top}X = I_k$

Invariant volume

The uniform measure is preserved by left rotation $X \mapsto QX$.

Density estimation on manifoldsSample spaces
The Stiefel manifold collects orthonormal frames while the Grassmann manifold collects subspaces. Both inherit rotation invariance from the orthogonal group, and that invariance is exactly the symmetry our kernels should respect so that estimates do not privilege any direction.

The Manifold Kernel Estimator

Average of kernels

One bump per sample, then average.

Normalizer

$c(\kappa)$ forces the estimate to integrate to one.

Equivariance

Rotating the data rotates the estimate.

Estimator

$\hat f(X) = \dfrac{1}{n\,c(\kappa)} \sum_{i=1}^{n} K_\kappa\!\big(X, X_i\big)$

Reading it

The kernel $K_\kappa$ measures invariant alignment between $X$ and each sample $X_i$.

Density estimation on manifoldsEstimator
A manifold kernel estimator averages a kernel evaluated between each sample and the query point, then divides by a normalizing constant so the result integrates to one. The kernel depends only on an invariant alignment between frames, which keeps the estimator equivariant under the group action.

Rotationally Symmetric Kernels

Depends on trace

Only the invariant $\operatorname{tr}(X^{\top}X_i)$ matters.

Langevin template

Exponential in the alignment trace.

Smooth decay

Peaks at the center, falls off gently.

Matrix-Langevin kernel

$K_\kappa(X, X_i) = \exp\!\big(\kappa\,\operatorname{tr}(X_i^{\top} X)\big)$

Density estimation on manifoldsKernels
We focus on rotationally symmetric kernels whose value depends only on the trace of the aligned product. The matrix Langevin form supplies a natural template: it concentrates mass near a center and decays smoothly, mirroring the Gaussian bump used in the flat case.

Concentration as Inverse Bandwidth

Large $\kappa$

Narrow spike, fine resolution.

Small $\kappa$

Broad bump, heavy smoothing.

Analogy

$h \sim \kappa^{-1/2}$ plays the bandwidth role.

Width scaling

$\text{kernel width} \;\propto\; \kappa^{-1/2}$

Density estimation on manifoldsBandwidth
The concentration parameter plays the role of an inverse bandwidth. A large concentration produces a narrow spike that resolves fine structure, while a small concentration produces a broad bump that smooths aggressively. Choosing it well is the central tuning problem of the chapter.

Bumps Summing to a Density

One bump each

Every sample seeds a kernel.

Normalized sum

Overlapping bumps build the estimate.

Peaks emerge

Clusters of samples raise density.

Construction

$\hat f = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_i \text{bump}_i$

Density estimation on manifoldsConstruction
Here we see the estimator built constructively. Each sample contributes one rotationally symmetric bump on the circle, and the normalized sum of these bumps yields a smooth estimated density. Regions with many nearby samples accumulate overlapping bumps and rise into peaks.

Bias of the Estimator

Local averaging

Smoothing pulls toward neighbors.

Flattens peaks

Curvature of $f$ drives the leading term.

Shrinks with $\kappa$

More concentration, less bias.

Leading bias

$\mathbb{E}\,\hat f(X) - f(X) \approx \dfrac{b}{\kappa}\,\Delta f(X)$

Interpretation

The Laplacian $\Delta f$ encodes how curvature of the density is smoothed away.

Density estimation on manifoldsBias
Bias arises because smoothing pulls the estimate toward a local average of the true density. Expanding in the bandwidth shows the leading bias scales with curvature of the density and with the inverse concentration, so heavy smoothing systematically flattens peaks and fills valleys.

Variance of the Estimator

Sampling noise

Sensitivity to the drawn sample.

Grows with $\kappa$

Sharper kernels use fewer neighbors.

Shrinks with $n$

More data steadies the estimate.

Leading variance

$\operatorname{Var}\,\hat f(X) \approx \dfrac{v\,\kappa^{d/2}}{n}\,f(X)$

Opposing pull

Bias wants large $\kappa$; variance wants small $\kappa$. They must be balanced.

Density estimation on manifoldsVariance
Variance measures the estimator's sensitivity to the particular sample drawn. Concentrated kernels use fewer effective neighbors, so the variance grows with concentration and shrinks with sample size. The variance term and the bias term pull the bandwidth in opposite directions.

The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

Undersmoothed

Large $\kappa$: spiky, high variance.

Oversmoothed

Small $\kappa$: washed out, high bias.

MISE

$\text{MISE} = \int \text{bias}^2 + \int \operatorname{Var}$

Density estimation on manifoldsTradeoff
The mean integrated squared error sums squared bias and variance. Undersmoothing yields a jagged spiky estimate dominated by variance, while oversmoothing yields a washed out estimate dominated by bias. The optimal concentration balances the two competing error sources.

Bandwidth Selection

Cross validation

Score $\kappa$ by held-out likelihood.

Plug-in

Estimate the curvature term, then solve.

Goal

Target the error-minimizing $\kappa$.

Leave-one-out score

$\mathrm{CV}(\kappa) = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_i \log \hat f_{-i}(X_i)$

Rate

The optimal concentration grows with $n$, typically $\kappa^\star \propto n^{2/(d+4)}$.

Density estimation on manifoldsSelection
Because the optimal concentration depends on unknown smoothness, we choose it from data. Cross validation scores candidate concentrations by held out likelihood, while plug in rules estimate the curvature term directly. Both target the concentration that minimizes the integrated error.

Consistency

Shrinking width

$\kappa_n \to \infty$ as samples grow.

Enough mass

$n\,\kappa_n^{-d/2} \to \infty$ keeps neighbors.

Both errors vanish

Bias and variance go to zero.

Convergence

$\mathbb{E}\,\big(\hat f(X) - f(X)\big)^2 \to 0$

Conditions

Take $\kappa_n \to \infty$ while $n\,\kappa_n^{-d/2} \to \infty$; then $\hat f$ is mean-square consistent.

Density estimation on manifoldsConsistency
Consistency requires the bandwidth to shrink while the effective sample count grows. If concentration increases and the sample size times the kernel volume diverges, both bias and variance vanish, so the estimator converges to the true density in mean square as samples accumulate.
