Contrast

Compactness Is a Constraint, Not a Background Detail

Compact Playbook
Integrate identities, use global topology, and ask how scalar curvature, volume, index, and moduli behave on a closed manifold.
Non-Compact Playbook
Control the ends, build complete metrics, and keep track of whether the example is homogeneous, cohomogeneity-one, or genuinely inhomogeneous.
Ric(g) = λ g
Same equation, different global grammar3 / 16
Pause here to prevent a common misunderstanding: non-compact does not mean local or careless. Completeness, curvature bounds, and asymptotic behavior replace many closed-manifold tools. The Einstein equation is unchanged, but the global evidence that a metric is meaningful has a different shape.

Thread I

Nonhomogeneous Einstein Metrics

A metric may have enough symmetry to be computable without having enough symmetry to be homogeneous.
Ansatz Idea
Keep a group action with large orbits, then let metric coefficients vary along a transverse parameter.
g = dt2 + a(t)2g1 + b(t)2g2
  • ODEs replace the full tensor equation.
  • Special orbit behavior sets boundary data.
  • Variable curvature invariants witness nonhomogeneity.
Cohomogeneity makes a nonhomogeneous metric calculable5 / 16
Introduce cohomogeneity as a compromise between total symmetry and no symmetry. The diagram is deliberately schematic: the orbits are not all the same geometric size, so a transverse parameter carries real curvature information. This is why an Einstein metric built by an ansatz can be explicit without becoming homogeneous.
Computation Pattern

Einstein Equation Becomes a Boundary-Value Problem

Reduced Data
Choose invariant one-forms on the orbit and write the metric through coefficient functions. The tensor equation becomes a coupled system for those functions.
Ric(g(t)) - λ g(t) = 0
Endpoint Caution
Solving the ODE in the middle is not enough; smooth collapse or infinite-distance behavior at the ends is part of the geometry.
ODE success must be matched to geometric end conditions6 / 16
The reduced equation is useful because it turns tensor geometry into a system one can inspect, but it also makes endpoint conditions visible. Stress that the boundary conditions encode smoothness, collapse, and completeness, not merely convenience for solving equations. This is one reason non-compact examples are delicate even when the ansatz looks elementary.
Thread II

Bundle Constructions Organize the Metric

Base geometry, fiber geometry, and connection curvature all contribute to Ricci curvature.
Construction Lens
Start with a base, attach a fiber, and let the connection determine how horizontal and vertical directions interact.
  • Base curvature feeds horizontal Ricci terms.
  • Fiber scale changes vertical Ricci terms.
  • Connection curvature creates mixed corrections.
Bundle geometry turns algebraic choices into curvature terms7 / 16
Frame bundle constructions as a way to manufacture structured non-compact spaces rather than as a separate topic. The students should see the metric as a negotiated object: the base, the fiber scale, and the connection each leave a trace in Ricci curvature. This sets up why adjusting one ingredient can restore the Einstein equation.
Bundle Ricci Bookkeeping

Three Curvature Ledgers Must Balance

Horizontal
Base curvature and the size of horizontal lifts control the Ricci terms seen by directions tangent to the base.
Vertical
Fiber geometry contributes its own Ricci terms, often scaled by a warping or bundle radius function.
Mixed
Connection curvature bends horizontal lifts and adds terms that can help or prevent the Einstein balance.
The equation couples geometry that the construction initially separates8 / 16
This slide turns the bundle picture into Ricci accounting. The important teaching move is that the construction separates data at first, but the Einstein equation immediately couples it again. Students should hear that the mixed terms are not a nuisance; they are often the adjustable mechanism that makes the construction work.
Completeness

An End Is Geometric Only After Its Length Is Measured

Metric Length Test
If a curve can reach the missing boundary in finite length, the displayed space is usually incomplete until something is added or modified.
Length(γ) = integral sqrt(g(γ', γ')) dt
For non-compact Einstein metrics, the boundary may be infinitely far away even when it is close in a coordinate picture.
Coordinates can make a complete end look deceptively near9 / 16
Spend time distinguishing coordinate distance from metric distance. Many non-compact constructions live on spaces that look bounded or have visible edges in a model, but the metric can stretch those edges to infinite distance. This distinction prepares students for the bounded-domain examples, where the analytic boundary is not automatically a finite-distance boundary.
Thread III

Bounded Domains of Holomorphy

Complex analysis supplies natural potentials whose Hessians define Kähler metrics.
Kähler Ansatz
A strictly plurisubharmonic potential produces a Hermitian metric by taking complex second derivatives.
gi j̄ = partiali partialj̄ Φ
  • Holomorphic coordinates carry the structure.
  • The boundary can enforce completeness.
  • Einstein becomes a complex Monge-Ampère equation.
Analytic convexity becomes geometric curvature10 / 16
Introduce bounded domains as a different engine from group actions and bundles. The construction starts from complex analysis: a potential function gives a Kähler metric, and the Einstein condition becomes an equation for that potential. The bounded picture should not mislead students, because the metric may make the analytic boundary infinitely far away.
Complex Einstein Equation

The Potential Controls Volume and Ricci Curvature

Kähler Ricci Form
For a Kähler metric, Ricci curvature is encoded by the logarithm of the determinant of the Hermitian metric matrix.
rho = -i partial partial-bar log det(gi j̄)
Einstein Target
The metric is Kähler-Einstein when this Ricci form is a constant multiple of the Kähler form.
Potential equations translate analytic data into curvature11 / 16
This is the technical bridge for the bounded-domain thread. Avoid diving into proof details; instead, make the pipeline memorable. The potential gives the metric, the metric determinant gives a volume density, and the logarithmic second derivative of that density records Ricci curvature.
Comparison

Two Ways to Get Non-Compact Einstein Geometry

Symmetry-First
Begin with a group action, reduce the equation, and use orbit geometry to tune the metric. The strength is computability; the danger is mistaking partial symmetry for homogeneity.
Analysis-First
Begin with a complex domain or potential problem, solve a nonlinear equation, and read completeness from boundary growth. The strength is analytic flexibility; the danger is hiding geometry inside estimates.
Different construction routes still face the same tests12 / 16
Use this slide to keep the chapter from fragmenting into unrelated examples. Symmetry-first and analysis-first methods look different, but they both feed into the same diagnostic questions. The comparison also helps students understand why the chapter is a report: it gathers several construction cultures around one Einstein condition.
Failure Modes

Where a Promising Example Can Break

Residual
The reduced equations leave a nonzero Ricci component, often because one coefficient or connection term is mismatched.
Incomplete End
The metric solves the equation locally but reaches a missing boundary in finite length.
Hidden Homogeneity
The example advertised as new may be isometric to a known homogeneous model after a change of variables.
Negative tests are part of understanding the positive examples13 / 16
This is a useful checkpoint before moving to the lab-style slides. Ask students to classify a failed construction by which test it violates first. The third failure mode is subtle: changing coordinates or reinterpreting the group action can turn an apparently new example into an old one.
Mini Lab

Inspect a Two-Coefficient Ansatz

Suppose a metric is written as g = dt2 + a(t)2gF + b(t)2gH.
Student Task
Predict what changes if a(t) collapses at one end while b(t) stays bounded away from zero.
  • Which orbit dimension changes?
  • Which smoothness condition becomes sharp?
  • What would show nonhomogeneity?
Small perturbations reveal which hypotheses carry the geometry14 / 16
This lab is not asking students to solve the ODE system. It asks them to read geometry from the coefficient functions. If one scale collapses and another does not, they should think about singular orbits, smoothness at the end, and curvature quantities that vary with the transverse parameter.

Recap

What to Carry Forward

Main Takeaway
Non-compact Einstein geometry is not a relaxation of rigor; it is a change in which global tests are decisive.
  • Nonhomogeneous metrics can be highly structured.
  • Bundles make curvature bookkeeping visible.
  • Bounded domains turn analytic boundary growth into complete Kähler geometry.
  • Every example should be tested for Ricci residual, completeness, and symmetry.
