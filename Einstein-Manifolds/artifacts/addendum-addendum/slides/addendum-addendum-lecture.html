The Same Equation, A Broader Laboratory

Definition

A Riemannian metric g is Einstein when its Ricci tensor satisfies

Ric(g) = lambda g.

Addendum viewpoint

The examples are organized by what fixes, varies, or forces lambda.

03 / 16
Pause here long enough to reset notation. The Ricci tensor is the trace of curvature, while the right side is the metric itself scaled by a constant, so the Einstein condition says the trace curvature has no preferred direction beyond the metric. The addendum is interesting because wildly different geometric mechanisms can all land on this same balance equation.

Infinitely Many Einstein Constants

Conceptual claim

An example scheme may yield infinitely many normalized Einstein constants once topology, bundle data, or discrete parameters are allowed to vary.

The important word is normalized: rescaling a single metric is not the phenomenon.

04 / 16
Emphasize that a raw numerical change in lambda is not enough, because rescaling one metric already changes the displayed constant. The mathematical claim is stronger: after fixing a normalization, the construction still produces infinitely many inequivalent Einstein metrics or constants. Invite students to name what information would convince them that the points on the diagram are not just the same metric written at different scales.

A Diagnostic For Counting Constants

1. Fix A Normalization

Volume, scalar curvature convention, or a topological unit must be fixed before comparing constants.

2. Vary Discrete Data

Parameters such as bundle class or integer weights can create genuinely different metrics.

3. Separate From Isometry

The list only counts after quotienting out the obvious equivalences.

05 / 16
Turn this slide into a checklist students can use when reading existence results. First identify the normalization, because otherwise constants are not comparable. Then identify the discrete or geometric data being varied, and finally ask how isometry or diffeomorphism equivalences have been removed; without all three steps, the phrase many constants is not yet mathematically precise.

Construction Pattern: Reduce PDE To ODE

Typical mechanism

Symmetry can reduce the Einstein equation to a boundary value problem along an orbit space coordinate.

g = dt^2 + a(t)^2 g_1 + b(t)^2 g_2

The hard step is not writing an ansatz; it is proving smooth collapse at the singular orbits and satisfying the Einstein residual.

06 / 16
Explain that symmetry can turn a tensor PDE into an ODE system, but this reduction does not make the problem automatic. The functions a(t) and b(t) must satisfy the Einstein equations in the interior and also meet smoothness conditions where orbits collapse. The diagram should help students see why endpoint behavior is part of the proof, not an afterthought added after solving the differential equations.

G2 Holonomy: Ricci-Flat By Parallel Form

Exceptional holonomy principle

If the holonomy lies in G2, the metric is Ricci-flat and carries a parallel stable 3-form phi.

nabla phi = 0 => Ric(g) = 0

The form is not decoration: it encodes the metric and the cross-product structure.

07 / 16
For G2, stress the shift from solving directly for Ricci equals zero to constructing a torsion-free geometric structure. A stable 3-form phi determines the metric algebraically, and the condition nabla phi = 0 places the holonomy inside G2. Once the holonomy reduction is achieved, Ricci-flatness follows from the special holonomy package rather than from a separate Ricci calculation on every component.

Spin(7): The Cayley 4-Form

Spin(7) package

A torsion-free Spin(7) structure is equivalent to a parallel Cayley form Omega.

Einstein consequence

Holonomy contained in Spin(7) forces Ric = 0, but construction usually requires delicate asymptotic or gluing control.

The dimension changes from seven to eight and the controlling parallel form changes from the G2 3-form to the Spin(7) Cayley 4-form Omega. What stays fixed is the logic: torsion-free special holonomy gives Ricci-flatness, so the construction problem becomes the search for a global parallel form with the right regularity.

Exceptional Holonomy As A Boundary Problem

  • Start with a principal orbit carrying invariant forms.
  • Write torsion-free equations for the evolving form data.
  • Impose endpoint or asymptotic regularity conditions.
  • Read Ricci-flatness from the resulting holonomy reduction.

Use the shooting picture to make the analytic burden visible. The algebra of exceptional forms supplies the variables and first-order equations, but an actual complete metric also requires the trajectory to satisfy boundary or asymptotic conditions. This is a good place to remind students that special holonomy examples are not merely representation theory; they are representation theory tied to global analysis.

Inhomogeneous Kahler-Einstein Metrics

Kahler-Einstein equation

On a complex manifold, the Einstein condition becomes a Ricci-form equation inside a fixed complex-geometric class.

rho(omega) = lambda omega

Misconception

Kahler-Einstein does not mean homogeneous; symmetry can guide construction but need not explain the final metric.

10 / 16
Introduce inhomogeneous Kahler-Einstein metrics as a useful correction to a common mental shortcut. Earlier chapters make homogeneous and symmetric spaces feel very natural, but the Kahler-Einstein equation can be solved in settings where no transitive symmetry group explains every point. The visual field on the right is meant to suggest balanced curvature on a complex manifold without suggesting that the manifold is uniform in every direction.

Three Proof Roadmaps

Family Count

Choose a normalized parameter set, build metrics, prove non-equivalence, then read distinct constants.

Holonomy Reduction

Construct a torsion-free form, recover the metric, and use holonomy to force Ricci-flatness.

Kahler-Einstein

Fix complex data, solve a Ricci-form equation, and verify the resulting metric has limited symmetry.

13 / 16
Present the three roadmaps as templates students can reuse when reading beyond the course. A family-count proof starts with parameter data and ends by separating normalized constants; a holonomy proof starts with a torsion-free form and ends with Ricci-flatness; a Kahler-Einstein proof starts with complex-geometric data and ends with a Ricci-form equation. This makes the addendum feel less like a list of announcements and more like a small taxonomy of proof strategies.

Common Traps In Reading The Addendum

Trap 1

Counting rescalings as new Einstein constants.

Trap 2

Treating G2 or Spin(7) as labels instead of holonomy reductions with parallel forms.

Trap 3

Assuming every explicit Kahler-Einstein metric is homogeneous.

Ask students which trap they are most likely to fall into, because naming the trap often improves how they read the next example.
