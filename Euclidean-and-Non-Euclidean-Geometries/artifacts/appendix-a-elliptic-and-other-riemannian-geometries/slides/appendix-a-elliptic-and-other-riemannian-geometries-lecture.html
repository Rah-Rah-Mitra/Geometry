Parallel Problem

The Third Possibility Is No Parallel Through the Point

Axiom-Level Reading

Changing the parallel statement does not merely alter one theorem. It forces a new interpretation of lines, separation, distance, and model evidence.

Teaching Cue

Ask what must count as a line if every pair of lines is required to meet. The sphere suggests an answer, but it also creates a double-intersection problem.

Use this slide to recover the course narrative before adding new machinery. Euclidean geometry gives one parallel, hyperbolic geometry gives many, and elliptic geometry removes parallels in the plane. The important transition is that a changed parallel statement demands a changed model language, not just a different drawing style.

Spherical Model

Great Circles Behave Like Lines, But the Sphere Is Not Yet the Elliptic Plane

Model Tension

On the sphere, great circles meet in antipodal pairs. Incidence wants one intersection point for two lines, but the raw sphere supplies two.

Do Not Hide the Defect

The defect is productive. It tells the lecturer exactly why the next construction identifies opposite points rather than merely renaming spherical arcs as elliptic lines.

Present the sphere as an excellent first model and also as an incomplete one. Great circles are convincing line candidates because they are symmetric and locally shortest. Yet two distinct great circles meet at two antipodal points, so a plane incidence axiom expecting one intersection has not been modeled cleanly yet.

Quotient Move

Antipodal Identification Repairs Incidence

Operational Definition

An elliptic point is an antipodal pair on the sphere. An elliptic line is a great circle with antipodal points already identified.

Immediate Payoff

  • Two elliptic lines meet in one elliptic point.
  • No elliptic line separates the whole plane into two sides.
  • The model becomes finite in diameter but still unbounded along lines.
The quotient operation is the central construction in the elliptic part of the appendix. Stress that it is not a visual trick. It changes what counts as a point, so the two antipodal meetings of great circles become a single elliptic meeting. That repair also changes separation and sidedness.
Definition bank: elliptic line as a great circle with antipodal points identifiedModel: sphere quotient by antipodal pairing

Order Language

Without Ordinary Betweenness, Circular Separation Carries the Order Data

Replacement Habit

Instead of saying one point lies between two others on a line, the elliptic setting records which pair separates another pair on a circular line.

Why This Matters

Segments and triangles must be rebuilt with care. If ordinary between-language is imported unchanged, the model silently becomes Euclidean again.

This is the most syntactic slide in the elliptic sequence. The aim is not to list every separation axiom, but to explain why the axioms are needed. Once a line is circular and antipodal points are identified, ordinary between-language no longer has the same meaning, so separation becomes the replacement relation.

Incidence Rebuilt

Segments and Triangles Need Quotient-Aware Definitions

Instructor Statement

Once a line closes up, a segment is no longer the portion of an infinite straight line singled out by ordinary betweenness. It is a chosen shorter route between elliptic points, read through the quotient.

Triangle Caution

Three elliptic points determine three elliptic lines, but the same spherical representatives can produce visually different arcs. The definition must live in the quotient, not in a preferred drawing.

Here the lecture should slow down and separate representation from definition. A spherical drawing chooses representatives, but the elliptic object is an equivalence class under antipodal identification. That distinction prevents students from trusting a single sketch too much when defining segments, triangles, or sides of a line.

Beyond the Plane

Higher-Dimensional Elliptic Geometry Has Its Own Parallel-Like Phenomena

Clifford-Parallel Idea

In elliptic three-space, one can study line families that remain at constant distance from one another. They are not plane parallels, but they preserve an equidistance relation.

Why Include This

The appendix uses the higher-dimensional remark to show that a model can keep the no-parallel plane axiom while still supporting new global phenomena.

Keep this slide conceptual. The point is not to develop elliptic three-space in full, but to show students that the word parallel can mutate when dimension and model change. A plane theorem about intersecting lines does not forbid higher-dimensional equidistant line families, so the vocabulary must track the setting.

Riemannian Turn

Riemannian Geometry Makes Measurement Local

Riemannian Metric

A metric assigns an inner product to each tangent plane. It tells the geometry how to measure lengths, angles, and eventually curvature at that point.

Conceptual Shift

The model no longer begins with a global parallel axiom. It begins with a local rule for measuring tangent vectors and studies what that rule implies.

This slide marks the transition from elliptic geometry to Riemannian geometry. The key phrase is local measurement. Instead of first asking how many parallels exist, Riemannian geometry equips each tangent plane with a measuring rule and then derives distance, angle, curvature, and geodesic behavior from that rule.

Curves First

Curvature Begins With the Osculating Circle

Curve Curvature

κ = 1/r

The osculating circle is the circle that best kisses the curve at a point. Its radius translates visual bending into a number.

Sign Convention

For a plane curve, a chosen side of the tangent line lets positive and negative curvature record which way the curve bends. At an inflection point the signed curvature passes through zero.

The appendix builds surface curvature by first recalling curve curvature. Make the osculating circle tactile: it is the circle that best matches position and tangent direction at the point. A smaller radius means tighter bending. Signed curvature then records which side of the tangent line the bending occupies.
Formula: curve curvature sample κ = 1/rConcepts: tangent line, osculating circle, inflection

Surfaces

Gaussian Curvature Multiplies the Two Principal Bends

Principal Curvatures

K = k1 k2

Normal sections through a tangent direction give curve curvatures. The largest and smallest of these are the principal curvatures.

Three Signs to Inspect

  • Sphere: both bends same sign, so K > 0.
  • Saddle: bends opposite signs, so K < 0.
  • Cylinder: one bend zero, so K = 0.
Connect this slide to the previous one by saying that a surface can be sliced by many planes through the normal line. Each slice gives an ordinary curve curvature. The principal curvatures are the extreme values, and their product is Gaussian curvature. The sign examples should be read before the formula is trusted.

Theorema Egregium

Some Curvature Can Be Detected Without Leaving the Surface

Intrinsic Message

A cylinder bends in three-dimensional space, but it has zero Gaussian curvature. Distances on the cylinder can be unrolled to a flat sheet without stretching.

Contrast

A sphere cannot be flattened this way without distortion. Its positive Gaussian curvature is not just an artifact of how it sits in space.

Use the cylinder to explain the intrinsic point gently. Students may think all visible bending counts as curvature. The cylinder bends extrinsically, but its Gaussian curvature is zero because the surface can be unrolled without changing internal distances. That prepares the meaning of Gauss's remarkable theorem.

Metric Formula

The Metric Tensor Is a Local Distance Machine

Surface Coordinates

ds2 = E dx2 + 2F dx dy + G dy2

The functions E, F, and G encode how the coordinate grid measures small displacements.

Inspection Target

Ellipses show the unit vectors for the metric. Round ellipses mean isotropic measurement; tilted or stretched ellipses record directional preference and coordinate coupling.

Translate the metric formula into a measuring device. The expression for ds squared tells the surface how to convert small coordinate changes into length. The ellipses make this visible: they are what the metric considers unit vectors at different points, so varying ellipses mean varying local geometry.

Higher Dimensions

Riemann's Step Was to Abstract the Measuring Rule

General Form

ds2 = Σ gij dxi dxj

The surface formula becomes an n-dimensional rule for measuring infinitesimal displacements.

Named Curvatures

Sectional curvature, Ricci curvature, and scalar curvature are different summaries of how the metric bends distance, volume, and geodesic families.

This slide should feel like a conceptual widening. Riemann's innovation was not simply a new surface example; it was the abstraction of geometry into a smoothly varying quadratic form in any dimension. Once that rule is available, curvature can be encoded by tensors and summarized in several useful ways.

Straightness

Geodesics Are the Metric's Version of Straight Lines

Local Rule

A geodesic has zero geodesic curvature. Equivalently, it is locally the path that the metric treats as straight.

Local Is Not Always Global

A geodesic can be locally shortest without being the unique shortest route between distant points. Closed geodesics and cylinder helices make that distinction visible.

Return to the word line, but now through the Riemannian lens. A geodesic generalizes straightness by asking the metric for locally shortest or zero geodesic curvature paths. Use the great circle and cylinder helix to separate local straightness from global shortest distance.

Synthesis

The Appendix Reframes Parallelism as a Shadow of Metric Structure

Lecture Thesis

Parallel behavior is not the final invariant. It is one visible consequence of deeper structure: the model, topology, metric, and curvature that determine geodesic behavior.

How to Read the Appendix

Start with elliptic geometry because it stresses the parallel postulate. Then let Riemannian geometry explain why curvature and metric are the broader organizing language.

This synthesis slide supplies the lecture thesis explicitly. The appendix starts from a familiar course question about parallels, but it ends by suggesting that parallelism is only one observable feature. The deeper objects are model choice, topology, metric, curvature, and geodesics, which together organize modern geometry.

Instructor Activity

A Short Lab: Rebuild One Familiar Claim in the New Language

Prompt

Choose a familiar Euclidean claim about lines or distance. Rebuild it in the elliptic model, then explain which part survives, changes, or becomes meaningless.

Suggested Claims

  • Two distinct lines meet in at most one point.
  • A line divides the plane into two sides.
  • A shortest path between nearby points is straight.
  • A rectangle can be enlarged while preserving angles.
End with an activity that forces translation. Students should not simply repeat that elliptic geometry has no parallels. They should take a familiar claim, state what the model's points and lines are, decide whether a metric is needed, and then identify the exact place where the old Euclidean proof changes.

Takeaways

What Appendix A Adds to the Course

Three Durable Lessons
  • Elliptic geometry shows the no-parallel alternative by quotienting the sphere.
  • Curvature turns bending into an invariant, not just a visual impression.
  • Riemannian metrics make geometry a local measuring rule with global consequences.
Next Reading Habit

When a geometric claim feels surprising, ask which object changed first: the model, the identification, the metric, the curvature, or the geodesics.

Close by returning to the course arc. Appendix A adds a compact preview of modern differential geometry while staying tied to the original parallel problem.
