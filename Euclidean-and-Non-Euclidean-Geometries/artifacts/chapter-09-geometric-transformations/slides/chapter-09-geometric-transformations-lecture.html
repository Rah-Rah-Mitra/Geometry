Groups of transformations

Structure-Preserving Maps Multiply

Automorphism

A one-to-one transformation of a model onto itself that preserves the selected structure: incidence first, then betweenness, congruence, angle, or other relations as required.

Group Law

For transformations S and T, the product ST means apply T first, then S. Identity, inverse, and associativity come from function composition.

Warning

Composition is usually not commutative. Rotating a triangle and then reflecting it can land vertices in a different order than reflecting first and rotating second.

The same generators can produce different products when the order is reversed.
automorphisms form groupscomposition is the multiplication
This slide makes composition visible. Transformations can be multiplied by doing one and then the other, but the order matters. Keep the triangle and mirror example in view while explaining identity and inverse. Students should leave knowing that a geometry's automorphisms form a group because structure-preserving maps compose and undo.

Motions and similarities

Not Every Automorphism Preserves Length

Motion

A transformation is a motion when every segment keeps its length: A'B' = AB.

Similarity

A transformation is a similarity when lengths are scaled by a fixed positive constant: A'B' = k AB.

Structural Point

A relation not invariant under the selected automorphism group is not part of that geometry's intrinsic language.

Motions keep the measuring stick; similarities keep the shape but change the scale.
motions are a subgroup of automorphismssimilarities form a larger Euclidean group
Separate automorphism, similarity, and motion before giving formulas. An automorphism preserves the chosen incidence and order structure, a similarity preserves shapes while scaling length by one constant, and a motion preserves length exactly. This distinction explains why hyperbolic automorphisms become motions, while Euclidean planes also allow non-motion similarities.
Reflections

The Basic Generator Has a Visible Fixed Set

Definition

The reflection R_l across a line l fixes every point of l and sends every other point to the opposite side along a perpendicular segment.

Fixed-Point Tests

  • Two fixed points on a line constrain a motion strongly.
  • Three noncollinear fixed points force the identity.
  • A nonidentity motion fixing a line pointwise is a reflection.
The fixed line is enough to see why reflections reverse orientation.
reflection = opposite motionfixed points classify maps
Present reflection as the elementary generator. The mirror fixes one line pointwise and swaps the two sides by perpendicular segments. The important proof habit is to read fixed points. A map fixing two points on a line is forced to respect that line, and three noncollinear fixed points force identity.
Products of reflections

Two Mirrors Produce Rotations or Translations

Intersecting Mirrors

A product R_l R_m with mirrors meeting at A is a rotation about A.

Common Perpendicular

Mirrors in the same nonintersecting pencil produce a translation along the common perpendicular direction.

products of two reflections are direct motionspencils of lines control the type
Products of two reflections are the first classification engine. Intersecting mirrors produce a rotation about their intersection, while mirrors with a common perpendicular produce a translation in the appropriate direction. Emphasize that the same algebraic product has different geometric names depending on the pencil of lines involved.
Hyperbolic motion diagnostics

Ideal Endpoints Record Limiting Behavior

Ideal Point

An ideal point is an equivalence class of rays that approach the same limiting direction. It is not an ordinary point of the hyperbolic plane.

Why It Matters

Translations, parallel displacements, and glides can be classified by their fixed ideal endpoints and invariant lines even when they have no ordinary fixed point.

ordinary fixed points: 0, 1, or 2   |   ideal fixed points: boundary data
Boundary data distinguishes motions that have no interior fixed points.
ideal boundary is a diagnostic, not part of the planehyperbolic motions need endpoint language
Use this slide to mark where Euclidean and hyperbolic behavior diverge. In the hyperbolic plane, ideal endpoints let translations, parallel displacements, and glides be recognized by their action near the boundary. The boundary is not part of the plane, but it records limiting directions and fixed ideal points.

Automorphisms of the Cartesian model

Euclidean Motions Become Simple Complex Formulas

Translations

z ↦ z + z0

Direct Motions

z ↦ eiθ z + z0

Opposite Motions

z ↦ eiθ conjugate(z) + z0

Complex notation packages rotation, translation, and reflection into compact formulas.
direct group: rotations plus translationsopposite group: add conjugation
Move from synthetic language to coordinates carefully. Cartesian formulas make the Euclidean motion group computable: translations add vectors, rotations multiply by a unit complex number, and opposite motions add a conjugation. The formula z maps to exp(i theta) z plus z0 should be read as rotation followed by translation.
Motions in the Poincare model

Half-Plane Motions Are Fractional Linear Maps

Upper Half-Plane

Geodesics appear as vertical rays or semicircles meeting the real boundary at right angles.

Direct Motions

z ↦ (a z + b) / (c z + d),   ad - bc = 1

Projective Matrix View

Scaling all four real matrix entries by the same nonzero factor does not change the transformation.

The real boundary is fixed as a set, and the upper half-plane maps to itself.
Poincare geometry uses real projective matricesSTIX and KaTeX support loaded
The Poincare half-plane slide shows why fractional linear transformations appear. Semicircles and vertical rays are the visible geodesics, and real coefficient Mobius transformations preserve the boundary line and the family of such geodesics. Mention that matrices are used projectively, so scaling all entries does not change the map.
Hyperbolic fixed point classification

Interior and Ideal Fixed Points Name the Motion Type

Elliptic

Rotation-like motion around a point inside the model.

Parabolic

Parallel displacement behavior with a single boundary fixed point.

Hyperbolic

Translation along an axis joining two limiting endpoints.

fixed equation: x = (a x + b) / (c x + d)   gives 0, 1, or 2 finite solutions
fixed points are classification datacoordinate roots match geometric behavior
Classify hyperbolic motions through fixed points. Elliptic transformations have one interior fixed point, parabolic transformations have one ideal fixed point, and hyperbolic translations have two ideal fixed points. This is the coordinate version of the earlier geometric classification, now expressed through roots of a fractional linear equation.
Congruence described by motions

A Group Action Can Define Measurement

Flag

A flag consists of a point, a ray from that point, and one side of the line containing the ray.

Simply Transitive Action

For any two flags there is exactly one motion sending the first to the second.

Outcome

Congruence of segments and angles can be defined by existence of a motion sending one object to the other.

A flag pins down position, direction, and side, making the motion unique.
measurement can be rebuilt from a motion groupreflection behavior supplies congruence axioms
This slide connects transformations back to the congruence axioms. A flag is a point, a ray, and a side of that ray. If a group acts simply transitively on flags and has the right reflection behavior, then congruence can be described by motions. This turns measurement into group action.
Symmetry

A Figure's Symmetries Form a Subgroup

Definition

A symmetry of a figure is a motion that leaves the figure invariant as a set. The full collection of symmetries is the figure's stabilizer subgroup.

Examples in the Chapter

  • A point has all rotations and all reflections through the point.
  • A frieze pattern can have an infinite cyclic group.
  • A regular polygon has a dihedral symmetry group.
Symmetry groups are stabilizers: allowed motions that return the figure to itself.
finite and infinite examples share the same group ideasymmetry is an application of invariance
Introduce symmetry as the practical payoff. The symmetries of a figure are exactly the motions that leave the figure invariant, and these motions form a subgroup. The richer the subgroup, the more symmetric the figure. Use the frieze, triangle, and square examples to distinguish infinite, cyclic, and dihedral behavior.
Worked example

The Square Symmetry Group Is Dihedral

A quarter-turn and a reflection generate all eight symmetries.

Generators

R is a 90 degree rotation about the center. T is one reflection axis through the center.

Relations

R4 = I,   T2 = I,   TR = R-1T

Group

The eight symmetries are I, R, R2, R3, T, TR, TR2, TR3, the dihedral group D4.

cyclic rotations plus one reflectiondihedral presentation in motion form
The square example is a compact group presentation. A quarter-turn R and a reflection T generate all eight symmetries, with R to the fourth equal to identity, T squared equal to identity, and TR equal to R inverse T. Encourage students to verify the relations by tracking vertices, not by trusting the notation.
